On 22 October 1956, students at the Building Industry Technological University in Budapest printed sixteen demands as leaflets. They survive in a translation reproduced in a United Nations report, and they matter because the demands were political and economic before any fighting began. "We demand the immediate withdrawal of all Soviet troops in accordance with the provisions of the Peace Treaty." ([students of the Building Industry Technological University, 22 October 1956](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/1956hungary-16points.asp)) The students asked for secret-ballot elections, a government led by Imre Nagy and an end to the one-party system. "We demand complete freedom of opinion and expression, freedom of the Press and a free Radio" ([same resolution, 22 October 1956](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/1956hungary-16points.asp)). They also wanted revised work norms, a minimum wage and the removal of the Stalin statue. The last point called a demonstration for the next day and invited factory workers to join it. This was a programme of specific claims, issued as the authorities began loosening the rigid controls of Stalin's rule after his death, a shift known as de-Stalinisation.

## The demands before the fighting

The demonstration of 23 October drew students, workers and soldiers. A delegation went to the Radio Building to ask that all sixteen points be broadcast and was refused; the ÁVH, the state security police, fired on the crowd, and the first weapons came from soldiers and factory stores. The UN Special Committee, which heard 111 refugees in five cities in early 1957, concluded: "What took place in Hungary was a spontaneous national uprising, caused by long-standing grievances." ([UN Special Committee on the Problem of Hungary, findings, June 1957](https://files.libcom.org/files/Report%20of%20the%20UN%20Special%20Committee%20on%20the%20Problem%20of%20Hungary%20%28compiled%201957%29.pdf)) An anonymous professor of philosophy told the committee: "that the Hungarian revolution had no leaders. It was not organized; it was not centrally directed." ([anonymous witness heard by the UN Special Committee, 1957](https://files.libcom.org/files/Report%20of%20the%20UN%20Special%20Committee%20on%20the%20Problem%20of%20Hungary%20%28compiled%201957%29.pdf)) Peter Fryer, correspondent of the British Communist *Daily Worker*, reached Budapest on 31 October and recorded the same pattern of security-police shootings in the provinces. The committee was denied entry to Hungary and Fryer was a communist breaking with his party; their agreement on the sequence carries weight.

## Nagy's five days

Imre Nagy was a communist prime minister, not an opponent of the regime. His broadcasts show a position shifting under pressure. On 28 October he still spoke of a "great national democratic movement" while warning that "criminal elements" had used it; by 30 October he had abolished the one-party system and named a coalition cabinet. On 1 November he declared neutrality, meaning Hungary would leave the Soviet-led military alliance, the Warsaw Pact: "The Hungarian people desire the consolidation and further development of the achievements of its national revolution without joining any power blocs." ([Imre Nagy, declaration of neutrality, 1 November 1956](https://worldjpn.net/documents/texts/docs/19561028.S1E.html)) On 4 November, as Soviet troops returned, he said: "Our troops are fighting. The Government is in its place." ([Imre Nagy, radio broadcast, 4 November 1956](https://worldjpn.net/documents/texts/docs/19561028.S1E.html)) A variant text of his last address, with no recording or named translator, has him saying "it is possible that I shall only be able to stay at my post for one or two hours" ([Imre Nagy, "Last Message", 4 November 1956](https://web.viu.ca/davies/H102/Nagy.LastSpeech.1956.htm)). He asked leaders to leave the country if they could. The two English versions disagree, so the exact final wording is unsettled.

## What Moscow decided

Budapest could not see the decision that sealed the revolt. On 31 October the Soviet leadership reversed its own decision to withdraw. Khrushchev told the Presidium: "We should reexamine our assessment and should not withdraw our troops from Hungary and Budapest. We should take the initiative in restoring order in Hungary." ([Nikita Khrushchev, CPSU Presidium, 31 October 1956](http://www.coldwar.hu/starting%20page/56doc6.pdf)) The notes show a plan to install a provisional government under János Kádár before any Kádár government existed. At five in the morning on 4 November, Soviet guns opened on the city. The American legation telexed Washington that shelling had begun, tanks stood near the bridges and Cardinal József Mindszenty had arrived, protesting: "I protest against this aggression and I ask for forceful and speedy defense of my country from the USA and other powers." ([Cardinal József Mindszenty, statement relayed by the US legation, 4 November 1956](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1955-57v25/d162)) Radio Budapest went off air and the team at Tököl was arrested. By 22 November three Soviet officials reported that hostility to Kádár remained strong and the workers' councils were still being used against the government. Nagy, they wrote, was to leave the Yugoslav Embassy that evening under a guarantee of no prosecution, and "The necessary measures in this connection have been prepared together with comrades Serov and Münnich." ([Malenkov, Suslov and Aristov, report to the CPSU Central Committee, 22 November 1956](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB76/doc9.pdf))

## Councils and the argument over terror

The challenge to the restored order came from workers' councils, elected factory bodies that claimed the right to run production. After the Soviet offensive they became a rival authority. On 14 November delegates from Greater Budapest factories met at the Egyesült Izzó factory in Újpest and resolved: "We regard the means of production as collective property which we are at all times ready to defend." ([Central Workers' Council of Greater Budapest, 14 November 1956](http://www.marxists.org/archive/nagy-balazs/budapest.pdf)) They asked that Nagy head a government, protested the recruitment of ÁVH men into the new security forces, demanded Soviet withdrawal and said work would resume only when their demands were met. Balázs Nagy, a council official, recalled that the delegates refused to recognise Kádár's government while seeking a compromise with it; in his rendering Kádár told them that if they would not work the government could take their place. The government installed by Soviet guns had to negotiate with the workers it had defeated.

Kádár justified the invasion by invoking the "White Terror", reprisals against security policemen and their suspected collaborators. The UN committee recorded the Kádár government's justification that only Soviet force had prevented counter-revolutionaries from slaughtering workers and peasants. The committee recorded lynchings and shootings, and judged that in almost all cases they fell on ÁVH men or suspected collaborators. Fryer described a crowd at Magyaróvár hanging ÁVH Lieutenant József Stefko after the security police shot a peaceful demonstration, and wrote: "I saw for myself that the uprising was neither organised nor controlled by fascists or reactionaries" ([Peter Fryer, introduction to *Hungarian Tragedy*, written 1956](https://www.marxists.org/archive/fryer/1956/dec/introduction.htm)). Both accounts describe real violence; they disagree over who was terrorising whom and whether it justified a second invasion. Fryer's figure of at least 20,000 dead was an estimate, not a count.

## What the record cannot establish

Testimony is uneven. Budapest, students, soldiers and organised workers are well represented; peasants, women, rural fighters and families of the disappeared are not. The UN committee heard 111 witnesses but published its report, not the verbatim record, and 81 asked to remain anonymous. Nearly every first-hand text comes through English translation, and the Soviet documents are translations of Russian originals whose archive references have not been physically checked. Two English versions of Nagy's last broadcast disagree, with no recording to settle them. No document set called the "Hungarian Round Table" belongs to 1956; that name attaches to 1989. A monitored transcript of Radio Free Europe broadcasts survives only as a scanned page with no usable text layer, and film such as the CBS film *Hungary in Flames* has not been examined. Nothing here establishes what happened in the Yugoslav Embassy after 22 November, how many people the councils represented, or a reliable death toll.

## How historians have read it

- **Csaba Békés, Malcolm Byrne and János M. Rainer, eds., *The 1956 Hungarian Revolution: A History in Documents* (2003).** The standard documentary collection of Soviet, Hungarian and Western records; its headnotes supply the interpretive frame used for the declassified papers above. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/search.json?q=The+1956+Hungarian+Revolution+A+History+in+Documents)
- **Mark Kramer, "The Soviet Union and the 1956 Crises in Hungary and Poland: Reassessments and New Findings", *Journal of Contemporary History* 33, no. 2 (1998), 163–214.** Uses newly available Soviet records to reassess Moscow's decision-making; the scholarly counterpart to the 31 October notes, and different from older accounts that treated the intervention as one coherent plan. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/002200949803300201)
- **Charles Gati, *Failed Illusions: Moscow, Washington, Budapest and the 1956 Hungarian Revolt* (2006).** Argues that Soviet, American and Hungarian leaders each misread the others; declassified CIA material supports the finding that outside powers were surprised by the uprising. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/search.json?q=Failed+Illusions+Charles+Gati)
- **Victor Sebestyen, *Twelve Days: The Story of the 1956 Hungarian Revolution* (2006).** A narrative synthesis drawing on interviews and archives; useful for sequencing rather than as a primary source. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/search.json?q=Twelve+Days+Victor+Sebestyen)
- **Eliav Lieblich, "At Least Something: The UN Special Committee on the Problem of Hungary, 1957–1958", *European Journal of International Law* 30, no. 3 (2019), 843–876.** Treats the UN inquiry as an early commission of inquiry and stresses its mixed effects rather than its failure to secure withdrawal; the main scholarly guide to using A/3592 critically. [DOI: chz042](https://doi.org/10.1093/ejil/chz042)
- **Melvin J. Lasky, ed., *The Hungarian Revolution: A White Book* (1957).** A contemporary collection assembled from Hungarian sources in the revolution's defence; the trade-union recommendation above comes from it, so it is both a source and an advocacy document. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/search.json?q=The+Hungarian+Revolution+A+White+Book)

Broad agreement exists on the sequence of events, the spontaneous character of the uprising and the fact of a second Soviet invasion. Live disagreement concerns the weight to give Soviet security fears, the meaning of the neutrality declaration, the scale of popular violence against ÁVH men and whether Western broadcasts raised false hopes. The Kádár government's "counter-revolution" thesis and the witnesses' "popular uprising" account are not equally well evidenced. Several of the works above are known from catalogue records and published descriptions rather than from full texts.
