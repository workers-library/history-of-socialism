Harry Braverman put the organisation of work itself at the centre of Marxist political economy. He argued that under capitalism the control of how work is done passes from the people who do it to the people who employ them, and that this transfer reshapes factory, office and service jobs alike. His book *Labor and Monopoly Capital* became one of the most cited works of post-war social science and also one of the most contested. This profile sets out what Braverman argued, what can be checked in the texts opened for this research and where later scholars objected. It reports his claims as claims, not as settled findings about every workplace.

## Biography

The fullest checked account comes from John Bellamy Foster's introduction to the 1998 edition of *Labor and Monopoly Capital*. Braverman was born on 9 December 1920 in New York City, the son of Morris Braverman, a shoeworker, and Sarah Wolf Braverman. He entered Brooklyn College but left after about a year because of the Depression. From 1937, according to Foster, he apprenticed at the Brooklyn Naval Shipyard as a coppersmith, moved into pipefitting and eventually supervised a team of eighteen to twenty workers refitting pipes on docked ships. He was drafted in 1945 and, as an army sergeant at Cheyenne, Wyoming, taught and supervised locomotive pipefitting. In 1947 he and his wife Miriam settled in Youngstown, Ohio, where he worked in steel layout and fitting at Republic Steel, William B. Pollock Co. and Owen Structural Steel. Foster says he was dismissed from Republic Steel at the instigation of the FBI. Chris Smith and Paul Thompson, writing in *Contributions to Political Economy* in 2024, give a slightly different chronology: a four-year coppersmith apprenticeship from 1938 to 1942, three further years at the shipyard supervising up to twenty men, then military service and steel fabrication work. The two accounts agree on the substance and differ by about a year over the starting date. Neither difference is resolved here.

Smith and Thompson divide his working life into skilled manual work from 1938 to 1951 and journalism and publishing from 1952 to 1976, while cautioning that most of his working life was actually white-collar. Foster records that he joined Grove Press as an editor in 1960, became vice president and general manager, was credited with publishing *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* and completed a B.A. at the New School for Social Research during those years. In 1967 he became director of Monthly Review Press, a post he held until his death on 2 August 1976. The Monthly Review Press book page confirms that he was director of the press at the time of his death; the Marxists' Internet Archive biography says he died of cancer in August 1976.

Foster describes him as identifying with socialism from his teenage years in Brooklyn, first in the Young People's Socialist League and then in the Socialist Workers Party, part of the American Trotskyist movement. During the 1940s and early 1950s he wrote frequently for SWP publications under the party name Harry Frankel. The Marxists' Internet Archive index says he used that name for *Fourth International* and *The Militant* until 1952, which can be checked against the archived bylines: the May 1944 review "A Defamer of Marxism" and the September 1952 essay "Capitalism and Democracy" are headed "Harry Frankel", while the October 1955 article "Automation: Promise and Menace" is headed "Harry Braverman". Foster says that in 1953 he broke with the SWP, left the steel industry and founded an independent periodical, *The American Socialist*, with Bert Cochran. Foster dates the magazine to 1960, while the Marxists' Internet Archive biography says it lasted until 1959. Both dates are recorded here without choosing between them.

## Intellectual tradition

Braverman wrote from within American Trotskyism and then from the orbit of Monthly Review Marxism. The 1944 Frankel review is a defence of Trotsky's analysis of the Soviet Union as a "degenerated workers' state" against Max Shachtman's argument that the Stalinist bureaucracy had become a new exploiting class. Frankel insisted that the class character of the Soviet Union turned on property forms, nationalised property and the monopoly of foreign trade. Of the claim that this question was "not even a decisively important part" of Trotskyism, he wrote: "This is like saying that a man could function without a heart." Smith and Thompson place him in the Cochranite tendency, expelled with it from the SWP in the early 1950s, after which he edited *The American Socialist*. No opened source records him rejecting a label that others applied to him. What the sources do show is a persistent preference for a Marxism rewritten to fit changed conditions rather than defended as inherited text.

At Monthly Review Press he worked in the circle of Paul Sweezy and Harry Magdoff, and Foster explicitly places the book as a contribution to the theory of monopoly capital developed in Baran and Sweezy's *Monopoly Capital* (1966) and Magdoff's *The Age of Imperialism* (1969). Sweezy's foreword says Braverman's direct experience of industrial work equipped him to combat the "myths and fallacies" promoted by capitalism's apologists. The Monthly Review connection should not be read as a full intellectual identity: Sweezy also notes that Braverman deliberately did not pursue what Sweezy called the "subjective aspects" of working-class development. Braverman's own 1958 essay "Marx in the Modern World" argues against treating Marx's writings as complete descriptions of the present. For the place of this argument in the book's wider framework, see [The Theoretical Backbone](/theoretical-backbone).

## Major contributions

Braverman's central contribution was to put the organisation of work at the centre of Marxist political economy. He began the book with the distinction between labour power, the capacity to work that a worker sells, and labour, the work itself. Foster quotes him: once workers "have been forced to sell their labor power to another", they "also surrender their interest in the labor process, which has now been 'alienated'". Control over the labour process then passes to the capitalist, and management becomes, in Braverman's phrase quoted by Foster, "war by other means". The first three chapters restate this framework; the next two examine scientific management, meaning Frederick Winslow Taylor's system of studying work from management's side and dictating the approved method for each task, and its effects.

His account of Taylorism rests on three principles, summarised by Foster from the book: the dissociation of the labour process from the skills of the workers; the separation of conception from execution, that is the splitting of deciding what is to be done and how from carrying it out, so that the worker performs a task without understanding or choosing the method; and management's monopoly over knowledge, used to control every step of the labour process and its mode of execution. In Braverman's own words in chapter 4, "This should be called the principle of the separation of conception from execution, rather than by its more common name of the separation of mental and manual labor", because mental labour is itself subdivided by the same rule. Human labour is distinctive, he wrote, because it combines execution with a conception of the thing to be done; under capitalism that unity is broken so that the worker executes without comprehending. He called scientific management "nothing less than the explicit verbalization of the capitalist mode of production" and argued that Taylor understood the Babbage principle better than anyone of his time: work was divided so that employers could buy exactly the quantity of skill and strength each fragment required. The full text of the book used here comes from a PDF of the 25th anniversary edition hosted on a third-party WordPress files server, not from a publisher-hosted file; that provenance matters because it is not the publisher's own digital edition.

His second contribution was to trace this process into occupations that conventional sociology treated as non-manual. The longest chapter is on clerical workers, and another covers service occupations and retail trade. This is the process usually called deskilling, meaning the loss of craft knowledge and skill as production is broken into simpler tasks controlled from outside, though Foster notes that Braverman did not use that word. He wrote instead of "the destruction of craftsmanship" and argued that "the capitalist mode of production systematically destroys all-round skills where they exist". He did not claim that the average level of skill in society as a whole would fall. His subject was, in his opening sentence, "the structure of the working class, and the manner in which it had changed". In the 1975 talk printed as an appendix he put the transformation another way: in capitalist society "the wage becomes the sole equity of the worker in the job". The historical detail behind this argument is treated in [Who Controlled the Workplace](/who-controlled-the-workplace).

Braverman's own statement of the task of a socialist theorist is equally characteristic. In "Marx in the Modern World" (1958) he wrote that "the job is not to see where 'Marx was wrong' so much as to make a fresh application of his theory to the world around us as it is, not as it once was", and that socialists needed "Faradays and Maxwells" or even "Einsteins and Plancks" rather than people who merely "confine themselves to knocking Isaac Newton".

## Analysis of capitalism

For Braverman, capitalism was a system in which the purchase of labour power gave the buyer no automatic right to a particular form of work, so the labour process had to be brought under control actively. Machinery and what he called "scientific" management were not neutral techniques that happened to be used by capital; they were forms in which the social relation of command over living labour was built into the organisation of production. He rejected the view that Taylorism had been superseded by human relations or job enrichment. Smith and Thompson report that in the book he dismissed the human-relations school as the "maintenance crew for the human machinery", and they judge that verdict "a little simplistic" while accepting that it matched widespread scepticism of job-enrichment schemes.

He traced this control through the detailed division of labour, the separation of conception from execution, the substitution of cheaper labour for skilled labour and the deskilling of office as well as factory work. Foster quotes his "general law of the capitalist division of labor": "a structure is given to all labor processes that at its extremes polarizes those whose time is infinitely valuable and those whose time is worth almost nothing." Smith and Thompson note that he also examined the conversion of independent producers into wage workers, the growth of a "universal market" that tries to commodify all social activity, the reserve army of labour, surplus value, the modern corporation, the state and productive and unproductive labour. In the 1975 talk he described the result as an "alienation which feeds on itself", producing a "profound antagonism between those who work and those who manage them".

He was not a technological pessimist. In the introduction to the book he called himself "always a moderniser", and he closed *Labor and Monopoly Capital* with an ideal of collective workers controlling machinery rather than being controlled by it. The 1955 article "Automation: Promise and Menace" treats automation as a genuine leap in productive technique whose consequences under capitalism are destructive because production is for profit rather than use. It quotes Norbert Wiener that the first industrial revolution devalued the human arm and the second would devalue the human brain in routine decisions. The factory setting of these changes is covered in [Industrial Labour and the Factory](/industrial-labour-and-the-factory).

## Analysis of socialism

Braverman wrote no blueprint of socialist institutions, and the sources opened for this profile contain none. What they contain is a normative standard by which he judged existing societies, plus a defence of collectivised property written under the name Harry Frankel.

In the introduction to *Labor and Monopoly Capital* he stated that his view of work was governed by "nostalgia for an age that has yet to come into being", in which "the craft satisfaction that arises from conscious and purposeful mastery of the labour process" would be combined with science and engineering. Foster develops the point with Braverman's example of modern pin manufacture: the whole process is reunified in one machine, which could in principle be operated by associated producers who rotate tasks and take part in design, repair and improvement rather than repeating one fragment all their lives. The ideal is worker control of the labour process joined to scientific knowledge, not a return to pre-industrial craft.

"Capitalism and Democracy" (September 1952, signed Harry Frankel) argues that private property in the means of production in its modern monopolistic form is a barrier rather than a guarantee of democracy. Frankel treats the political dictatorship in the Soviet Union as a distortion produced by revolution, civil war and hostile capitalist pressure, not as a property of collectivised economy as such. He quotes Engels on the withering away of the state: "The government of persons is replaced by the administration of things and the direction of the processes of production." He holds that social antagonisms cannot be eliminated until material scarcity is overcome, while the repressive functions of the state can diminish as want diminishes. In "Marx in the Modern World" he criticised the Soviet model for ossification and bureaucracy while insisting that the task was to renew Marxist theory rather than abandon it. In the book he wrote, as quoted by Smith and Thompson, that Soviet industrialisation "imitated the capitalist model" and that the Soviet working population came to bear "all the stigmata of the Western working classes".

His 1976 "Two Comments" replies to the charge of pessimism: "Marxism is not merely an exercise in satisfying intellectual curiosity, nor an academic pursuit, but a theory of revolution and thus a tool of combat", and "I have every confidence in the revolutionary potential of the working classes of the so-called developed capitalist countries."

## Recommended reading

These selections are starting points rather than a complete list of Braverman's books and articles.

- *Labor and Monopoly Capital: The Degradation of Work in the Twentieth Century* (1974). It puts the organisation of work, the sale of labour power and the separation of conception from execution at the centre of political economy, and remains the fullest statement of his case.
- "Marx in the Modern World" (1958). It argues that Marx's theory must be applied afresh to the present rather than defended as a finished text, which is the clearest statement of his method.
- "Automation: Promise and Menace" (1955). It treats automation as a real advance in technique whose effects under capitalism are destructive because production is for profit rather than use, making it the best short statement of his view of technology.
- "Capitalism and Democracy" (1952). It argues that private property in its monopolistic form obstructs democracy and treats Soviet dictatorship as a distortion rather than a result of collective ownership, making it the clearest statement of his political standard.

Smith and Thompson describe "The Making of the U.S. Working Class", published in *Monthly Review* in 1994, as a transcribed lecture given by Braverman in 1975. The *Monthly Review* archive itself catalogues the piece as an "Article" and gives no date of delivery. The claim that it derives from a 1975 lecture is therefore attributed to Smith and Thompson and is not independently confirmed here.

No interview with Braverman was found in the opened sources, and no other recorded lectures were found. No audiovisual material is claimed in this profile.

## Intellectual influences

Marx is the decisive influence, and the evidence is Braverman's own text rather than inference. In "Two Comments" he rejects the idea that the distinction between the social division of labour and the detailed division of labour was his invention: "the entire treatment comes from Chapter 14, especially Section 4 of the first volume of *Capital*". Foster records that the book's theoretical framing restates Marx's chapter 7 on the labour process, and Smith and Thompson note that Braverman relies throughout on Marx's discussion of value, the evolution of the labour process and the role of technology and science in volume 1.

The classical political economists appear as sources to be read against one another. Foster traces Braverman's use of Adam Smith, Charles Babbage and Andrew Ure on the division of labour, and Smith and Thompson note that the book incorporates contemporary studies by James Bright, Joan Woodward and Ivar Berg as well as official occupational statistics. Braverman's 1955 automation article quotes Norbert Wiener's *Cybernetics* on the devaluation of human brain work. In his 1958 essay he engages John Strachey and Earl Browder on immiseration and the "iron law of wages", and defends Marx's method by quoting Marx and Engels, including Engels's late acknowledgement that capitalism had outgrown the "juvenile state of exploitation" he had described fifty years earlier.

Frederick Winslow Taylor is an object of critical study rather than a positive influence. Braverman took Taylor's writings seriously enough to reproduce long passages from *Shop Management*, *The Principles of Scientific Management* and Taylor's testimony to a House committee, in order to show that the "science of work" was reserved to management. The 1944 Frankel review shows Trotsky's continuing influence on his analysis of the Soviet state. Baran and Sweezy's monopoly-capital framework and Magdoff's work on imperialism shape parts 3 and 4 of the book, according to Foster and to Smith and Thompson.

## Influence and legacy

Smith and Thompson judge *Labor and Monopoly Capital* "one of the most influential and consequential in the history of post-war social science". They report that it has never been out of print, has sold more than 120,000 copies in English, mostly between 1976 and 1990, and has been translated into Chinese, Italian, Spanish, Japanese, Portuguese, French, Swedish, German, Dutch, Greek, Norwegian and Serbo-Croatian. The 1998 publisher edition carries Foster's note that the Social Science Citations Index listed roughly 500 citations for 1976 to 1980 and a practically identical number for 1992 to 1996. They also stress that Braverman died within two years of publication, so the debate around the book took shape after his death and he could answer only with the short 1976 reply.

Foster's introduction describes the book as opening the way to a flood of radical labour-process studies. Smith and Thompson identify successive waves: application studies in the United States, such as Andrew Zimbalist's 1979 collection of case studies; the Nuffield College conference on skill in 1978; foundational alternatives from Andrew Friedman (1977), Richard Edwards (1979), Michael Burawoy (1978 and later) and Craig Littler; the complaint about "Bravermania" from Littler and Salaman in 1982; and the establishment of the annual International Labour Process Conference in 1983, which carried the perspective into management and organisation studies. British feminist sociologists combined labour-process and gender analysis, and Smith and Thompson note that Arlie Hochschild acknowledged a debt to Braverman in developing the idea of emotional labour. Later research on lean production, call centres, platform work and algorithmic management is described by them as a continuation of the same questions about control, measurement and degraded work. Questions of worker organisation are taken up in [Unions](/unions).

Reception was uneven across countries. Smith and Thompson report that the book was translated into Chinese as early as 1978 but was not taken up by Chinese scholars studying Chinese transformations, that German and Austrian skill-formation institutions made a simple degradation thesis hard to apply, and that Japanese debates exposed the American basis of his assumptions. They conclude that the book remains a landmark but a "route map" that needs revision, not a text to which researchers should simply return.

## Criticisms and controversies

Braverman answered several criticisms himself in "Two Comments" (1976). Feminist readers, including Rosalyn Baxandall, Elizabeth Ewen and Linda Gordon, welcomed the book's contribution to feminist analysis while faulting its silence on household labour. Braverman replied that household work lay outside his study and argued that the modern women's movement was itself a product of women being drawn out of the household by capital accumulation, so that the dynamic to analyse was the breakdown of the traditional household economy. He also rejected the charge that he was pessimistic about working-class consciousness, saying that his description of capitalism's effects was milder than Marx's and that he had deliberately studied the objective structure of the class first because the class struggle was then relatively quiescent.

Subsequent scholarship has pressed the same and other objections. Smith and Thompson record that the "sin of omission most noticed" was the absence of worker resistance, consciousness and the subjective side of work. Braverman's claim to describe the working class "as it really is" did not, in their view, survive scrutiny, because objective trends in deskilling and control can be slowed or modified by worker reaction. They find that his confidence in revolutionary potential rests largely on a circular inference from proletarianisation and homogenisation to working-class unity, without evidence about agency or about occupational, ethnic and gender divisions. Feminist research on the sexual exclusivity of skilled and craft work, by writers such as Verity Beechey, Cynthia Cockburn and Anna Pollert, further complicates his account of skill. The book's index contains no entry for race or slavery, and the racial organisation of work in the United States is not discussed.

Other criticisms concern scope and evidence. Smith and Thompson note that historians such as William Lazonick challenged Braverman's readings of Marx, machinery and skill, and that where training institutions sustained high supplies of skilled labour, as in Germany and Austria, a universal trend of skill degradation could be questioned. They also find several parts of the book thinly sourced: the chapters on the state, surplus value, productive and unproductive labour and the "middle layers" of employment rest on very few references. Their judgement on his treatment of management thought is balanced: the dismissal of human relations as a mere maintenance crew was "a little simplistic", but it answered a real orthodoxy. Foster's introduction warns against reducing the book to a simple theory of universal deskilling and points out that Braverman did not use that term. Debates from the 1980s onward about post-Fordism, global production networks, financialisation and platform work have required labour-process theory to add national, spatial and geographical dimensions that the book did not supply.

## Related thinkers

Braverman's immediate intellectual ancestors are Marx and Engels, with Adam Smith, Babbage and Ure as the classical writers on division of labour whom he reads against the grain. Frederick Winslow Taylor is the central object of his critique, and the Babbage principle supplies the link between task division and the purchase of skill. Leon Trotsky, as read through the 1944 Frankel review, shaped his defence of the Soviet Union as a degenerated workers' state; Max Shachtman was the target of that polemic. Paul Sweezy wrote the foreword and placed the book in the Monthly Review tradition of Baran and Sweezy and Harry Magdoff, while John Bellamy Foster wrote the 1998 introduction and later defended the book's continuing relevance.

Michael Burawoy, Andrew Friedman, Richard Edwards, Craig Littler and Graeme Salaman represent the next generation of labour-process theory that both developed and revised his framework. Peter Meiksins's 1994 review and Bryan Palmer's 1999 study of the early Harry Frankel writings are the principal scholarly secondary works consulted here, and Chris Smith and Paul Thompson's 2024 retrospective supplies the fullest recent assessment. Writers such as Cynthia Cockburn, Verity Beechey, Anna Pollert and Arlie Hochschild connect his analysis of work to gender and emotional labour. No published profile of Braverman or of these related thinkers exists yet in this repository, so no internal profile links are claimed for them here.
