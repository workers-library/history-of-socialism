In 1957, in Monroe, North Carolina, members of the local NAACP branch took up guns to defend Black homes against Ku Klux Klan violence. Its president, Robert F. Williams, later accepted responsibility. The sentence that opened his 1962 book *Negroes with Guns* was direct: "Because a Negro community in the South took up guns in self-defense against racist violence — and used them." ([Robert F. Williams, Prologue, *Negroes with Guns*, 1962](https://archive.org/download/williams_negroes/williams_negroes_djvu.txt)). The book was assembled in exile from a Havana taped interview while he was wanted on a kidnapping charge: a defence of his own conduct, not a neutral record.

Williams said he was not opposed to Martin Luther King's nonviolence, only to what he called the lack of flexibility in pacifist commitments; when law and order broke down, citizens might defend themselves. "It is remarkable how easily and quickly state and local police control and disperse lawless mobs when the Negro is ready to defend himself with arms." ([Robert F. Williams, Prologue, 1962](https://archive.org/download/williams_negroes/williams_negroes_djvu.txt)). That was his account of police behaviour, not an independent finding, and the book's dates needed an errata page. The argument was already whole in 1962: armed self-defence, a rejection of gradualism, and the claim that the state protected the wrong people.

## "UNITE or PERISH"

Three years later the Revolutionary Action Movement, a small clandestine organisation, printed its own version in the Summer–Fall 1965 issue of its journal *Black America*, with Williams listed as chairman-in-Exile. The journal said its purpose was to bring "clarity and direction in revolutionary struggle" and unite "Black America with the Bandung world" of Asia, Africa and Latin America. It ended with "UNITE or PERISH-WE WILL WIN." ([RAM, *Black America*, Summer–Fall 1965, statement of purpose](http://freedomarchives.org/Documents/Finder/DOC513_scans/RAM/513.RAM.Black.America.Summer.1965.pdf)).

Under "Revolutionary Black Internationalism" it argued that the United States contained two nations: a white America and a captive colonial Black America, and that the vanguard was the sub-proletariat of exploited races rather than the industrial working class. It rejected civil-rights liberalism as "bourgeois reformism" and Black capitalism and separatist cultural politics as "bourgeois nationalism". The scan drops letters, so exact wording needs checking against page images; it preserves a programme, not a record of what members did, and its language about white people was the movement's own.

RAM predated the Panthers, founded in Oakland in 1966, but the connection is argued rather than settled. Seale had been involved with RAM, and Umoja and Jones argue that it shaped the vocabulary of Newton and Seale while recording a tactical disagreement: RAM treated the vanguard as underground, the Panthers built a party in the open. The Party's own account has Newton insisting the founders chose the name. Scholars agree that repression shaped the turn to clandestine work; they disagree on how much RAM organised and whether armed struggle was a rational response or a strategic error.

## The underground after 1971

After the Panthers' internal crisis and the split of 1971, a clandestine formation called the Black Liberation Army carried part of that programme forward. Jalil Abdul Muntaqim, a former Panther imprisoned from 1971, wrote in 1979: "The Black Liberation Army is a politico-military organization, whose primary objective is to fight for the independence and self-determination of Afrikan people in the United States." ([Jalil Muntaqim, "On the Black Liberation Army", written 18 September 1979](https://archive.org/download/on-the-black-liberation-army/OnTheBlackLiberationArmy_djvu.txt)). He dated the underground to 1968 or 1969 and the split to 1971. Assata Shakur, quoted by Umoja, described autonomous collectives rather than a central organisation; Umoja argues that repression replenished its ranks from the Panthers while conceding the idea predated the party. The record cannot adjudicate.

Its *Study Guide '77–78*, reproduced in a 2023 collection, stated: "We identify the enemies of the Black Nation not as white people per se, but as the monopoly capitalist class and its agents, be they Black or white." ([BLA Coordinating Committee, *Study Guide '77–78*, reproduced in *Collected Works of the Black Liberation Army*, Vol. I, 2023 edition](https://archive.org/download/collected-works-of-the-black-liberation-army-volume-1-distribution/Collected%20Works%20of%20the%20Black%20Liberation%20Army%20Volume%201%20%28Distribution%29_djvu.txt)). That draws a line between a class enemy and white workers as such. The guide discussed alienated labour and surplus value and proposed Black communes based on economic self-reliance. Muntaqim conceded that armed struggle began prematurely and that losing an above-ground base mattered. His chronology of armed incidents comes from a Justice Department report he called one-sided: evidence of how the BLA read police records, not an independent account.

## The defeat, in their own words

Kuwasi Balagoon, a New York Panther and BLA soldier, explained defeat differently. At the opening of the 1981 Brinks trial he rejected the court's authority and the category "defendant", called himself a prisoner of war, and said: "Repression breeds resistance." ([Kuwasi Balagoon, statement at the opening of the Brinks trial, 1981](https://archive.org/download/statement-by-new-afrikan-prisoner-of-war-kuwasi-balagoon-031-merged/StatementByNewAfrikanPrisonerOfWarKuwasiBalagoon031_merged_djvu.txt)). He argued that the BLA had survived police claims that it was broken and placed his formation in the Panthers, Malcolm X, Robert Williams and Mao. The judge stopped him before he finished, so the text is incomplete: an argument about legitimacy, not an account of the robbery or shootings.

In a later piece published in 2019 at an unstated date, Balagoon blamed informers rather than infiltrated agents and wrote: "A revolutionary, and a revolutionary combatant in particular, must have impeccable character." ([Kuwasi Balagoon, "On Traitors", published 2019, date of writing unknown](https://archive.org/download/on-traitors-reading/On%20Traitors%20Reading_djvu.txt)). He gave his own count of informers and named people he held responsible. These are one participant's accusations, never tested in court; they show how defeat was understood inside the underground, not findings about those people. The text endorses violent internal discipline. That language is his and should not be softened.

## Black feminism and a common front

The question of who could speak for the movement was taken up by the Combahee River Collective, a Boston group of Black feminists meeting since 1974. Their statement, dated April 1977, defined their politics as struggle against racial, sexual, heterosexual and class oppression and wrote that "the major systems of oppression are interlocking" ([Combahee River Collective, statement dated April 1977](https://archive.org/stream/Combahee1979/Combahee%201979_djvu.txt)). It declared: "We realize that the liberation of all oppressed peoples necessitates the destruction of the political-economic systems of capitalism and imperialism as well as patriarchy." ([Combahee River Collective, 1977](https://archive.org/stream/Combahee1979/Combahee%201979_djvu.txt)). They had worked in the Black liberation and women's movements and found both wanting. They broke with the National Black Feminist Organization over what they called its "bourgeois-feminist stance" and listed work on sterilisation abuse, abortion, battered women, rape and health care. "Identity politics" belongs here to 1977; the statement records a programme, not results.

Twenty years on, the Black Radical Congress tried to hold the traditions together. Its Principles of Unity, adopted in June 1998, described a "center without walls" for Black working and poor people, recognised socialism, revolutionary nationalism and feminism as tendencies within Black radicalism, and said: "We reject racial and biological determinism, Black patriarchy and Black capitalism as solutions to problems facing Black people." ([Black Radical Congress, Principles of Unity, 1998](https://web.archive.org/web/20010127135900/http://www.blackradicalcongress.org/aboutus/principles.html)). Its Freedom Agenda, ratified in Baltimore on 17 April 1999, set fifteen points covering employment, health, education, prisons, police brutality, the death penalty, gender equality, LGBT rights, reparations and international solidarity. It framed the fight as follows: "We will fight to advance beyond capitalism, which has demonstrated its structural incapacity to address basic human needs worldwide and, in particular, the needs of Black people." ([Black Radical Congress, Freedom Agenda, ratified 17 April 1999](https://web.archive.org/web/20010127123300/http://www.blackradicalcongress.org/aboutus/freedomagenda.html)). These are coalition documents: agreed language rather than the disagreements behind it, and proposals rather than achievements.

## How historians have read it

- **Akinyele Omowale Umoja, "Repression Breeds Resistance: The Black Liberation Army and the Radical Legacy of the Black Panther Party" (1999).** Argues from movement documents and autobiographies that Panther members carried the party's radical programme into the BLA after 1971; agrees with Muntaqim on the centrality of the split but gives more weight to squads such as Geronimo ji Jaga's than Muntaqim's own narrative does. [Internet Archive: Resistance djvu](https://archive.org/download/rep_resist/Resistance_djvu.txt)
- **John Jones, "Unite or Perish!: A History of the Revolutionary Action Movement" (MA thesis, George Washington University, 2019).** Synthesises RAM's internal documents and the Muhammad Ahmad papers to argue that RAM has been underrated because it was clandestine and its records were seized; agrees with Umoja that RAM shaped the Panthers' theoretical vocabulary while stressing Third World and Maoist sources more than the domestic-organising question. [Source: r.jina.ai](https://r.jina.ai/https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/1960-1970/jones-ram-history.pdf)
- **Robin D. G. Kelley and Betsy Esch, "Black Like Mao: Red China and Black Revolution", in *Afro Asia* (Duke University Press, 2008).** Describes RAM as the first sustained postwar attempt to combine Marxism, Black nationalism and Third World internationalism; cited in Jones's thesis, so no wording is taken from it directly here.
- **Peniel E. Joseph, *Waiting 'Till the Midnight Hour: A Narrative History of Black Power in America* (Henry Holt, 2006).** Treats Black Power as a current running through and beyond the civil rights era rather than a simple reaction against it; cited in Jones's thesis, so no wording is taken from it directly here.
- **Anne-Marie Angelo, "The Black Panthers in London, 1967–1972: A Diasporic Struggle Navigates the Black Atlantic", *Radical History Review* 103 (2009).** The main scholarly study of the British Black Panthers and their unaffiliated relationship to the US party; its existence marks the international-chapter gap noted below. [Source: rememberolivemorris.files.wordpress.com](https://rememberolivemorris.files.wordpress.com/2009/08/rhr-angelo.pdf)

## Limits of the record

The surviving testimony is uneven and politically shaped. Nearly every source comes from a leader, a defendant or an organisation seeking support. There is no testimony from ordinary members, families, or those who opposed these groups within Black communities; the Combahee women are a partial exception, though theirs is a collective manifesto. RAM's records were hidden or destroyed, and BLA clandestinity left no ordinary archives. The Brinks statement is incomplete and "On Traitors" may be unfinished. The RAM journal and BLA pamphlets are scans with transcription errors, and the 2023 *Collected Works* editor states that gendered language was changed. Quotations come from digital text; physical page images have not been examined and no editorial verification beyond URL and text checks has occurred. The Congress pages survive only as captures of a site that no longer carries them.

No French or Spanish sources were consulted, and no first-hand source from a Panther chapter outside the United States was verified. The British Black Panther oral histories, the Freedom Archives RAM collection and the ProQuest *Papers of the Revolutionary Action Movement, 1962–1996* remain untapped leads; the last two would need a subscription or a physical visit.

These texts contain inflammatory rhetoric about white people, endorse armed action and, in Balagoon's case, describe violent internal discipline. The chapter reports those positions, attributes them to their authors, and neither adopts nor softens them. Claims about wages, lynching, informers or the scale of state violence belong to the speakers unless independently corroborated.
