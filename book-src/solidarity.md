On 31 August 1980, the day the Gdańsk Agreement was signed, Krzysztof Korczyński photographed two plywood boards mounted by Gate No. 2 of the Lenin Shipyard. They carried twenty-one demands painted for the strike that had shut the yard. The image, held by the European Solidarity Centre, shows the list as a public claim addressed to people beyond the factory. The first demand was the one that mattered most: "Akceptacja niezależnych od partii i pracodawców wolnych związków zawodowych, wynikająca z ratyfikowanej przez PRL Konwencji nr 87 Międzynarodowej Organizacji Pracy dotyczącej wolności związkowych." ([The 21 Demands, point 1, Gdańsk, 16–17 August 1980](https://pl.wikisource.org/wiki/21_postulat%C3%B3w_Mi%C4%99dzyzak%C5%82adowego_Komitetu_Strajkowego_z_17_sierpnia_1980)). In translation: "Acceptance of free trade unions independent of the party and employers, arising from ILO Convention No. 87 ratified by the PRL, concerning freedom of association." The reference was to an international treaty on freedom of association Poland had ratified.

## The boards at Gate No. 2

The strike was a workplace occupation with an explicit political programme. The Inter-Factory Strike Committee, the MKS, drew the list from demands sent in by striking workplaces. With free unions went a right to strike, free publication, the reinstatement of workers dismissed after the strikes of 1970 and 1976, and the release of named political prisoners. Economic demands sat beside these: a 2,000-złoty rise, food supply, cheaper meat and free Saturdays. Arkadiusz Rybicki, who helped paint the boards, recalled years later: "Do komisji wpłynęło około 700 postulatów" ([Arkadiusz Rybicki, interviewed by Polskie Radio, published 17 August 2012](https://polskieradio24.pl/artykul/256689,jak-robotnicy-tworzyli-21-postulatow)), rendered as "About 700 demands reached the commission." The final twenty-one were a narrowing of a wider set that included leaving the Soviet-led alliance; the number is a recollection, not a minute.

The protocol shows what was conceded and deferred. Officials accepted unions outside the state trade-union register, the CRZZ, and promised no discrimination. The MKS declared the unions would not act as a political party and affirmed "socialist" ownership and the leading role of the PZPR, the ruling communist party. The right to strike, censorship reform and the five-day week were all put off to future bills. The record fixes the strike's end at five on 31 August and notes that Andrzej Gwiazda did not sign. Deadlines are evidence of what was left for later, not of delivery.

## Fifteen months

For fifteen months Solidarity existed legally. It published a national weekly, the first issue on 3 April 1981, and at its September congress delegates addressed workers in other communist-ruled countries, drawing condemnation from several allied leaderships. Lech Wałęsa, the electrician who chaired the strike committee, described the agreement from Oslo when he accepted the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983: "Addressing you, as the winner of the 1983 Nobel Peace Prize, is a Polish worker from the Gdansk Shipyard, one of the founders of the independent trade union movement in Poland." ([Lech Wałęsa, Nobel lecture, 11 December 1983, Nobel Foundation English translation, read by Bogdan Cywinski](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/1983/walesa/lecture/)). He called the agreement a charter of workers' rights and insisted Solidarity had not sought power: "During the 15 months of 'Solidarity's' legal existence nobody was killed or wounded as a result of its activities." ([Lech Wałęsa, Nobel lecture, 11 December 1983](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/1983/walesa/lecture/)). That was his claim, not a checked statistic, and the lecture was self-defence delivered while he was interned. Of what followed he said, "Let the veil of silence fall presently over what happened afterwards. Silence, too, can speak out." ([Lech Wałęsa, Nobel lecture, 11 December 1983](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/1983/walesa/lecture/)).

On 12–13 December 1981 the government imposed martial law, rule by the military with strict controls. Władysław Frasyniuk, leader in Wrocław, learned of it in a station restaurant: "Nobody imagined that this seemingly weak government would prove strong enough to turn the police (who had set up their own independent trade union) or the army (our workmates and neighbors) on us." ([Władysław Frasyniuk, interview of June 1982, in *Konspira: Solidarity Underground*](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/konspira-solidarity-underground.html)). Bogdan Lis, vice-chair at Gdańsk, drove past trucks of ZOMO, the riot police, and judged that "Solidarity was shooting off at the mouth while the authorities were getting ready to shoot for real." ([Bogdan Lis, interview of January or February 1984, in *Konspira*](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/konspira-solidarity-underground.html)). Bogdan Borusewicz, an older organiser who had resigned, recalled: "I simply began to see how people were changing, how ambition and position were going to the heads of people who used to be good friends" ([Bogdan Borusewicz, interview of October/November 1982 or March 1984, in *Konspira*](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/konspira-solidarity-underground.html)). The interviewers removed their questions, selected from tapes and wrote the links; these are witnesses judging their own movement.

## Underground

Most leaders were interned, but Bujak, Frasyniuk and Lis evaded arrest and rebuilt the union in hiding with couriers, safe apartments, a newspaper, *Tygodnik Mazowsze*, and a radio station whose first eight-and-a-half-minute broadcast went out on 12 June 1982. Bujak explained why the union was defenceless: its openness, a virtue in a democratic body, made hiding funds and presses impossible. "We planned to hide fifty million but we only managed ten." ([Zbigniew Bujak, interview of October 1982 or February 1984, in *Konspira: Solidarity Underground*](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/konspira-solidarity-underground.html)). Frasyniuk blamed mental rather than technical preparation; contingency instructions of November 1981 reached only a fraction of factories. Borusewicz described a cult of leadership and a lack of trust that made clandestine work impossible. His bitterness is part of the evidence, since he had resigned. A union of millions survived as leaflets and shortwave transmissions.

## The road to the Round Table

By 1988 the government faced strikes again and began talking. On television on 30 November, Wałęsa debated Alfred Miodowicz, chairman of the official unions, who conceded missing medicines, queues and debt but argued pluralism would bring chaos. Wałęsa replied: "Political monopoly, trade union monopoly, economic monopoly—these must finally be crushed." ([Lech Wałęsa in the televised debate with Alfred Miodowicz, 30 November 1988, in *Droga do wolności* (1991), translated by James Bjork](https://1989.rrchnm.org/items/show/540.html)). The excerpt comes from Wałęsa's own book, so the selection may favour him.

Before the talks, Wałęsa treated procedure as the first terrain: "The organization of the 'Roundtable' talks has not been, as yet, precisely defined. Preliminary arrangements are needed very quickly." ([Lech Wałęsa, note of 1988, translated by Jan Chowaniec](https://1989.rrchnm.org/items/show/144.html)). Andrzej Slowik, an activist excluded from the delegation, wrote in February 1989: "The 'Solidarity's' delegation represents only one group, and even if it is now a group in control of the main spheres of the Union's life, it is still only one group" ([Andrzej Slowik to Władysław Findeisen, 12 February 1989, translated by Jan Chowaniec](https://1989.rrchnm.org/items/show/486.html)). He warned that a deal made by one group might be rejected by society. These sources do not settle whether the Round Table was a necessary compromise or a surrender; they show both claims.

## What the record cannot establish

Most primary material is in Polish. The two central documents survive as transcriptions, one without a stated printed source, and the signed protocol should be checked in the European Solidarity Centre or IPN before fine wording is printed. The surviving English testimony is weighted to leaders: the *Konspira* interviews were edited by journalists, the Nobel lecture is a translated self-defence, the 1988–89 papers are leaders' papers. Rank-and-file members, rural Solidarity and families of internees are largely absent. Women appear as signatories, Anna Walentynowicz, Alina Pienkowska and Henryka Krzywonos, but give no extended account here. Rybicki's 700 figure was remembered decades later and cannot be checked. No verified Radio Free Europe broadcast exists here; passing references in *Konspira* show only that the broadcasts were part of the environment. A CIA item on the accords and the full *Droga do wolności* remain unverified. The sources assembled here do not establish casualty figures beyond a parliamentary commission figure of more than ninety dead and nine killed at Wujek, nor a verified membership beyond the repeated ten million, though the effects of the 1989 settlement are documented elsewhere.

## How historians have read it

- **Timothy Garton Ash, *The Polish Revolution: Solidarity* (1983; 3rd ed., Yale University Press, 2002).** A participant-observer's narrative built around named places and moments, the shipyard, Rzeszów, the March 1981 crisis and "War", followed by a reflective section asking "What revolution?"; it treats Solidarity as both a workers' movement and a national one. [Internet Archive: polishrevolution00timo](https://archive.org/details/polishrevolution00timo)
- **Roman Laba, *The Roots of Solidarity: A Political Sociology of Poland's Working-Class Democratization* (Princeton University Press, 1991).** A sociological account that stresses the working-class and workplace roots of 1980, standing closer to the strike records than readings that treat Solidarity chiefly as an intelligentsia project. [Internet Archive: rootsofsolidarit0000laba](https://archive.org/details/rootsofsolidarit0000laba)
- **Jan Kubik, *The Power of Symbols against the Symbols of Power: The Rise of Solidarity and the Fall of State Socialism in Poland* (Pennsylvania State University Press, 1994).** A cultural-sociological study of how symbols and rituals worked as political weapons, which helps explain the boards, posters, masses and underground names in this record. [Internet Archive: powerofsymbolsag0000kubi](https://archive.org/details/powerofsymbolsag0000kubi)
- **Michael Szporer (ed.), *Solidarity: The Great Workers Strike of 1980* (Lexington Books; edition verified 2014).** A collection of twenty-five interviews with workers and intellectual allies of Solidarity, supplying rank-and-file voices missing from the leadership-heavy sources above. [Internet Archive: solidaritygreatw0000szpo](https://archive.org/details/solidaritygreatw0000szpo)

Broad agreement holds that the Gdańsk agreements created a genuine independent union, that martial law destroyed its legal existence, and that the Round Table and the June 1989 election ended communist rule in Poland. Live disagreement concerns the weight to give class against nation, whether the strategy of limiting Solidarity's aims was realistic or a surrender, and how far the settlement was imposed by a narrow leadership. The first-hand sources document the disagreement rather than resolve it. These books' arguments are described from catalogue and contents records rather than from full texts.
