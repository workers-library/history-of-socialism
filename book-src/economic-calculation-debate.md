Economic calculation means comparing the cost of different uses of a scarce resource in order to choose between them. In an economy with capital markets, meaning markets in which savers lend to firms and receive a price called interest, the prices of factories and raw materials carry much of that comparison. Socialist economies largely abolished such markets in the means of production. How, then, did a planner decide whether steel should go into a bridge or a lathe? They answer unevenly, with official doctrine, a Chilean computer experiment and admissions of failure. They do not contain the classic books on the subject. That absence shapes what can be said.

The Soviet textbook tradition treated planning as a law rather than a choice. "Social, socialist ownership of the means of production, and large-scale socialist production both in industry and in agriculture, make the planned, proportional development of the whole economy objectively possible and necessary," the Academy of Sciences textbook stated, calling planning "an economic law of socialism" and "the regulator of socialist industry" ([*Political Economy*, chapter 30](https://www.marxists.org/subject/economy/authors/pe/pe-ch30.htm)). Stalin's 1952 *Economic Problems* drew a harder distinction. Commodity production means making goods for sale rather than for direct use. Where it existed, "the law of value must also exist". That is the rule that goods tend to exchange according to the labour socially necessary to produce them. But "the law of value has no regulating function in our socialist production, but it nevertheless influences production, and this fact cannot be ignored when directing production" ([Stalin, *Economic Problems*, §3](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/stalin/works/1951/economic-problems/ch04.htm)). Mao criticized the textbook from the other side, complaining that it "is not concerned with people; it considers things, not people" ([Mao, "Critique of Stalin's *Economic Problems of Socialism in the USSR*"](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-8/mswv8_66.htm)). These are arguments about what ought to govern allocation, not measurements of prices at work.

The textbook did allow a practical device. Economic accounting meant comparing inputs and outputs in money terms to check profitability. It was "a method of planned economic management in socialist enterprises, based on the operation of the law of value" ([*Political Economy*, chapter 34](https://www.marxists.org/subject/economy/authors/pe/pe-ch34.htm)). Money prices were used inside enterprises but denied the role of allocating investment between industries. Stalin defined the aim as "the securing of the maximum satisfaction of the constantly rising material and cultural requirements of the whole of society through the continuous expansion and perfection of socialist production on the basis of higher techniques" ([*Economic Problems*, chapter 8](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/stalin/works/1951/economic-problems/ch08.htm)), a target with no account of how it is to be calculated. The textbook also claimed that under socialism "capital, surplus-value, capitalist profit, price of production, wage-labour" would cease to exist ([*Political Economy*, chapter 29](https://www.marxists.org/subject/economy/authors/pe/pe-ch29.htm)). That is a claim about a future society, not a finding about one.

## Information and its limits

The book's most direct attempt to solve the information problem is cybernetics, the study of control and communication in machines and living things. Norbert Wiener, who coined the word, described feedback as feeding information about the result of an action back into a system so that it could adjust: "It is these boundary regions of science which offer the richest opportunities to the qualified investigator" ([Wiener, *Cybernetics*, 1948](https://archive.org/download/wiener-1938/Cybernetics%20or%20Communication%20and%20Control%20in%20the%20Animal%20and%20the%20Machine%20-%20Norbert%20Wiene_OCR_djvu.txt)). In the Soviet Union the idea became a philosophical argument. Andryushchenko insisted that "Man predetermined the character of the reaction," because the purpose came from outside ([Andryushchenko, 1960](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA357950/DTIC_ADA357950_djvu.txt)). Whether cybernetics was reformist language or administrative power is contested: Gerovitch reads it as a discourse absorbed by orthodoxy, while Peters gives more weight to bureaucratic obstacles in the failed Soviet computer network ([Gerovitch](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/3137.001.0001); [Peters](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9780262034180.001.0001)). No primary text by the network's chief architect was opened.

In Allende's Chile the problem was tackled in factories rather than in philosophy. Stafford Beer built a Telex network, a teleprinter system sending typed messages over wires, linking enterprises to Santiago. An algedonic channel, named from the Greek for pain and pleasure, carried urgent warnings upward. Beer described the aim: "The project aimed to acquire the benefits of cybernetic synergy for the whole of industry, while devolving power to the workers at the same time" ([Beer, Zaheer Lecture, 5 December 1974](https://archive.org/download/agt-beer-73a580/beer_djvu.txt)). His own account narrows the claim. The system covered about seventy-five per cent of nationalised industry; the large animated model never worked; the economy was never commanded from the operations room; and reports were meant to stay confidential to each enterprise so that monitoring would not become central direction. Whether that devolution was real is disputed by critics whom Beer names but does not quote, and historians stress that the project was built jointly with Chilean engineers ([Medina, *Cybernetic Revolutionaries*](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/8417.001.0001)). None of these sources measures its economic effect.

## Incentives and the human cost

Information was only half the question. Stalin tied productivity to consumption directly: "If there is a shortage of bread, a shortage of butter and fats, a shortage of textiles, and if housing conditions are bad, freedom will not carry you very far" ([Stalin, speech to Stakhanovites, 17 November 1935](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/year-of-the-stakhanovite/year-of-the-stakhanovite-texts/stalin-at-the-conference-of-stakhanovites/)). Guevara subordinated material incentives to moral ones in 1965 ([Guevara, March 1965](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)). Decades later Cuba's congress reported that its model "does not provide sufficient incentives for work or innovation" ([Raúl Castro, *Central Report*, 16 April 2021](https://en.granma.cu/cuba/2021-02-16/central-report-to-the-eighth-congress-of-the-communist-party-of-cuba)). One aims to change people, the other diagnoses a model that failed to reward them.

Historical research checks the official claims. Mark Harrison concludes that the Soviet economy was designed to build national power rather than to converge on Western living standards: it achieved rapid industrialisation and military strength by repressing consumption, while underlying productivity growth was never lifted ([Harrison, CEPR, 2017](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/soviet-economy-1917-1991-its-life-and-afterlife)). Richard Connolly describes a centrally planned system that delivered industrialisation and military success, and asks at what human cost ([Connolly, *The Russian Economy*, 2020](https://academic.oup.com/book/28460/chapter/229054563)). Neither treats the textbook's claim that planning was an objective economic law as settled. The sources here do not rank the explanations for poor productivity: weak incentives, absent market prices, political priorities and the cost of repression all appear, but none is isolated by a measurement.

## The debate that is not here

The socialist calculation debate is a large gap. Ludwig von Mises, Friedrich Hayek, Oskar Lange and Abba Lerner are not represented in this source base. There is no text by any of them here, and no chapter that summarises their arguments. The same is true of János Kornai, Michał Kalecki, Joan Robinson and Elinor Ostrom. Nothing in this chapter should be read as an account of what those writers argued; quoting them or assigning them positions would require sources that are not present.

What the book does contain is the shape of the question in other vocabularies. The textbook states the case for planning as an economic law; Stalin concedes that commodity exchange still requires the law of value; Mao treats the issue as one of things rather than people ([*Political Economy*, chapter 30](https://www.marxists.org/subject/economy/authors/pe/pe-ch30.htm); [Stalin, §3](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/stalin/works/1951/economic-problems/ch04.htm); [Mao](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-8/mswv8_66.htm)). The transition chapter records the argument over whether state ownership was socialism or a new capitalism: Pannekoek wrote that in Russia "the work is planned, financed and managed by the state; the directors of industry are appointed by the state and profits are considered the income of the state," while Bordiga held that wherever commodity production and wage labour exist the economy is capitalist ([Pannekoek, 1936](https://www.marxists.org/archive/pannekoe/1936/dictatorship.htm); [Bordiga, 1952](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bordiga/works/1952/stalin.htm)). The cybernetics chapter asks whether information can be gathered without becoming central command ([Beer](https://archive.org/download/agt-beer-73a580/beer_djvu.txt)). These fragments cannot substitute for the missing literature.

## Limits of the record

No archive or original text was consulted. The official texts are translations or transcriptions carried by other chapters, and the Soviet textbook is doctrine written long after the events it describes. The Chilean material is one participant's retrospective, not a survey, and no enterprise records test his percentages. The historians cited agree broadly about industrialisation and its human cost but supply no enterprise-level price data. The gravest gap is the calculation literature. Mises, Hayek, Lange and Lerner should be read directly, with the later critiques of planning by Kornai and the institutional work of Ostrom, before any verdict is attempted on allocating capital without capital markets. Kalecki and Robinson test the absent side. This is a priority for future research, not a footnote.
