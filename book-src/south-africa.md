The Communist Party of South Africa was founded in Cape Town on 30 July 1921 by a merger of socialist bodies. Its manifesto "makes its appeal to all South African workers, organised and unorganised, white and black, to join in promoting the overthrow of the capitalist system..." ([founding manifesto, 30 July 1921](http://www.sacp.org.za/docs/history/manifesto21.html)). It sought a Commonwealth of Workers, anticipated repression and linked black labour to the world capitalist system. The years that followed show how hard that appeal was to hold.

## The Native Republic argument

In 1928 the Executive Committee of the Communist International, the body that directed communist parties across the world, called South Africa a colony of a special kind. It noted that the CPSA was then predominantly African in composition, with about 1,750 members, and told the party to join daily racial demands to "the slogan of an independent native South African republic as a stage towards a workers' and peasants' republic, with full equal rights for all races, black, coloured and white" ([ECCI resolution, 1928](https://omalley.nelsonmandela.org/index.php/site/q/03lv01538/04lv01600/05lv01603/06lv01605.htm)). This was the "Native Republic" line: a national democratic revolution had to precede socialism.

The line was contested. E. R. Roux, just back from Moscow, wrote to the general secretary Douglas Wolton on 5 September 1928: "It is equally necessary to say 'natives unite; unite as black men to free yourselves from slavery'." ([Roux to Wolton, 5 September 1928](https://omalley.nelsonmandela.org/index.php/site/q/03lv01538/04lv01600/05lv01603/06lv01604.htm)). He wanted a workers' and peasants' republic as the basis for a government of the native majority, argued that a blunt Black Republic slogan would cost the party its footing in white trade unions, and complained that the framers refused even an editorial change. The letter proves the instruction was disputed, not how widely.

The two-stage idea survived. The SACP's clandestine programme of 1962, *The Road to South African Freedom*, named "colonialism of a special type" as the condition to defeat: "The destruction of colonialism and the winning of national freedom is the essential condition and the key for future advance to the supreme aim of the Communist Party: the establishment of a socialist South Africa" ([SACP programme, 1962](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sections/sacp/1962/road-freedom.htm)). It rejected "African Socialism" as a substitute for Marxism-Leninism. The continuity from 1928 to 1962 is in the party's own words. A programme states doctrine, not which members read it.

## Wages, land and care

Economic demands were never an afterthought. In 1943 the African National Congress, or ANC, the principal African nationalist organisation, adopted claims for universal suffrage, the repeal of the pass laws, land and equal pay, including "the statutory recognition of the right of the African worker to collective bargaining under the Industrial Conciliation Act." ([ANC claims, 16 December 1943](https://omalley.nelsonmandela.org/index.php/site/q/03lv01538/04lv01600/05lv01609/06lv01610.htm)). The colour bar, rules reserving skilled jobs for white workers, was what organisers met. Billy Nair, a Durban trade unionist and MK member, recalled the 1922 miners' rebellion and its slogan "Workers of the World unite for a White South Africa". "The slogan 'Workers of the World unite for a White South Africa' does violence to the militancy and heroism displayed by the workers." ([Nair, "Through the Eyes of the Workers"](https://omalley.nelsonmandela.org/index.php/site/q/03lv01538/04lv02009/05lv02010/06lv02022/07lv02023.htm)). His essay is a later polemic.

The Women's Charter of 17 April 1954 joined national liberation to the subordination of women. It began: "We women share with our menfolk the cares and anxieties imposed by poverty and its evils." ([Women's Charter, FEDSAW, 17 April 1954](https://sahistory.org.za/article/womens-charter)). It demanded equal pay, property and marriage rights, free compulsory education, maternity homes and creches, and named the burdens on women left in towns and on farms while husbands worked in the mines. FEDSAW was the Federation of South African Women. Frances Baard remembered building it from a meeting of forty or fifty women in Port Elizabeth in 1953 and the conference of about 150 delegates that adopted the Charter in 1954. She recalled Florence Matomela refusing a three-minute speaking limit: "'I am a defiers, and I shall speak for as long as I like!'" ([Baard, "Forming the Federation"](https://sahistory.org.za/archive/forming-federation)). This was remembered years later, not minuted at the time.

The Freedom Charter, adopted at Kliptown on 26 June 1955, gathered the demands into a common programme. "The mineral wealth beneath the soil, the Banks and monopoly industry shall be transferred to the ownership of the people as a whole" ([Freedom Charter, 1955](http://sahistory.org.za/article/freedom-charter)). Its labour clauses asked for union rights, equal pay, a forty-hour week, a minimum wage and an end to child and contract labour. It was a compromise, and its economic clauses were later disputed by Africanist and Marxist critics.

## Sharpeville and the turn to arms

The Treason Trial of 1956 to 1961 was the state's attempt to prove that the Congress Alliance was a communist conspiracy. Mandela, speaking for the accused in 1960, said the Youth League had failed to expel communists and that he was drawn to a classless society while the ANC had no policy on one: "Well, I don't know if I did become a Communist." ([Treason Trial testimony, 1960](https://omalley.nelsonmandela.org/index.php/site/q/03lv01538/04lv01600/05lv01615/06lv01616.htm)). He described stay-at-homes and economic pressure as the road to a people's democracy. This is an edited selection from testimony given as part of a defence.

On 21 March 1960 police fired on a crowd at Sharpeville. Humphrey Tyler of *Drum*, who was there, said: "Before the shooting, I heard no warning to the crowd to disperse. There was no warning volley." ([Tyler, 21 March 1960](http://sahistory.org.za/article/eyewitness-accounts-sharpeville-massacre-1960)). The photographer Ian Berry described finding many of the dead in the ground he had just walked across. These accounts contradict the police claim of stoning and "ferocious weapons", though two of the three witnesses were white reporters who did not speak the languages of the crowd. The official figure was 69 dead; Nancy Clark and William Worger, using testimony under oath and hospital records, argue for at least 91 ([*Voices of Sharpeville*, 2023](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9781003257806-6/massacre-nancy-clark-william-worger)). The witnesses cannot settle the total.

On 16 December 1961 Umkhonto we Sizwe, or MK, announced its first sabotage attacks. "The time comes in the life of any nation when there remain only two choices: submit or fight. That time has now come to South Africa." ([MK manifesto, 16 December 1961](https://www.anc1912.org.za/anc-manifesto-1961-manifesto-of-umkhonto-we-sizwe)). It was a body of all races under the movement's political guidance, cited Sharpeville and promised to avoid bloodshed. At Rivonia Mandela explained the turn: "It was only when all else had failed, when all channels of peaceful protest had been barred to us, that the decision was made to embark on violent forms of political struggle, and to form Umkhonto we Sizwe." ([Mandela's statement from the dock, 20 April 1964](https://omalley.nelsonmandela.org/index.php/site/q/03lv01538/04lv01600/05lv01630/06lv01631.htm)). Sabotage was chosen because it did not take life, he said.

Police found another document at Rivonia in July 1963. Operation Mayibuye, drawn up by MK's High Command, proposed guerrilla bands, foreign training and supply, a Provisional Revolutionary Government and 7,000 men in four areas. "The white state has thrown overboard every pretence of rule by democratic process" ([Operation Mayibuye, May 1963](https://omalley.nelsonmandela.org/index.php/site/q/03lv01538/04lv01600/05lv01626/06lv01628.htm)). This was a plan, not an outcome; nothing shows it was approved or attempted. Its recovery gave the prosecution a central exhibit. Stephen Ellis argues for Mandela's underground SACP membership and decisive party influence on the armed turn; Tom Lodge and Thula Simpson accept strong evidence of association while warning that the sources do not establish everything Ellis claims. Mandela's own words resist the claim that he was directed by communists.

## The prison years

Ahmed Kathrada, a Rivonia co-defendant imprisoned for twenty-six years, described his arrival on Robben Island on 13 June 1964 in a speech of 1993: "We cannot forget the first months at the quarry where we mined stone - we came back with blisters, bloody hands, and sore muscles." ([Kathrada, 26 May 1993](http://overcomingapartheid.msu.edu/sidebar/kid=163-581-8)). Stone quarrying, cold cement floors and cold showers lasted for years, and clothing was differentiated by race. Sedick Isaacs, imprisoned from 1964 to 1979, told an oral-history project: "The day I found myself in prison I made the resolution to resist imprisonment, to continually endeavour to escape" ([Robben Island Museum oral histories](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/9AXxnEns2W2KKw)). He described repeated escape attempts and a duplicate cell key made from measurements of a British-made lock and ground from a blank in the quarry blacksmith's shop. Recorded decades later for a public exhibit, they are the voices of prisoners other than Mandela; the key-making story is a participant's claim.

## Limits of the record

Programmes and court statements are elite texts. Most testimony comes from leaders, exiles and men, with Frances Baard as the exception. The archives are curated digital collections. The O'Malley archive was compiled by one scholar for the Nelson Mandela Foundation; its public pages preserve the distinction between Mandela's prepared Rivonia speech and the words he delivered, though the archive item itself was unavailable. SAHO reproduces documents selected by its staff. The Treason Trial material is an extract from an anthology and the Sharpeville accounts a selection of three witnesses. Afrikaans, isiZulu, isiXhosa and Sesotho sources were not searched, and the archives at Wits, Pretoria, Robben Island and Moscow were not visited. The Sharpeville Commission transcripts and *Regina v. Monyake* were identified but not read. Book-length memoirs by Ray Alexander Simons, Ahmed Kathrada and Ruth First remain outside this record. Unrecorded conversations in the High Command, the authorship of Operation Mayibuye and the extent of SACP direction of the ANC cannot be reconstructed. Party programmes cannot say what socialism would have meant after 1994, and testimony about the years after 1976 was not gathered, so the account stops in 1964.

## How historians have read it

- **Nancy L. Clark and William H. Worger, *Voices of Sharpeville: The Long History of Racial Injustice* (Routledge, 2023).** Uses testimony given under oath in 1960 and 1961 and later interviews with survivors to argue that the official death toll was understated and the crowd was peaceful. [Source: taylorfrancis.com](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9781003257806-6/massacre-nancy-clark-william-worger)
- **Jack and Ray Simons, *Class and Colour in South Africa, 1850–1950* (1969; 1983).** The central "Communist school" account, arguing that the class struggle merged with the national liberation struggle. Its interpretation is summarised and contested in the review below.
- **Lucien van der Walt, "Triumph and tragedy of a South African Communist: Ray Alexander Simons, 1914–2004" (2007).** Argues that the Communist-school narrative treats the Comintern's two-stage line and the Soviet bloc uncritically and sidelines anarchist and syndicalist traditions. It is a critique from the libertarian left and should be read as such. [Source: theanarchistlibrary.org](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/lucien-van-der-walt-triumph-and-tragedy-of-a-south-african-communist)
- **Stephen Ellis, "Nelson Mandela, the South African Communist Party and the origins of Umkhonto we Sizwe," *Cold War History* 16, no. 1 (2016), pp. 1–18.** Argues for Mandela's underground SACP membership and decisive party influence on the armed turn. [Taylor & Francis: 2015.1078315](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14682745.2015.1078315)
- **Tom Lodge, "Mandela and the Left," *Journal of Southern African Studies* (2019), and Thula Simpson, "Nelson Mandela and the Genesis of the ANC's Armed Struggle: Notes on Method," *JSAS* 44, no. 1 (2018), pp. 133–148.** Both accept substantial evidence of association with the left while warning that the sources do not establish everything Ellis claims; Simpson concentrates on method and the limits of the surviving record. [Taylor & Francis: 2019.1691818](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03057070.2019.1691818) and [Taylor & Francis: 2018.1404264](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03057070.2018.1404264)
- **"Debating the ANC's external links during the struggle against apartheid" (ASC Leiden, PDF).** A collective critique of Ellis and Macmillan, noting that Mandela consistently denied party membership and that claims of SACP control are selectively sourced. [Source: ascleiden.nl](https://ascleiden.nl/sites/default/files/anc-debate-ellis-macmillan-africajournal.pdf)

Broad agreement: the CPSA and SACP were central to the Congress Alliance, the two-stage strategy shaped ANC thinking and the 1961 sabotage campaign marked a real break. Live disagreement: over Mandela's party membership, over who drove the turn to arms, over whether the Native Republic line was imposed or locally arrived at, and over whether the "Communist school" of historiography is apologetic or accurate. These positions are not equally supported. The documentary record is strongest for the existence of the strategy and weakest for questions of individual membership and private intention.
