On 15 March 1921, with the revolt at Kronstadt still unresolved, Lenin told the Tenth Party Congress that the revolution had to give way to the peasantry. The New Economic Policy, or NEP, is the name later given to that retreat: a fixed tax in place of requisitioning, and permission for peasants to sell what remained in markets. "The question of substituting a tax for surplus-grain appropriation is primarily and mainly a political question, for it is essentially a question of the attitude of the working class to the peasantry." J. P. Goodrich, surveying the Volga famine zone for the American Relief Administration, chose villages away from the railways and found people eating cabbage leaves and melon rinds ([Lenin at the Tenth Party Congress](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1921/tenth-party-congress/tenth-party-congress-texts/lenin-explains-the-tax-in-kind-and-the-new-policy); [Goodrich's report](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1921/famine-of-1921-22/famine-of-1921-22-texts/american-descriptions-of-the-volga-famine)).

Lenin's official case was that state ownership and management could serve a workers' government. "State capitalism would be a step forward as compared with the present state of affairs in our Soviet Republic," he wrote in April 1921, naming concessions, co-operatives, commission agents and leases. That was a justification of a retreat, not a neutral analysis. Pannekoek and Bordiga read the same institutions from the opposite end, one calling Soviet industry state capitalism, the other holding that wherever commodities and wage labour circulate the economy is capitalist ([Lenin, *The Tax in Kind*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1921/apr/21.htm); [Pannekoek, 1936](https://www.marxists.org/archive/pannekoe/1936/dictatorship.htm); [Bordiga, 1952](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bordiga/works/1952/stalin.htm)).

## The Soviet precedent: crisis, retreat and argument

Walter Duranty watched cafes reopen and traced a Finnish trader who moved from a trestle table to a shop. A Nepman, the period's word for a private trader, became a recognisable type, though Markoosha Fischer remembered that NEP also brought unemployment, with young workers shed first. By October 1923 industrial prices stood at 276 per cent of their 1913 level against 89 per cent for agricultural prices, a historian's calculation known as the scissors because the two lines pulled apart. Trotsky demanded cheaper nails, cloth and matches rather than a repeat of the slogan of the *smychka*, the bond between town and country; Bukharin replied that conflict between worker and peasant was possible rather than inevitable ([Duranty](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1921/new-economic-policy/new-economic-policy-texts/moscow-from-ara-to-nep/); [Fischer](https://www.thetedkarchive.com/library/markoosha-fischer-my-lives-in-russia); [Siegelbaum](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/scissors-crisis/); [Trotsky](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1923/newcourse/ch06.htm); [Bukharin](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/scissors-crisis/scissors-crisis-texts/bukharin-on-the-worker-peasant-alliance/)).

Lenin's last article "On Cooperation" conceded that "by adopting NEP we made a concession to the peasant as a trader, to the principal of private trade". Whether NEP was a temporary concession or a new route was left unsettled, and Moshe Lewin's study of Lenin's final months tests that question. Broad agreement holds that NEP was a retreat forced by crisis, that Nepmen reappeared because the state could not supply exchange, and that the worker-peasant alliance was the central political problem. Historians still disagree over whether the last articles reorient strategy and whether the scissors was mainly a pricing failure or a structural imbalance ([Lenin, "On Cooperation"](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1923/jan/06.htm); [Lewin](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3958717W); [Ball](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/russias-last-capitalists/paper)).

## Yugoslavia: decentralisation without private trade

After the Cominform expelled the Yugoslav party in 1948, Tito told the Federal Assembly in June 1950 that state ownership had not delivered "the factories for the workers" and that directors were still appointed from above. The Basic Law passed that day provided for workers' councils elected by the workers' collectives, Yet the state plan remained in force and the appointed director sat on the board, hired workers and represented the enterprise. In 1957 Tito claimed that more than six hundred thousand workers, about a third of those employed, had taken part in management, while also recording that enterprise officials denied councils their rights. Perlman argued that self-management masked bureaucratic power; Hoxha called it capitalist in practice. Neither reading can be tested here, because no council minutes or price series are available ([Tito, 1950](https://www.marxists.org/archive/tito/1950/06/26.htm); [Basic Law](https://www.marxists.org/subject/yugoslavia/self-management/1950/06/x01.htm); [Tito, 1957](https://www.marxists.org/archive/tito/1957/06/25.htm); [Perlman](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/perlman-fredy/1969/revolutionary-movement-yugoslavia.htm); [Hoxha](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hoxha/works/1978/yugoslavia/index.htm)).

## Vietnam and China: crisis as the parent of reform

Vietnam's *doi moi*, meaning renewal, was adopted at the Sixth National Congress in December 1986 in response to crisis. Adam Fforde records market inflation above 700 percent a year, exports worth about US$500 million against imports of US$1,221 million, and areas on the edge of famine. Output contracts let farm households keep a surplus; Contract 10 of April 1988 gave long-term land rights, ended centrally planned targets and allowed sales on the open market. Vietnam's 2013 constitution names the result a "socialist oriented market economy" with multiple forms of ownership in which the state sector leads. Jayne Werner argued that household contracts weakened collective institutions and shifted women's work into the household; Fforde replied that the evidence was thin and the old system less equal than assumed ([Fforde](https://www.bannedthought.net/Vietnam/Academic/DoiMoi/Fforde-DoiMoi-TenYearsAfter1986Congress-1997-OCR-sm.pdf); [Werner](https://doi.org/10.1080/14672715.1984.10409805); [Fforde's reply](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/31724/); [Vietnam's constitution](https://www.constituteproject.org/constitution/Socialist_Republic_of_Vietnam_2013)).

The 1982 constitution accompanied China's reform settlement; amendments in 1988 allowed private enterprise within limits, and in 1993 renamed the goal a "socialist market economy". Article 6 says that in the primary stage of socialism public ownership is the mainstay while diverse forms of ownership develop together; Article 11 calls private businesses "an important component of the socialist market economy"; Article 15 states that "the state shall practice a socialist market economy". The primary stage is the doctrine that China will remain for a long time at an early level of socialist development, needing markets and material growth before a fuller socialism. In 2024 the Third Plenum set 2035 as the date for finishing a high-standard socialist market economy. These are official goals, not evidence that the goal has been reached ([China's constitution](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/lawsregulations/201911/20/content_WS5ed8856ec6d0b3f0e9499913.html); [Third Plenum communique](https://english.www.gov.cn/news/202407/18/content_WS669906cdc6d0868f4e8e93db.html)).

## Cuba and North Korea: repair and survival

Cuba's return to markets followed the Special Period, the emergency after Soviet collapse cut off subsidies, oil and trade. Jorge Perez-Lopez records that the economy contracted by a third or more between 1989 and 1993; Mesa-Lago found that rationing in Havana covered about half of recommended calorie intake. The 2011 *Lineamientos* began an Update of the economic model, and the Reordering Task of January 2021 unified currencies and exchange rates, reformed wages and pensions and reduced subsidies. Raul Castro described the aim as "an appropriate combination of centralized planning and decentralization, with the necessary autonomy at intermediate and basic levels of the enterprise system and local governments". The same report admitted "excessive bureaucracy, inadequate control of resources" and a model that "does not provide sufficient incentives for work or innovation", while ruling out shock therapies. Samuel Farber read such reforms as a turn toward a Sino-Vietnamese model of state capitalism, a claim that goes beyond the documents ([Perez-Lopez](https://ascecubadatabase.org/asce_proceedings/the-cuban-economic-crisis-of-the-1990s-and-the-external-sector); [Mesa-Lago](https://repositorio.cepal.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/3cd9b743-ba48-4d96-b9a9-b92185af21e2/content); [Raul Castro, 2021](https://en.granma.cu/cuba/2021-04-22/central-report-to-the-eighth-congress-of-the-communist-party-of-cuba); [Farber reviewed](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/reviews/capsule-review/2012-01-01/cuba-revolution-1959-critical-assessment)).

The contrast with earlier moral language is sharp: Guevara wrote in 1965 that material incentives should be subordinated to moral ones ([Guevara, 1965](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)). North Korea is a counter-case. Kim Il Sung said in 1982 that the economy was "unaffected by world economic fluctuations"; in the mid-1990s came one of the worst famines of the twentieth century. Haggard and Noland argue that later reforms were reactive moves to control an economy that had partly marketised from below; Hazel Smith argues that marketisation changed the interests of party members, soldiers, women and young people. Both accept that informal markets grew ([Kim Il Sung](https://www.marxists.org/archive/kim-il-sung/2020/20200729120144.pdf); [Haggard and Noland](https://www.piie.com/bookstore/famine-north-korea-markets-aid-and-reform); [Smith](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/north-korea/C2E4A593E3BB7FF436F32F3566168AFF)).

## What the evidence establishes

The claims that survive are narrower than any single theory. Every case revised planning after a visible crisis: war and blockade in 1921, the Yugoslav break with Moscow, near-famine in Vietnam in 1986, Soviet collapse for Cuba, famine for North Korea. Planning was revised rather than abolished. The party kept its leading role, public ownership of major assets remained, and socialist language was retained. Officials justified the change as a primary stage, an incentive problem, a better mix of planning and local decision making, or a defence of living standards. Historians explained the same revisions as responses to crisis, to the state's failure to supply exchange, to household survival and to markets that had grown without permission. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the sources here do not rank them. East Germany is a check: the SED retreated from a work quota in June 1953 only after builders marched, and a 1979 report still found goods ordinary people could not afford ([SED statement, 1953](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/occupation-and-the-emergence-of-two-states-1945-1961/from-a-statement-by-the-politburo-of-the-central-committee-of-the-sed-june-16-1953); [SED report, 1979](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/two-germanies-1961-1989/a-shortage-of-everyday-commodities-may-15-1979)).

## Limits of the record

Nothing here rests on physical archive access. The record is weighted toward official texts, party congresses and English translations; the original Russian, Serbo-Croatian, Vietnamese, Chinese and Spanish documents were not consulted. Yugoslavia has no council minutes or price series in these sources. Duranty's later reporting on Ukraine is heavily disputed, so his NEP observations are used only for Moscow; the scissors figures reach us through a historian's essay. There is no independent reporting from inside the DPRK, no Cuban enterprise records, no household survey behind China's amendments, and no rank-and-file testimony from Vietnamese villages.
