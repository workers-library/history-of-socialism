In June 1918 Nestor Makhno, a Ukrainian anarchist commanding the Revolutionary Insurgent Army of Ukraine, was shown into the Kremlin to speak with two of the men who had taken power in October. He recalled Sverdlov assuming that southern peasants were either kulaks, meaning better-off peasants, or supporters of the Ukrainian Rada. Makhno's own grievance was military. "The Red Guards, regardless of their numbers, carried on the attack against the enemy by moving along the railroads." (Nestor Makhno, recalling his June 1918 conversation with Lenin; written in exile in the 1930s, translated and published by the Kate Sharpley Library. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/makhno-nestor/works/1930/my-visit-to-the-kremlin.html)) He wrote a dozen or more years later, for a movement audience, and the editor of the transcription warns that the dialogue cannot be taken as a literal transcript. The complaint still catches the central problem of these years. The revolution had to be defended by armies, and armies had to eat, and the food was in the villages. War Communism, the set of measures adopted from 1918, is usually described from the government's side as nationalisation, a monopoly in grain and compulsory requisitioning. To the people who underwent it, it was a series of struggles over food and authority in villages, railway stations, prison corridors and famine camps.

## Armies in a broken country

After the treaty of Brest-Litovsk took Russia out of the war in March 1918, German forces occupied large parts of the country and peasants seized land. The Volunteer Army formed at Novocherkassk from officers, cadets and schoolboys. Its commander, General Anton Denikin, remembered it in a memoir written after defeat: "There were deeds of valour and base depravity; heroism and cruelty; compassion and hatred; social tolerance and class antagonism." (General Anton Denikin, *The White Army*, 1930; translated from the Russian by Catherine Zvegintzov. [Source](https://archive.org/download/whitearmy/WhiteArmy_djvu.txt)) His army lived from theft and donations, and he records both the shock of finding mutilated Volunteer prisoners and the fact that his own men shot captured Bolsheviks in revenge. Denikin's political judgement was harsher than his own evidence. "From the very outset the Bolsheviks had summarized the nature of the civil war in a single word: Extermination!" (Denikin, *The White Army*, 1930; translated by Catherine Zvegintzov. [Source](https://archive.org/download/whitearmy/WhiteArmy_djvu.txt)) The memoir that makes that claim also documents White reprisals, which is why the record supports a cycle of violence rather than a single origin.

The White rear was as hungry as the Red. General Pyotr Wrangel, the last commander of the White forces in the south, described regiments hoarding railway trains, troops living off the population, and requisitioning that damaged the very area the army depended on. "The excessive and disorderly requisitioning by the troops had completed the economic disruption" (General Pyotr Wrangel, memoirs written before his death in 1928; translated from the Russian via the French by Sophie Goulston. [Source](https://archive.org/download/memoirsgeneralwrangel/MemoirsGeneralWrangel_djvu.txt)) He tried to bring soldiers before military courts for murder, pillage and illegal requisitioning, but his criticism was administrative, about discipline, not a challenge to the social programme his side defended. His English text comes through a French edition from which, at his own instruction, accounts of events of purely Russian interest were omitted. On the Red side, Makhno's complaint about the railways was a complaint about a war fought from armoured trains while villages defended themselves. Both armies lived from the country they were trying to govern.

## Grain and power

The grain monopoly and *prodrazvyorstka*, the compulsory seizure of surplus grain from peasants, were meant to feed the cities and the army. In practice the burden fell unevenly. Arthur Ransome, a British correspondent in Moscow in early 1919 who moved in government circles, reported the Finance Commissar Krestinsky admitting as much. "It was intended to affect the propertied classes only, and thus to weaken the Kulaks (hard-fists, rich peasants) in the villages" (Finance Commissar Krestinsky, reported by Arthur Ransome, February 1919. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/history/archive/ransome/works/1919-russia/ch12.htm)) Ransome adds that in some districts the Extraordinary Tax was assessed equally on rich and poor peasants, provoking resentment. His conversations were reported from memory, and the officials he quoted had an interest in presenting their policy as rational and temporary. Foreign visitors saw the result in the cities. H. G. Wells, on a visit of a fortnight and a day in 1920 with an official guide, found closed shops, ruined roads, a falling population, hospitals without supplies and soup-kitchen rations. He also noticed the other half of the transaction. "This does not prevent their resisting whenever they can the attempts of the Red Guards to collect food at regulation prices." (H. G. Wells, *Russia in the Shadows*, from his October 1920 visit. [Source](https://archive.org/stream/russiainshadows00well/russiainshadows00well_djvu.txt)) Wells conceded that visitors were not shown the worst, and his broad judgements shape what he noticed, but the resistance he describes is corroborated by other observers.

Ethel Snowden, a member of a British Labour and trades union delegation that spent six weeks in Russia in 1920, recorded bread at four hundred roubles a pound for black bread and one thousand for white bread on the open market against the official ration, and a hunger she saw in provincial streets. She described how peasants spoke of the man who wanted their grain. "Lenin is no more than a name, a devil to the rich peasant, a name with which to conjure out of both rich and poor peasant the stocks of food they are believed to be hiding." (Ethel Snowden, *Through Bolshevik Russia*, 1920. [Source](https://archive.org/stream/throughbolshevik00snowuoft/throughbolshevik00snowuoft_djvu.txt)) She wrote to influence British policy and declared herself opposed to the coup, so her anecdotes need corroboration. Read beside Krestinsky's admission and Wells's observation, hers makes a simple point that the government's own vocabulary of "kulak" tends to hide: the food question was fought over by all sides, and the peasantry was not a passive object of policy.

## Hostages and reprisals

Political violence in these years was institutionalised, not incidental. After the attempt on Lenin's life and the killing of Uritsky in August 1918, the Cheka, the regime's political police, issued decrees ordering named numbers of hostages shot in towns across the country. One such journal recorded: "The Commission has shot forty-one persons from the enemy's camp." (Nizhny Novgorod Cheka journal, autumn 1918, quoted in Melgunov; translated from the Russian. [Source](https://archive.org/stream/redterrorinrussi0000serg/redterrorinrussi0000serg_djvu.txt)) Sergei Melgunov, a historian and Popular Socialist imprisoned in the Butyrka and later sentenced to death, collected these decrees in exile. His book is an indictment written by an opponent, and the chain of quotation, through parliamentary papers, émigré newspapers and unnamed witnesses, varies in reliability. His own testimony is the strongest part. "For I myself was a prisoner in the Butyrka, and had to go through that appalling succession of nightmares." (Sergei Melgunov, writing in exile in 1923–24 about his imprisonment in 1918; English translation from the Russian, translator anonymous. [Source](https://archive.org/stream/redterrorinrussi0000serg/redterrorinrussi0000serg_djvu.txt)) He also repeated a letter from the Reverend B. S. Lombard to Lord Curzon dated 23 March 1919, reproduced in a British parliamentary paper, alleging that "two barge-loads of Russian officers were scuttled in the Gulf of Finland" (quoted in Melgunov. [Source](https://archive.org/stream/redterrorinrussi0000serg/redterrorinrussi0000serg_djvu.txt)). The chain runs from a letter, through a minister and a parliamentary paper, into an exile's book; the incident has not been corroborated here and belongs among the leads, not the established facts.

The regime defended its own logic at the time. Ransome reported that Dzerzhinsky told the Executive Committee in February 1919 that the Cheka's dictatorial powers should end in peacetime but would return if armed counter-revolution resumed. The White side had its own reprisals, as Denikin's admission of shootings and Wrangel's courts show. Melgunov claimed the true number shot greatly exceeded the official figure of five hundred, but neither his count nor the Cheka's partial lists can settle the total. What the hostile accounts share is the fact of reprisal on both sides.

## The famine and the turn

By 1921 the war had taken its toll on the land as well as the cities. Tom Mann, a British trade unionist attending the congress of the Red International of Labour Unions in Moscow, travelled with Kalinin's Commission of Inquiry and reported what he was shown. "famine does actually prevail over a considerable area some eight hundred miles in length and about five hundred in width at widest part" (Tom Mann, *Russia in 1921*. [Source](https://cdm21047.contentdm.oclc.org/digital/api/singleitem/collection/russian/id/2660)) His pamphlet was published by the body whose congress he attended, written as a rebuttal of hostile press reports, and defends the revolution while admitting the hunger. The American Relief Administration entered in 1921 under Herbert Hoover. Its medical director, Henry Beeuwkes, recorded the arrival of his unit and the conditions it found. "The first members of the Russian Unit of the A.R.A. arrived in Russia August 27, 1921." (Henry Beeuwkes, *American Medical and Sanitary Relief in the Russian Famine, 1921–1923*. [Source](https://archive.org/stream/cu31924003454117/cu31924003454117_djvu.txt)) He describes food reserves in the affected areas as practically nil, and institutions dealing with typhus, hunger oedema and undernourished children. The report is an organisation's record of its own operation, and its figures are the organisation's own.

Relief was contested from the start. Willy Münzenberg, organising workers' relief for the Comintern, reported that some committees would feed only children and would aid the Soviet government under no circumstances. "The poor, innocent children were to be saved; the wicked adult Bolsheviks could die of starvation." (Willy Münzenberg, *International Press Correspondence*, 23 December 1921. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/muenzenberg/1921/12/relieftoaid.html)) By the end of 1921 the same organisations were discussing trade, credits and recognition, and Münzenberg presents the change as a victory for his own campaign. The famine had environmental and policy causes, and the sources here cannot allocate responsibility between drought, war damage, requisitioning and blockade. What the witnesses establish is that food became a weapon and then a field of argument. By spring 1921 crop failure, the end of the civil war and peasant resistance had forced a retreat. The Tenth Congress adopted a tax in kind in place of compulsory requisitioning, the first step of the New Economic Policy. That turn, and the uprisings at Kronstadt and Tambov that pressed it, belong to the next chapter.

## Limits of the record

The first-hand record is uneven and weighted toward the articulate. Generals, party leaders, foreign correspondents and relief officials are well represented; peasant women, rank-and-file Red soldiers, non-Russian nationalities and the people named in Cheka lists are not. Where ordinary people appear, it is usually as reported speech inside someone else's memoir. No Russian-language archive was visited and no Russian-language source was opened for this account. All Russian testimony reaches English through translation, and Wrangel's English comes through French. The reliability of the contested material varies sharply: Denikin wrote to vindicate a losing cause, Melgunov to indict a winning one, Makhno to explain a defeated revolution, and the relief sources to justify their own operations.

Several gaps matter. The number shot in the Red and White terror cannot be reconciled from these sources. The story of officers drowned in the Gulf of Finland rests on a letter to a British minister reproduced in a parliamentary paper and repeated in an exile's book; it is a lead, not established fact. Deaths in the 1921 famine are disputed in the scholarly literature, and none of these witnesses offers a method for settling the figure. The American relief photographs were not in the accessible edition, and the slides and photograph albums in the Warwick collection returned access errors, so no visual claim is made here. Kronstadt and Tambov are deliberately out of scope. The accounts should not be mined for invented scene-setting: Wells's tram accident, Denikin's station platform and Melgunov's "Chamber of Souls" are what those witnesses reported, not material for a reconstructed past. What the record gives us is the leadership of the war, its foreign observers and the machinery of relief. It gives us far less of the people who bore it.

## How historians have read it

- Orlando Figes, *A People's Tragedy: The Russian Revolution 1891–1924* (1996). [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL72141W): a narrative social history built from letters, diaries and memoirs; it treats the civil war as a national catastrophe rather than a simple contest of armies.
- Evan Mawdsley, *The Russian Civil War* (1987; later editions 2008). [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL2281519W): a military and political account that stresses Red organisational superiority and White disunity; broadly compatible with Kenez on the Whites' weaknesses.
- Peter Kenez, *Civil War in South Russia, 1918–1920: The Defeat of the Whites* (1977), following *Civil War in South Russia, 1918* (1971). [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL1917984W): argues that the Whites lost because they were politically and socially narrow and could not win the peasantry; agrees with Mawdsley on outcomes but puts more weight on politics than on operations.
- Robert V. Daniels, *The Conscience of the Revolution: Communist Opposition in Soviet Russia* (1960). [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL2716694W): a study of internal party opposition using party documents; useful because it shows War Communism was contested inside the Bolshevik party, not simply imposed and then abandoned.
- Andrea Graziosi, *The Great Soviet Peasant War: Bolsheviks and Peasants, 1917–1933* (1996). [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL500475W): frames peasant resistance as a war against the state and treats requisitioning as central; agrees with Lewin on the weight of the peasant question but takes a more confrontational view of the countryside.
- Moshe Lewin, *Russian Peasants and Soviet Power* (1968; English edition 1975). [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3958718W): argues from economic and social history that the crisis of War Communism forced the retreat to NEP; closer than Graziosi to seeing peasant pressure as a structural constraint rather than a declared war.

Broad agreement: the peasantry resisted requisitioning; the White movements were politically weak; War Communism was improvised and coercive; the famine had both environmental and policy causes. Live disagreement: how far drought rather than requisitioning explains 1921; how intentional the Red Terror was; how much the Whites could have done to win peasants; whether "peasant war" is the right description of rural resistance. These positions are not equally supported on every point: the archival studies of Kenez, Daniels and Lewin rest on party and state records, while broad narrative claims about motives rely more heavily on the memoir literature just cited.
