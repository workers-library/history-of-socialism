Georgios Zacharias was eight years old when militiamen came to his family's home in Odessa. He remembered the hour for the rest of his life. "21 декабря 1937 года около 2.30 - 3 часа ночи к нам домой пришли милиционеры и провели обыск." (Georgios Zacharias, memoir written in 2007; Russian text at Greek Martirolog, [greek-martirolog.com](https://www.greek-martirolog.com/book_witness/book_w_0.php)), that is, "Around half past two or three in the morning on 21 December 1937, militiamen came to our home and searched it." His father Dionysios was sentenced in January and shot in Odessa on 2 April 1938. The search was part of an operation named for a nationality. This chapter follows the documents that named the nationalities, and the people who lived under them.

## A system of categories

The Soviet state had spent the 1920s promoting native languages and local officials in its republics. By 1937 the same identities were being read as evidence of disloyalty. The machinery belonged to the NKVD, the Soviet secret police, and it worked through secret operational orders. The first of the national operations in time was Order No. 00439 of 25 July 1937, which named German citizens at military plants, defence workshops and railways. "Начиная с 29 июля с. г. приступить к арестам всех установленных вами германских подданных" (NKVD Order No. 00439, 25 July 1937, Russian text at [Russian Wikisource](https://ru.wikisource.org/wiki/%D0%9F%D1%80%D0%B8%D0%BA%D0%B0%D0%B7_%D0%9D%D0%9A%D0%92%D0%94_%D0%BE%D1%82_25.07.1937_%E2%84%96_00439)), or "From 29 July, begin arresting all German citizens identified by you." Lists were to be compiled in three days and arrests completed in five. The order targeted citizenship in the first instance; its extension to Soviet citizens of German descent was a later step, documented in the scholarship rather than in this order alone.

Five days later came the broader order that historians call the kulak operation, though its reach went well beyond peasants deemed wealthy. NKVD Operational Order No. 00447 of 30 July 1937 assigned numerical quotas to each region and republic and divided victims into two categories. "a) The first category are the most hostile of the enumerated elements. They are subject to immediate arrest, and after their cases have been considered by a three-person tribunal [troika] they are TO BE SHOT." (NKVD Operational Order No. 00447, 30 July 1937; English translation by Evan Mawdsley, reproduced by Seventeen Moments in Soviet History, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/great-terror/great-terror-texts/mass-repressions/)). A troika was a three-person panel empowered to sentence without a normal trial. The quotas included 500 in the first category and 1,000 in the second for the Armenian republic, and 1,500 and 3,750 for Azerbaijan, which shows that the campaign was administered through the national republics. The target groups also included what the order called non-Russian nationalists.

The Polish operation followed on 11 August. "(b) all prisoners of war from the Polish Army remaining in the USSR; (c) refugees from Poland regardless of the time of their arrival in the USSR" (NKVD Order No. 00485, 11 August 1937; English translation published by the Institute of National Remembrance / Mieroszewski Centre, [operacja-polska.pl](https://operacja-polska.pl/nke/about-polish-operation/document/857,11th-August-1937-Moscow-Operational-order-No-00485-of-the-Peoples-Commissar-for-.html)). The order set a start date of 20 August, a three-month timetable and two categories, those to be shot and those to be imprisoned for no less than five to eight years. Lists were approved in batches and results telegraphed every five days. Its claim that a Polish military network existed was the state's allegation, not an established fact. A fourth order, No. 00486 of 15 August, reached into the household. Wives of men labelled spies or enemies of the people were sent for five to eight years to the Temnikov camp for failing to report their husbands, and children were often placed in orphanages or labour camps.

Lev Tsel'merovskii, a nineteen-year-old factory worker and Komsomol member from Petrozavodsk, wrote to the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet in September 1938 from exile in Chimkent. His father had been arrested under Article 58, the clause of the Soviet criminal code used for political offences. "my father (he was arrested and I am exiled as a family member without trial) (I wasn't even interrogated)." (Lev Tsel'merovskii, letter of 13 September 1938; translated by Siegelbaum and Sokolov, *Stalinism as a Way of Life*, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/great-terror/great-terror-texts/autobiography-of-an-exile/)). He stressed his loyalty, his shooting skills and his irreproachable sisters, because he wanted a passport, military service and a skilled career back. His letter shows what a sentence passed on a relative meant in one life.

## The names on the lists

The orders survive as administrative texts. The survivors describe what a name on a list meant. Cleopatra Maroufidou, a Soviet Greek, visited her father in a camp in early 1937 and learned that he had been shot as a spy in December. She was arrested in Murmansk a day after the directive against the Greeks, held for ten months in a women's prison in Leningrad without trial, and later sentenced to three years that the war turned into five in Kazakhstan. "I was lucky, because if they had sent me to the gulag, I would probably have died, as did so many others." (Cleopatra Maroufidou, interview published 3 July 2006, [ekathimerini.com](https://www.ekathimerini.com/news/41545/they-accused-my-father-of-spying-and-executed-him/)). Recorded nearly seventy years later in a hostile interviewer's framing, her account is a late-life judgement as well as a memory. In Batum, in Georgia, fifteen-year-old Valia Mouratidou watched her father taken from the foundry on 19 December 1937. "Two hours later, an uncle of mine came and told me that they had taken him from the foundry." (Valia Mouratidou, recalling 19 December 1937, interview published 3 July 2006, [ekathimerini.com](https://www.ekathimerini.com/news/41543/fabricated-charges/)). She described other Greek prisoners signing confessions under torture while her father refused and was exiled anyway. His death reached the family as hearsay in 1947.

The charges could be absurd. Reporting in 2024 quoted directive 50215 of 11 December 1937, which ordered the arrest of Greeks on 15 December, and reproduced examples: a worker accused of copying a defence line, and a man recorded under a name written as "Ksi-Di-AS". In Armenia, the schoolteacher Maro Alazan was arrested in Yerevan in November 1937. "'You are arrested citizen Muradyan,' said Chekist Abulyan when I entered one of the rooms of the Ministry." (Maro Alazan, memoir written in the 1970s; English translation published by EVN Report, translator not named, [evnreport.com](https://evnreport.com/arts-and-culture/from-the-forgotten-pages-of-history-the-tragedy-of-maro-alazan/)). She described the strip search, interrogation about her husband, nine months in a cell with other wives of "enemies", messages scratched on the walls and a beating by a guard. She called the years 1936 to 1938 a second genocide visited upon her people. That is her own political judgement, not a historian's classification.

At the other end of the process were the people who dug the ditches and fired the shots. A shepherd from Tsalka, Talib Aliyev, told investigators decades later that he had watched trucks bring handcuffed prisoners to pits near Tbilisi in 1937 and 1938. The Institute for Development of Freedom of Information cross-checked his account against 1952 aerial photographs and archival files. Among the statements it reproduces from NKVD personnel is this: "Расстреливали обычно, по 20, 40 человек, а иногда и 100." (testimony of an NKVD participant in the 1937–38 executions, quoted by IDFI in Russian, [idfi.ge](https://idfi.ge/en/news-8)), that is, "They usually shot twenty or forty people at a time, sometimes a hundred." Those statements come from the 1950s, when the speakers had strong reasons to shift blame, and the court that heard them was the perpetrators' own institution.

## Deportation and its afterlife

The Korean operation worked differently. Rather than shooting selected individuals, the state removed a whole population from the Russian Far East to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan in 1937. A Kazakhstani Korean woman, seven years old at the time, remembered the journey through her grandmother: "During transportation, many died along the way because it was very cold, and they couldn't use the toilet. The dead were thrown out of the wagons along the way." (as recalled by her granddaughter in an interview conducted in 2021, [peripheralhistories.co.uk](https://www.peripheralhistories.co.uk/post/memories-of-deportation-from-the-russian-far-east-to-central-asia)). The journey is a memory of a memory, recorded eighty-four years after the event. Maria Medvedskaya recalled the deportation of 1949, which continued the same logic into the postwar years: "В вагоне мама резала свои платья на пелёнки – месяц же добирались, в вагоне ничего не постираешь, не высушишь." (Maria Medvedskaya, recalling the 1949 deportation, Kavkaz.Realii, 15 December 2024, [svoboda.org](https://www.svoboda.org/a/33238631.html)), or "In the wagon my mother cut up her dresses for nappies — we were a month on the way, and you couldn't wash or dry anything in the wagon." One respondent in the same oral-history work said her grandmother's Korean name was suppressed until her death in 2013.

Georg Hildebrandt, a Soviet German in Ukraine, described an earlier overlap between collectivisation and nationality repression: "Very early one morning in March 1930, militia and secret agents of the state police occupied our entire town. All men and boys from 16 years of age were arrested and jailed. I was among them." (Georg Hildebrandt, author's note on the publisher's page for *Why are you still alive?*, [ndsu.edu](https://grhc-northdakotastate-ndus.nbsstore.net/why-are-you-still-alive)). The memoir itself was not read; this is the author's own note.

The numbers depend on who is counting. The Institute of National Remembrance gives at least 139,835 repressed and 111,091 murdered in the Polish operation; Andrej Kotliartchouk's 2025 review of the NKVD accounting gives 139,815 sentenced and 111,071 executed ([IPN](https://eng.ipn.gov.pl/en/news/977,What-was-the-Polish-Operation-by-the-NKVD.html); [Kotliartchouk, 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/understanding-stalins-terror-against-western-minorities-the-national-operations-of-the-nkvd-in-contemporary-academic-research/C857CFCE5920997FA2BFF04444B18760)). Across all the national operations the same accounting reports 335,513 sentenced and 247,157 executed between July 1937 and November 1938, the aggregate Nicolas Werth also uses; the figures are compatible, not rival body counts ([Kotliartchouk, 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C857CFCE5920997FA2BFF04444B18760/S0018246X24000487a.pdf/understanding-stalins-terror-against-western-minorities_the-national-operations-of-the-nkvd-in-contemporary-academic-research.pdf); [Werth, 2024](https://legrandcontinent.eu/fr/2024/04/07/les-operations-nationales-de-la-grande-terreur-de-1937-1938)). For the Koreans, Helsinki Watch cited an estimate of 40,000 dead in the first year from editors of the newspaper *Koryo Ilbo*; other scholarship says there is no reliable figure. The deaths are not established by the sources gathered here.

## How historians have read it

- **Andrej Kotliartchouk, "Understanding Stalin's Terror against Western Minorities: The National Operations of the NKVD in Contemporary Academic Research", *The Historical Journal* 68(1), 2025.** A review that sets out the archival basis for the scale of the operations and maps the disagreement between ethnic-targeting and security-dilemma explanations ([Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/understanding-stalins-terror-against-western-minorities-the-national-operations-of-the-nkvd-in-contemporary-academic-research/C857CFCE5920997FA2BFF04444B18760)).
- **Terry Martin, "The Origins of Soviet Ethnic Cleansing", *Journal of Modern History* 70(4), 1998.** Traces population removal back through earlier nationality policy and the borderlands of the 1920s and 1930s, treating 1937 as an escalation rather than an isolated turn ([DOI](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/3229636)).
- **Nicolas Werth, "The NKVD Mass Secret Operation n°00447 (August 1937 – November 1938)", *Mass Violence and Resistance*, Sciences Po, 2010.** Uses the discovered orders to argue that the Great Terror had a hidden mass-repression half directed at ordinary people, with detail on secrecy, quotas and confessions ([Sciences Po](https://web.archive.org/web/20250222082747/https://www.sciencespo.fr/mass-violence-war-massacre-resistance/en/document/nkvd-mass-secret-operation-n-00447-august-1937-november-1938)).
- **Jon K. Chang, *Burnt by the Sun: The Koreans of the Russian Far East*, University of Hawai'i Press, 2016.** Based on interviews with elderly Koreans and archives; argues that tsarist and Russian nationalist assumptions shaped the decision to deport a population treated as racially unreliable ([University of Hawai'i Press](https://uhpress.hawaii.edu/title/burnt-by-the-sun-the-koreans-of-the-russian-far-east)).
- **Levan Avalishvili, "The 'Great Terror' of 1937–1938 in Georgia" and Eduard Melkonian, "Repressions in 1930s Soviet Armenia", *Caucasus Analytical Digest* no. 22, 2010.** Regional studies of how central directives ran through the Georgian and Armenian party apparatuses ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3929/ethz-a-006256278)).
- **Golfo Alexopoulos, "The Collective Punishment of Kin under Stalin", Estonian Institute of Historical Memory, 2023.** Explains the family as a unit of repression and the significance of Order 00486 ([Estonian Institute of Historical Memory](https://communistcrimes.org/en/collective-punishment-kin-under-stalin)).

There is broad agreement on the existence, timing and approximate scale of the operations, on the involvement of Stalin and Yezhov, and on the use of quotas and extrajudicial bodies. The live arguments concern motive, whether ethnicity or state security on the borders explains the targeting, the weight of local initiative, and whether the operations should be termed genocide or a form of ethnic cleansing. The orders support the first two readings in part, because they name nationality through alleged foreign intelligence work. They do not settle the third.

## Limits of the record

The people who were shot left no testimony. What survives comes from survivors, children, perpetrators speaking after the fact and officials recording their own actions, and nearly all of it was written or recorded decades later. Memoirs are shaped by the need to explain a life, and the compiler of the Greek collection warns that descendants' stories accumulate legend. The surviving voices are uneven by nationality and by class. Poles and Greeks have substantial published testimony; Koreans have a documentary film, a monograph based on interviews and one small oral-history article; for Armenians and Georgians the accessible first-hand material is largely from intellectuals and their families, or from perpetrators at the 1955 trial. Ordinary villagers, railway workers and the many non-Poles swept into an operation named for Poles are barely represented. There is almost nothing from uninvolved neighbours or from families who stayed silent. The orders tell us what the NKVD intended and counted, not how many people were arrested twice, how many died under torture, or how a particular local troika chose between the two categories. The machinery is documented in unusual detail; the inner life of most victims is recoverable only in fragments.
