In an essay written in October 1978 the playwright Václav Havel constructed an example: a greengrocer in Czechoslovakia placed a printed notice in his shop window, the revolutionary slogan shopkeepers were expected to display. Havel's greengrocer did not put it there out of conviction. He put it there because the authorities expected it, and because the alternative was to lose his shop, or worse. Havel made that ordinary act the key to understanding power in a state that called itself socialist. The regime did not rule chiefly by terror, he argued. It ruled by making people repeat its formulas, and each repetition rebuilt the thing it obeyed: "For by this very fact, individuals confirm the system, fulfill the system, make the system, are the system." Havel called the greengrocer's bargain "living within the lie" and named its opposite "living within the truth". He described Czechoslovakia as "post-totalitarian", meaning that power worked mainly through ritual and ideology rather than through open violence. Of a man who breaks the ritual he wrote: "He has said that the emperor is naked." ([Václav Havel, "The Power of the Powerless," October 1978](http://history.hanover.edu/courses/excerpts/165havel.html))

Havel's essay did not appear in an ordinary journal. It was typed and copied in *samizdat*, the name for the underground practice of reproducing uncensored writing on typewriters and carbon paper and passing it from reader to reader. To publish in samizdat was to break the state's monopoly on the printed word; to be caught with a duplicated text could mean prison. The writing that survived this way came from famous dissidents and from people whose names never travelled beyond their own city.

## A text in print, and the silence around it

In November 1962 the Soviet literary journal *Novy Mir* published Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn's novella *One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich*. It was fiction drawn from the author's eight years in the camps, but it described a prisoner's working day with a detail official writing had refused to supply. An old prisoner in the story tells the others, "It's the law of the taiga here, men. But a man can live here, just like anywhere else." The narrator counts the moments when a convict belongs to himself: "Apart from sleep, an old lag can call his life his own only for ten minutes at breakfast time, five at lunchtime, and five more at suppertime." ([old prisoner Kuzyomin and the narrator, quoted in Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, *One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich*, 1962](https://www.davar.net/EXTRACTS/FICTION/ONE-DAY.HTM)) The novella was a breach in the official silence about the camps. Readers' letters to the journal, studied later by Miriam Dobson, show that the response was more divided than a simple quarrel between liberals and conservatives. Solzhenitsyn was expelled from the Writers' Union, and his later books circulated in typescript. In his 1970 Nobel lecture, delivered only to the Swedish Academy, he put the matter in two sentences: "Woe to that nation whose literature is disturbed by the intervention of power." And, "ONE WORD OF TRUTH SHALL OUTWEIGH THE WHOLE WORLD." ([Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, Nobel literature lecture, 1970](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1970/solzhenitsyn/lecture/))

## Copies passed from hand to hand

The alternative press carried news that the official papers suppressed. *A Chronicle of Current Events*, an illegal human-rights periodical, summarised what was circulating in March 1975 in its thirty-fifth issue. It described Bukovsky and Gluzman's "A Manual on Psychiatry for Dissenters", written from prison and a labour camp, listed the contents of underground journals, and recorded searches and KGB interrogations. On the manual's account of prisoners who publicly recanted, the *Chronicle* noted that "it is precisely this fear and helplessness that the authors consider to be the reason behind recent unexpected 'repentances' and 'repudiations'". ([*A Chronicle of Current Events*, no. 35, section 35.12, 31 May 1975, Peter Reddaway translation](https://chronicle-of-current-events.com/2024/04/06/samizdat-update-march-1975-35-12/)) The same issue preserved a real disagreement among exiles. "Sakharov considers that Marxist ideology is merely a convenient facade for the rulers. But in Solzhenitsyn's opinion it is 'the evil-smelling root of present-day Soviet life'". ([*A Chronicle of Current Events*, no. 35, section 35.12, 31 May 1975](https://chronicle-of-current-events.com/2024/04/06/samizdat-update-march-1975-35-12/)) That argument should be left open: it was a dispute between participants, not a problem the record settles. The Polish poet Czesław Miłosz, a former cultural official who broke with the Polish regime, described in his 1980 Nobel lecture what censorship did to language: "that whoever wields power is also able to control language and not only with the prohibitions of censorship, but also by changing the meaning of words." And: "In a room where people unanimously maintain a conspiracy of silence, one word of truth sounds like a pistol shot." ([Czesław Miłosz, Nobel literature lecture, 8 December 1980](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/1980/milosz/lecture/)) Writing published abroad and smuggled back, known as *tamizdat*, gave such texts a second route to readers inside the country.

## A petition answered with criminal charges

Charter 77 measured Czechoslovakia against agreements the state had itself signed, including the 1966 United Nations covenants and the 1975 Helsinki Final Act. Its founding declaration, dated 1 January 1977 and published abroad a week later, listed dismissals for unorthodox views, barriers to the education of dissidents' children, police surveillance, church restrictions and the trial of young musicians. It called itself "CHARTA 77 je volné, neformální a otevřené společenství lidí různých přesvědčení, různé víry a různých profesí", or in a literal English rendering, "Charter 77 is a free, informal and open community of people of different convictions, different faiths and different professions". It insisted: "CHARTA 77 není základnou k opoziční politické činnosti.", that is, "Charter 77 is not a base for opposition political activity". ([Charter 77 founding declaration, 1 January 1977](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/en:cs:Prohl%C3%A1%C5%A1en%C3%AD_Charty_77)) That careful legal language was a strategy to stay inside Czechoslovak law, not a neutral description of what the group did. The authorities treated the petition as a crime. According to the records described in a published finding aid, the State Security opened proceedings on 6 January 1977 for "subversion", called the Charter a "crude attack" by "hostile elements", and on 14 January a prosecution opinion added "sedition" and described it as "untrue and grossly slanderous… clearly pursuing the aim of evoking hatred and hostility towards, or at least distrust of, the socialist social and state system of the republic". ([Czechoslovak prosecutors' legal opinion, 14 January 1977, quoted in the National Security Archive finding aid, translated by Derek Paton](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB213/index.htm)) A report of about 1 April recorded that 251 people had been interrogated and that the party leadership had decided not to charge anyone simply for signing. These are the persecutors' documents, so their labels are claims rather than findings.

## Sakharov's list

Andrei Sakharov, a Soviet physicist and human-rights campaigner, was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1975. He could not travel to Oslo, so Elena Bonner read his lecture there on 11 December. Sakharov joined censorship, prison and the right to leave to international politics, arguing that détente was worthless without freedom of information, conscience, publication and movement. He described restrictions on Jewish and German emigration, the ban on religious literature and the "special psychiatric clinics" used to confine people who dissented, and he named prisoners he knew. He credited the *Chronicle* by name. His conclusion was deliberately modest: "We need reform, not revolution." And: "Without losing sight of an overall solution of this kind, we must today fight for every individual person separately against injustice and the violation of human rights." ([Andrei Sakharov, Nobel Peace Prize lecture, 11 December 1975](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/1975/sakharov/lecture/)) The claim that civic rights come first was an argument, not a settled finding, and it conflicted with the Marxist view he explicitly rejected.

## The long wait for a visa

Two Leningrad interviews move the story away from the famous names. Aba Taratuta, a Jewish activist, gave up secure employment to apply for permission to leave for Israel. He recalled that the harsh sentences after the failed Leningrad airplane trial of 1970 settled the matter: "The severe sentences meted out to the participants for the unsuccessful airplane hijacking finally confirmed our intention to leave for Israel." ([Aba Taratuta, oral history recorded 2004, translated excerpts, Ann Komaromi](https://samizdat.library.utoronto.ca/interviews/en/aba-taratuta)) He described a whole community built around underground Hebrew classes, with a rule for the pupils: "Once you have mastered three lessons from the popular textbook *Elef milim*, pass on your knowledge to others." ([Aba Taratuta, same interview](https://samizdat.library.utoronto.ca/interviews/en/aba-taratuta)) A 1980 search seized typewriters, a 1902 Pentateuch and audio cassettes of Hebrew and English lessons. Evgeny Lein, a mathematician, applied with his wife Irina in 1978 after the 1967 war and the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia. "Irina and I were fired from work the day that we submitted our documents for emigration to Israel." ([Evgeny Lein, oral history recorded 2004, translated by Nika Gofshtein](https://samizdat.library.utoronto.ca/interviews/en/evgeny-lein)) Their apartment held seminars of forty to a hundred people. He was arrested in 1981 on a charge he said was invented, used the trial "as a tribune for protest", and served two years in prison and exile. In the late 1980s the pressure turned to their son: "They told us openly: 'We will call up your son for army service, and you will not leave without your son.'" ([Evgeny Lein, same interview](https://samizdat.library.utoronto.ca/interviews/en/evgeny-lein)) Both men credited letters and visitors from abroad with practical and moral support. These were people refused the right to emigrate, known as refuseniks, and the refusenik condition could last for years.

## What the record cannot establish

The surviving testimony is eloquent but narrow. It is heavily urban and drawn from an educated minority: playwrights, physicists, mathematicians, editors. Peasants, workers who did not sign, and people who chose accommodation appear mainly as Havel's hypothetical greengrocer or as unnamed categories in the Charter. Nothing here establishes how many people read samizdat, how often a text changed as it was copied, or what non-activist citizens thought of the dissidents. The two Leningrad accounts were recorded in 2004, translated and revised by the men who gave them; they show what these two people later said about their lives, not the precise wording of 1970s conversations. The novella is fictionalised and the lecture texts are rhetorical. Official records tell us what prosecutors believed and wrote, not what happened in an interrogation room. Mitrokhin's copied KGB files survive at Cambridge, but the holding is made up of redacted and edited typescripts, parts are closed, and it is strongest on foreign operations rather than on everyday dissent inside the country. Records from the Orthodox Church are missing, and a body of material referred to as "the Metropolitan" has not been identified among the surviving sources, so nothing is claimed for it. Precise page and file citations would require the Russian and Czech originals, the *Chronicle*'s Russian run and the Czech Security Services Archive in an authenticated archive.

## How historians have read it

- **H. Gordon Skilling, *Charter 77 and Human Rights in Czechoslovakia* (1981).** A political scientist's detailed study of the Charter as an initiative working through the Helsinki process; it takes the movement's legal strategy seriously. [Internet Archive: charter77humanri0000skil](https://archive.org/details/charter77humanri0000skil)
- **John Keane (ed.), *The Power of the Powerless: Citizens Against the State in Central-Eastern Europe* (1985).** Collects Havel's essay with nine other Czech and Slovak dissident texts; its framing made "living in truth" the standard English-language account of Czechoslovak opposition. [Internet Archive: powerofpowerless0000unse](https://archive.org/details/powerofpowerless0000unse)
- **Milan Hanyš, "Beyond 'The Power of the Powerless': the Political Thought and Polemics of the Czechoslovak Opposition, 1977–1980", *East Central Europe* 50(2–3), 2023, 279–303.** Argues that Havel's essay was contested among the opposition rather than simply accepted, a corrective to Keane's reception. [Source: brill.com](https://brill.com/view/journals/eceu/50/2-3/article-p279_007.xml)
- **Miriam Dobson, "Contesting the Paradigms of De-Stalinization: Readers' Responses to *One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich*", *Slavic Review* 64(3), 2005, 580–600.** Uses unpublished readers' letters to *Novyi mir* to show that responses to the 1962 publication were more divided than a liberal-versus-conservative model allows. [Source: api.crossref.org](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.2307/3650143)
- **Yasha Klots, *Tamizdat: Contraband Russian Literature in the Cold War Era* (2023).** Studies Russian literature published abroad and smuggled back, explaining how a censored text reached readers. [Source: api.crossref.org](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1017/slr.2024.428)
- **Vasiliy Mitrokhin and Christopher Andrew, *The Mitrokhin Archive* (1999).** The published version of the KGB notes catalogued above; strong on KGB operations, but its reliability has been disputed, so it must be set against dissident testimony. [Source: archivesearch.lib.cam.ac.uk](https://archivesearch.lib.cam.ac.uk/repositories/9/resources/1716)

There is broad agreement that the Charter, samizdat and the Helsinki process were central to how opposition survived in Czechoslovakia, that the 1962 publication was a genuine break in Soviet literary silence, and that Soviet Jewish emigration activists built durable underground institutions. There is live disagreement over how representative the dissidents were, whether ideology was a sincere belief or a facade, and how much credit is due to Western pressure.
