## The record

On the night of 30–31 August 1935 Aleksei Stakhanov, a hewer at a coal mine in the Donbass, cut 102 tons of coal in a single shift. A hewer is a miner who cuts coal at the working face. The *Pravda* correspondent who reported it wrote: "During a six-hour shift, Stakhanov gave 102 tons of coal, which constitutes 10% of the mine's daily production, and earned 200 rubles." ([*Pravda* report, 1 September 1935, reproduced in Stakhanov's memoir](https://www.directdemocracy4u.uk/dd4en-resources/socialist-construction/The_Story_of_My_Life_A_Stakhanov_1947_E_Book_en.pdf)). The figure became the name of a movement. Stakhanov's own account, published in 1937, complicates the story of the lone hero. He checked his jackhammer, a pneumatic drill, and arranged for two timberers, Shchigolev and Kalinin, to work behind him, with horse-drivers and a repair shift moved to serve his face. He told his fellow miners: "There is no secret in the fact that I cut 102 tons of coal. This can be done by every experienced hewer who has mastered the technique of his business, if his labor is divided and he is given the opportunity to cut with a jackhammer the entire shift..." ([Stakhanov, *The Story of My Life*, 1937](https://www.directdemocracy4u.uk/dd4en-resources/socialist-construction/The_Story_of_My_Life_A_Stakhanov_1947_E_Book_en.pdf)). Sergo Ordzhonikidze, a member of the leadership, said the same at the Stakhanovite congress in November 1935: "It is sometimes thought that a single man [Stakhanov – N.M.] produced 102 tons. This is not true. These 102 tons were produced by an entire brigade." ([Ordzhonikidze, quoted by N. Markin, 1936](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/ni/vol03/no01/markin.htm)). Stakhanov's memoir is a partisan official self-presentation, and this English text is a machine translation made in 2026; its wording needs checking against the Russian. Even so, the record was collective work.

The campaign used the exceptional result to reset expectations. Stalin told about three thousand Stakhanovites in the Kremlin on 17 November 1935 that the movement was "profoundly revolutionary" and had arisen "almost spontaneously... from below, without any pressure whatsoever from the administrators of our enterprises," while ordering party bodies to curb managers and engineers who resisted ([Stalin's speech to Stakhanovites, 17 November 1935](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/year-of-the-stakhanovite/year-of-the-stakhanovite-texts/stalin-at-the-conference-of-stakhanovites/)). He admitted that 102 tons could not become the general norm. He tied the campaign to consumption: "If there is a shortage of bread, a shortage of butter and fats, a shortage of textiles, and if housing conditions are bad, freedom will not carry you very far." ([same](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/year-of-the-stakhanovite/year-of-the-stakhanovite-texts/stalin-at-the-conference-of-stakhanovites/)). And he described the mood he wanted: "Life has improved, comrades. Life has become more joyous. And when life is joyous, work goes well." ([same](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/year-of-the-stakhanovite/year-of-the-stakhanovite-texts/stalin-at-the-conference-of-stakhanovites/)). The rewards were real for the few: 200 rubles for one shift, a new apartment with a telephone and gramophone, orders and promotions in Stakhanov's telling. A Stakhanovite was a worker whose record was held up as an example. A contemporary opponent, N. Markin, collected hostile press reports of resentment, assaults and trials of workers who resisted; he was not in the country and selected his items, so these are a digest, not court records ([Markin, "The Stakhanovist Movement," 1936](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/ni/vol03/no01/markin.htm)). Both accounts are partial. Stakhanov had genuine skill and real support from mine officials; the conditions he describes were available to very few.

## Nine girls on the steppe

A different picture comes from the land. Sergei Tretiakov, a writer who lived for two years in the countryside, reported in 1935 on a women's tractor brigade in the Don steppe led by Pasha Angelina. A kolkhoz was a collective farm in which land and the main implements were owned jointly and members worked under an elected chairman, usually keeping a small private plot. The male trainees scorned the women; there were no sleeping huts and no fuel, and the brigade shared a four-room house. Tretiakov wrote: "The first seven days, when they weren't given anywhere to sleep, they worked without leaving their tractors." ([Tretiakov, "Nine Girls," 1935, translated by James von Geldern](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/year-of-the-stakhanovite/year-of-the-stakhanovite-texts/nine-girls/)). He describes twenty-hour shifts to honour a promise of 1,200 hectares per tractor, frozen fingers and a gramophone in the field cabin. His reportage was written inside the campaign and shapes the women's speech; its praise is his. What it records is that the brigade won authority by work.

The rules of the kolkhoz were fixed in a model statute of 17 February 1935. It set land in perpetuity to the artel, the farm, and allowed private plots of a quarter to a half hectare, up to a hectare in some districts, with privately owned cows, pigs, sheep and hives. Wages were paid in "working days," units entered in a labour book monthly; piecework norms set the rate. There was a month off before and after childbirth at half pay. Penalties ran from a public blackboard through fines of up to five working days to dismissal; pilfering was called "a betrayal of the kolkhoz cause" ([Kolkhoz Statute, 17 February 1935](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/second-kolkhoz-charter/second-kolkhoz-charter-texts/kolkhoz-statute/)). The statute is exemplary, not proof that any one farm followed it. At the congress that produced it, delegates argued over plot sizes, proposing 0.12, 0.25 and 0.45 hectares. Stalin's reported intervention conceded the peasant case: "The kolkhozniks want to live a decent life, and for that this 0.12 hectares is not enough. We need to allocate a quarter to half a hectare, and even as much as one hectare in some districts." ([Debate reported in *Krest'ianskaia pravda*, 27 February 1935, reproduced by Fitzpatrick](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/second-kolkhoz-charter/second-kolkhoz-charter-texts/private-garden-plots)). This is a documented instance of the leadership yielding to ordinary demands, inside an institution that could fine and dismiss a member. It reaches us through Fitzpatrick's translation, not the original newspaper.

## Shoes, matches and bread

Scarcity was not confined to the famine years and was not only rural. V. A. Sychov, a prize-winning senior herdsman at a state farm, or sovkhoz, near Armavir, wrote to a trade-union official on 13 September 1935. His brigade went barefoot: "In regards to footwear our situation is bad. The brigade is all barefoot because we have noware to buy... We have nothing not even matches." ([Sychov letter, 13 September 1935](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/second-kolkhoz-charter/second-kolkhoz-charter-texts/life-of-a-herdsman/)). He had three years of village school; his rough spelling is preserved. He reported that the milk plan fell to 55 to 60 per cent in the second half of the year because there was no water and the herd had to be driven seven kilometres. P. I. Voronov wrote to the newspaper on behalf of a village meeting of twenty-five people in 1936, listing what equal rights with city workers would mean: "That there be an uninterrupted supply of goods for sale in our villages, especially flour, because we get flour very seldom here, and for bread kolkhoz farmers have to travel thirty kilometers to get baked bread to feed their family..." ([Voronov letter, 1936](http://yupnet.org/annals/siegelbaum/English_docs/Siegelbaum_doc_57.htm)). He asked for trade-union booklets for kolkhozniks and for work-days to be entered every ten days, and complained that the chairman, brigade leader and clerk did no work. A petition stresses what it wants; that it calls itself the view of a meeting of twenty-five may be a device. These are the complaints of plan-fulfilling workers and village spokesmen, not of "backward" peasants.

## The constitution as a language

The 1936 constitution gave such complaints a vocabulary. Article 119 promised: "Citizens of the U.S.S.R. have the right to rest and leisure. The right to rest and leisure is ensured by the reduction of the working day to seven hours for the overwhelming majority of the workers..." ([Constitution of the USSR, 5 December 1936](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/stalin/works/1936/12/05.htm)). Ksenia Chekina, a medical student whose internal passport was taken in Leningrad in July 1937 and who was exiled to a village, appealed five times to the head of state: "I know the Constitution, and somehow this is not working out that way." ([Chekina petition, 1937](http://yupnet.org/annals/siegelbaum/English_docs/Siegelbaum_doc_153.htm)). She wrote: "I have been without a job, without shelter, without a piece of bread for seven months already." ([same](http://yupnet.org/annals/siegelbaum/English_docs/Siegelbaum_doc_153.htm)). The internal passport was the document that decided whether a person could study, work and live with their family in a city; without it, Chekina was outside. Lev Kopelev recalled the ruling of 27 December 1932 that introduced passports for city residents in order to "count the population" and "unburden the cities," a measure he later came to see as a way of tying rural people to the village ([Kopelev, *The Education of a True Believer*](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1934/pavlik-morozov/pavlik-morozov-texts/education-of-a-true-believer/)). The campaign to discuss the draft constitution could itself be staged. I. Vasil'ev described a July 1936 meeting where officials replaced an advertised film with a lecture on the draft, refused refunds and kept police in the hall; when a factory worker spoke, the room filled with "an incredible din, shouting, laughter and hissing," and people were detained afterwards ([Vasil'ev letter, July 1936](http://yupnet.org/annals/siegelbaum/English_docs/Siegelbaum_doc_56.htm)). One voice pointed out that people do not pay money to work. The constitution was both a real language of claim and a hollow guarantee. Voronov and Chekina used its words; officials could stage its discussion. The sources cannot tell us how often such claims were made or what people thought in private, only that a language of claim and reply existed.

## Limits of the record

Several gaps run through this record. The 1936 constitution as published ends at Article 146, so the commonly cited "Article 154" does not appear in it; the everyday-life provisions are Articles 7, 12, 118 to 121, 125, 127, 128, 130 and 131. No Stakhanov-era first-hand account by a person named Klimov could be located; the record rests instead on Stakhanov's own narrative, the contemporary press digest and the tractor-brigade reportage. Almost all the voices reach English through translators, and Stakhanov's memoir through a machine translation made in 2026; its wording cannot serve as a citation until the 1937 Russian is checked. Sychov's letter preserves rough spelling, to which editors added punctuation. The people who wrote these documents were unusual: a famous miner, a prize herdsman, a village spokesman, a student and a party activist, people who addressed authority or were written about. Ordinary non-party miners who never set a record are almost absent, as are the families of those excluded from the passport system except through petitions. Petitions exaggerate suffering to win a decision, official memoirs exaggerate loyalty and the opposition digest exaggerated resistance. The newspaper reports on private plots survive only inside Fitzpatrick's book; the underlying *Pravda*, *Trud* and *Izvestiia* issues and the Russian manuscripts were not consulted. A fuller treatment would need the GARF files, the RGASPI files and the Kirov regional archives, together with Russian-language local press from the Donbass and the Urals. None of this evidence can measure how many workers shared these experiences, show what people thought in private, or tell whether the kolkhoz raised or lowered living standards over a lifetime. It supports a texture of daily life, a set of documented conflicts and a language of claim and reply, not a nationwide average or a single verdict on Stalinism.

## How historians have read it

- Lewis H. Siegelbaum, *Stakhanovism and the Politics of Productivity in the USSR, 1935–1941* (1988). The standard monograph; treats the movement as a struggle over shop-floor control, not simply state propaganda, with chapters on managers, on the making of Stakhanovites, and on relations between Stakhanovites and non-Stakhanovites. More attentive than Kotkin to conflict at the point of production. [Source: fulcrum.org](https://www.fulcrum.org/concern/monographs/z603qx68n)
- Stephen Kotkin, *Magnetic Mountain: Stalinism as a Civilization* (1995). An archival and interview-based study of Magnitogorsk arguing that ordinary people entered the socialist project and built lives within it while coercion and privilege remained structural. [Source: ucpress.edu](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/magnetic-mountain/paper)
- Sheila Fitzpatrick, *Stalin's Peasants: Resistance and Survival in the Russian Village after Collectivization* (1996). Uses peasant letters and police reports to argue that villagers called the kolkhoz a "second serfdom" yet extracted concessions such as private plots; shares Viola's rejection of the willing-peasant narrative but stresses everyday adaptation over open revolt. [Source: soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/second-kolkhoz-charter/second-kolkhoz-charter-texts/private-garden-plots)
- Lynne Viola, *Peasant Rebels Under Stalin: Collectivization and the Culture of Peasant Resistance* (1996). Uses OGPU reports to reconstruct mass village unrest in 1930; her counts are a lower bound because they record what the state noticed. [DOI: 001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780195101973.001.0001)
- Lewis Siegelbaum and Andrei Sokolov, eds., *Stalinism as a Way of Life: A Narrative in Documents* (2000; paperback 2004). 157 translated documents, mostly letters from ordinary citizens drawn from newly opened archives; its method is juxtaposition of official claims and private petitions. [Source: yalebooks.yale.edu](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300128598/stalinism-as-a-way-of-life/) ; [Source: yupnet.org](http://yupnet.org/annals/siegelbaum/siegelbaum_list.htm)
- Samantha Lomb, *Stalin's Constitution: Soviet Participatory Politics and the Discussion of the 1936 Draft Constitution* (2017; Routledge 2018, open access). Studies the six-month discussion through Kirov regional archives, showing that citizens used the campaign to press claims about daily life and that local anxieties contributed to the 1937 repression. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315194004)
- Robert W. Thurston, "Reassessing the History of Soviet Workers: Opportunities to Criticize and Participate in Decision-Making, 1935–1941". Uses émigré interviews, local newspapers and archives to argue that workers had real, bounded channels for complaint over norms, pay and housing, challenging the "fear-ridden slaves" picture. [Internet Archive: kupdf.com reassessing the history of soviet workers robert thurston dj](https://archive.org/stream/kupdf.com_reassessing-the-history-of-soviet-workers-robert-thurston/kupdf.com_reassessing-the-history-of-soviet-workers-robert-thurston_djvu.txt)

The live interpretive disagreement is this: Kotkin, Siegelbaum, Fitzpatrick and Lomb find agency within coercion, Thurston finds more room for ordinary influence, and the older totalitarian reading finds little of either. The letters themselves support both the existence of complaint channels and the presence of fear.
