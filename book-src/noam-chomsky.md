Noam Chomsky wrote in two fields that this collection reads together: linguistics, and a body of political essays and talks about war, media and concentrated power. The political work is not a rival system to Marxism. It is a sustained argument that private and state power together narrow democratic life, that official accounts of foreign policy cannot be taken at face value, and that ordinary people already possess the habits of mind needed to see through them. His positive alternative, libertarian socialism — the view that the people who do the work and are affected by it should control institutions directly, rather than private owners or a state bureaucracy doing it on their behalf — placed him at odds with liberal capitalism and with Leninist and state-socialist currents on the left alike. This profile reports what Chomsky argued and what his critics objected to. It does not convert his polemics into established history.

## Biography

Chomsky was born on 7 December 1928 in Philadelphia. The biographical note his own website reproduces from MIT gives the date and city, and the note reproduced from the University of Arizona gives the same. Both describe a career divided between linguistics and political writing, but they differ in emphasis: the MIT note lists linguistic publications and named lectures, while the Arizona note calls him the founder of modern linguistics and one of the most cited scholars in modern history ([chomsky.info, 2002](https://chomsky.info/2002____/); [University of Arizona, 2017](https://chomsky.info/noam-chomsky-ua/)).

He studied at the University of Pennsylvania, taking his BA and PhD there; the PhD was awarded in 1955. His dissertation was titled "Transformational Analysis", and its central claims appeared in the 1957 monograph *Syntactic Structures*. From 1951 to 1955 he was a Junior Fellow of the Harvard Society of Fellows. He joined MIT in 1955, became a full professor in 1961, held the Ferrari P. Ward Professorship from 1966 to 1976 and was appointed Institute Professor in 1976. He spent 1958–59 at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, and delivered the John Locke Lectures at Oxford in 1969, the Bertrand Russell Memorial Lecture at Cambridge in 1970, the Nehru Memorial Lecture in New Delhi in 1972 and the Huizinga Lecture in Leiden in 1977 ([chomsky.info, 2002](https://chomsky.info/2002____/)). MIT's linguistics page now lists him as Institute Professor and Professor of Linguistics emeritus ([MIT Linguistics](https://linguistics.mit.edu/user/chomsky/)). The Arizona record says he joined that university in autumn 2017 after his long MIT career ([University of Arizona, 2017](https://chomsky.info/noam-chomsky-ua/)).

His own account of how he became political is more particular than these institutional summaries. In an interview excerpted from *The Chomsky Reader* he recalls an experimental, Deweyite school he attended until about the age of twelve, where there was no ranking of pupils and "the sense was that everyone was doing something important". High school was the opposite: he found he was a good student, disliked the competition and regimentation, and remembered almost nothing of the classes. At Pennsylvania he planned to leave after two years for political interests. The philosophy course of C. West Churchman, Arabic classes connected partly to politics and partly to his father's work in Hebrew, and the antifascist exile Giorgio Levi Della Vida mattered to him. Through these interests he met the linguist Zellig Harris, whose broad political and intellectual circle drew him into graduate work in linguistics and philosophy; Nelson Goodman recommended him for the Society of Fellows ([Chomsky, "Personal influences"](https://chomsky.info/reader01/)).

The same interview gives the political background. Chomsky describes his teenage politics as "anarchist or left-wing (anti-Leninist) Marxist", combined with a socialist, binationalist view of Palestine and support for the kibbutzim and cooperative labour institutions of the pre-state Jewish settlement. He was "strongly anti-Bolshevik" and could find no organised group that shared his views. An uncle ran a newsstand at Seventy-second Street and Broadway that served as a meeting place for radical debate in the late 1930s. His first article, written at about ten, was a school-newspaper editorial on the fall of Barcelona. He recalls the Depression, police strikebreaking, the rise of nazism in a hostile neighbourhood, and his isolation on the day of the Hiroshima bombing ([Chomsky, "Personal influences"](https://chomsky.info/reader01/)). He spent a short period on a kibbutz in 1953; in the same interview he says both "a few months" and "about six weeks", so the length is not settled by that source. He admired what he called a functioning libertarian community but was repelled by ideological conformity, by the defence of the Slánský trials and by the exclusion of Arabs under land law ([Chomsky, "Personal influences"](https://chomsky.info/reader01/)).

The essay that brought him a wider political audience was "The Responsibility of Intellectuals", first published in the *New York Review of Books* on 23 February 1967. It starts from Dwight Macdonald's question about collective guilt and turns it toward American intellectuals: "Intellectuals are in a position to expose the lies of governments, to analyze actions according to their causes and motives and often hidden intentions." Its central sentence is blunt: "It is the responsibility of intellectuals to speak the truth and to expose lies." The essay examines Vietnam, the Bay of Pigs and the willingness of academics to treat criticism of American policy as irresponsible while accepting official accounts without evidence ([Chomsky, 1967](https://chomsky.info/19670223/)). A later New Press edition paired it with a retrospective essay written on the tenth anniversary of 9/11 ([The New Press description](https://chomsky.info/books/)).

## Intellectual tradition

Chomsky's political position belongs to the anarchist and anarcho-syndicalist tradition, with a strong affinity to council communism and to the anti-Bolshevik left Marxism of Anton Pannekoek and Paul Mattick. Anarcho-syndicalism here means the view that workers' organisations should own and run production directly, rather than captured state power doing it on their behalf. In "Notes on Anarchism", written as an introduction to Daniel Guérin's *Anarchism: From Theory to Practice* in 1970, he states that "a consistent anarchist must be a socialist, but a socialist of a particular sort": such a person opposes private ownership of the means of production and wage labour, but also opposes "the organization of production by government". He quotes Pannekoek's "Five Theses on the Class Struggle" to make the point that the working class cannot free itself by putting "a new directing and governing class substituting itself for the bourgeoisie" in place of the old one ([Chomsky, 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/)).

In a 1970 talk published as "Government in the Future", he set out four positions: classical liberalism, libertarian socialism, state socialism and state capitalism. He identified his own view at the start: "the libertarian socialist concepts, and by that I mean a range of thinking that extends from left-wing Marxism through to anarchism", were "fundamentally correct" and the proper extension of classical liberalism into industrial society. State socialism, which he identified with "what has become of Bolshevism", and state capitalism, which he identified with the modern welfare state, were in his view "regressive and highly inadequate social theories" ([Chomsky, 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

That puts him at a distance from writers who treat the Soviet Union or other communist states as imperfect but genuine socialist projects. In "The Soviet Union Versus Socialism", published in *Our Generation* in 1986, he argues that the claim that the society built by Lenin, Trotsky, Stalin and their successors had any meaningful relation to socialism is a "fantasy" shared by "the world's two great propaganda systems"; the actual relation, he writes, "is the relation of contradiction". He defines socialism as "the liberation of working people from exploitation" and "mastery over production by the producers". He condemns the Bolshevik destruction of factory committees and soviets, and calls the structures created under Lenin and Trotsky "proto-fascist structures converted by Stalin into one of the horrors of the modern age" ([Chomsky, 1986](https://chomsky.info/1986____/)). This is Chomsky's polemical claim, not a finding established here.

He also rejected labels applied to him from other directions. In the *Chomsky Reader* interview he says he never joined a political organisation, because every group he knew on the left was Leninist, "either Stalinist or Trotskyite", and he saw little difference between them. He was drawn emotionally to Hashomer Hatzair but remained unaffiliated ([Chomsky, "Personal influences"](https://chomsky.info/reader01/)). In the 1971 debate with Michel Foucault he identified the concept of human nature as central to his own thinking and defended the place of innate structure in explanations of language and knowledge; Foucault challenged precisely that move ([Chomsky–Foucault debate](https://chomsky.info/1971xxxx/)). Cohen and Rogers, writing sympathetically in *New Left Review* in 1991, report that Chomsky denies originality for his social views and calls himself "a merely 'derivative fellow traveller' in the anarchist and libertarian socialist traditions" ([Cohen and Rogers, 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky)).

## Major contributions

The contribution most relevant to this book is the propaganda model he developed with Edward S. Herman in *Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media*. A propaganda model is an account of how news is selected and shaped rather than a claim that censorship is imposed by decree: in "a world of concentrated wealth and major conflicts of class interest", the media's integrating function "requires systematic propaganda". News passes through five filters — concentrated ownership and profit orientation; advertising as the main income source; reliance on government, business and approved "experts" for information; "flak" as a means of disciplining the media; and anticommunism as "a national religion and control mechanism". The authors argue that these filters fix "the premises of discourse and interpretation" and explain why dissident material is marginalised without any formal censorship ([Herman and Chomsky, chapter 1, full text](https://files.libcom.org/files/2022-04/manufacturing_consent.pdf)). In a 1997 interview Chomsky set out the method: study the institution's internal structure, study its setting in relation to other systems of power, and look for internal records in which insiders explain themselves to one another. He added a "second-order prediction" that the first-order finding would itself be excluded from respectable discussion ([Chomsky, 1997](https://chomsky.info/199710__/)).

The phrase behind the book's title matters here. Chomsky credits Walter Lippmann with the phrase "manufacture of consent" and Edward Bernays with the public-relations technique of engineering consent. Manufactured consent, in this usage, is public support that is produced by institutions through publicity and the selection and framing of news, rather than support that simply arises on its own. Chomsky describes both thinkers as influences he rejects, not predecessors he endorses ([Chomsky, 1997](https://chomsky.info/199710__/)).

The second contribution is his account of the responsibility of intellectuals. The 1967 essay argues that privileged people with access to information have a duty to expose government lies, and that the "cult of the experts" serves power by declaring moral and political questions too complex for ordinary citizens ([Chomsky, 1967](https://chomsky.info/19670223/)). In an *Understanding Power* excerpt he applies the same standard to his own intellectual community, recounting how Norman Finkelstein's demonstration that Joan Peters's *From Time Immemorial* was fraudulent was met with silence in the United States and with reviews in Britain that used his work without acknowledgement. Chomsky presents this as an example of how ideological control works, while noting that Finkelstein later published books with independent presses ([Chomsky, "The Fate of an Honest Intellectual"](https://chomsky.info/power01/)).

Third is his account of state power and foreign policy. *Hegemony or Survival: America's Quest for Global Dominance* argues that the United States has pursued a "grand imperial strategy" for more than half a century, and describes a drive toward "full spectrum dominance" that in his view endangers human survival ([Macmillan publisher record](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781429900218/hegemonyorsurvival/); [Chomsky's book list](https://chomsky.info/books/)). The 1970 talk supplies the domestic counterpart: a claim that "capitalism and democracy are ultimately quite incompatible", because the central institutions of the economy are placed outside democratic control and private power shapes what remains within it ([Chomsky, 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

Fourth is a theory of social change built on ordinary capacities. Against the view that people are easily manipulated, Chomsky argues in the *Chomsky Reader* interview that people apply considerable intelligence to sport but are discouraged from applying it to public affairs because they cannot see any way to affect decisions. He calls the capacity to see through official accounts "Cartesian common sense": it needs "normal skepticism and willingness to apply one's analytical skills", not specialist training. He concedes a criticism from friends that much of his work addresses particular atrocities rather than "revolutionary strategy", admits the force of the point and defends the practical value of concrete cases ([Chomsky, "What the World is Really Like"](https://chomsky.info/reader02/)).

## Analysis of capitalism

Chomsky's account of capitalism starts from the claim that modern capitalism has never been a free market left to itself. In "Government in the Future" he argues that Humboldt's classical liberalism, written before industrial capitalism, was in essence "profoundly, though prematurely, anticapitalist", because its premise that people develop through free, self-directed activity also condemns wage labour and alienated work. Humboldt did not foresee the corporate economy; once that economy existed, the same liberal assumptions led, in Chomsky's reading, to opposition to both state and private domination. He quotes Marx's account of alienated labour and of the worker "mutilated into a fragment of a human being" to show what he takes to be a convergence between early Marx and libertarian thought ([Chomsky, 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

In the same talk he separates political from economic power and argues that the range of decisions open to democratic control "excludes in law in principle the central institutions in any advanced industrial society, i.e. the entire commercial, industrial and financial system". Within the narrow democratic sphere, private power works through control of the media and political organisations and by supplying personnel to government. He quotes Richard Barnet's finding that most of the top 400 decision makers in the postwar national security system came from a small cluster of city blocks, and cites a Federal Trade Commission report on the concentration of manufacturing assets. His conclusion is that "the democratic system at best functions within a narrow range in a capitalist democracy", and that "capitalism and democracy are ultimately quite incompatible" ([Chomsky, 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

He also treats militarised state spending as support for private capital. In the 1970 talk he quotes the business historian Alfred Chandler on the economic effects of the Second World War and the persistence of central control, and concludes that government acts as "a coordinator of last resort" when private managers cannot sustain demand. He calls the cold war a propaganda device that served "the long time imperial enterprise" and helped the managers of both great power blocs control their own populations ([Chomsky, 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)). Alison Edgley, writing in 2009, summarises this as the claim that the state supplies the legislative conditions and coercive authority for capitalism, is itself a major economic actor, and provides "welfare for the rich" through subsidy and guaranteed markets ([Edgley, 2009](https://www.westminsterpapers.org/article/id/128/download/pdf/)).

Class appears in Chomsky's work less as a band of income than as a relation of control. He calls work performed because the worker must sell their labour power "wage slavery", and treats hierarchy inside the firm as a political question: "industrial feudalism" is his phrase for autocratic control of production. In "Notes on Anarchism" he insists that the reduction of people to "an appurtenance of the machine" could in principle be reduced by technology, "but not under the conditions of autocratic control of production by those who make man an instrument to serve their ends" ([Chomsky, 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/)). In the 1997 media interview he describes corporations as "basically tyrannies, hierarchic, controled from above", and argues that universities and media reproduce the same hierarchy through dependence on outside funders and through socialisation that selects for obedience ([Chomsky, 1997](https://chomsky.info/199710__/)).

## Analysis of socialism

Chomsky wrote no blueprint for a fully specified socialist economy, and he explicitly distrusts fixed social theory. Cohen and Rogers observe that he "does not have a theory of society or justice, in the sense of a clearly elaborated and defended set of fundamental principles", and that he treats social and ethical thought as relying on "guesses, hopes, expectations" until a theory of human nature exists ([Cohen and Rogers, 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky)). Within that limit his preference is clear. In "Government in the Future" he defines the libertarian socialist position as the elimination of state power in favour of "the democratic organization of the industrial society with direct popular control over all institutions by those who participate in as well as those who are directly affected by the workings of these institutions". He imagines "workers' councils, consumer councils, commune assemblies, regional federations" with representatives who are "directly answerable to and return directly to" the group they represent ([Chomsky, 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

He argues that "one might argue, or at least I would argue, that council communism in this sense is the natural form of revolutionary socialism in an industrial society", because democracy is "largely a sham" whenever production is controlled by "owners, managers, technocrats, a vanguard party, a state bureaucracy". He rejects two standard objections. Against the claim that free cooperation is contrary to human nature, he cites Rousseau, Kant, Humboldt and Luxemburg for the view that people learn freedom only by exercising it. Against the claim that democratic control is inefficient, he answers that the same technology which brings information to managers can bring it to the whole workforce, and that "the very concept of efficiency is dripping with ideology" ([Chomsky, 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

"Notes on Anarchism" adds that workers' organisations must create "not only the ideas, but also the facts of the future itself" before a revolution, quoting Bakunin. He praises the Spanish Revolution of 1936 as a case where years of patient organisation in unions and assemblies prepared workers to collectivise industry and agriculture, and warns that "spontaneous revolutionary action" is a misleading phrase if it suggests that no preparation occurred ([Chomsky, 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/)). The essay states the refusal of state socialism in the strongest terms: "control of production by a state bureaucracy, no matter how benevolent its intentions, also does not create the conditions under which labor, manual and intellectual, can become the highest want in life" ([Chomsky, 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/)).

The 1986 essay applies this standard to actually existing communist states. Chomsky argues that socialism means workers mastering production, and that the Bolshevik leadership destroyed the factory committees and soviets that might have realised it. He ends by calling for "a way to save the socialist ideal from its enemies in both of the world's major centres of power" ([Chomsky, 1986](https://chomsky.info/1986____/)). This is the sharpest contrast with [Michael Parenti](/michael-parenti) and Domenico Losurdo in the framing of this collection: both treat historical communist projects with more sympathy, but their works were not opened in the dossier for this profile, so no claim about the content of their arguments is made here. In practice Chomsky has supported limited use of state authority. George Fish, a socialist reviewer, notes that *Chomsky on Anarchism* records "limited, pragmatic support for state authority in order to protect the people's rights and gains" and that Chomsky says he votes, especially in local elections ([Fish, 2010](https://newpol.org/review/chomsky-anarchism-and-socialism/)). That observation comes from a critic and should be read as his interpretation of the collection.

## Recommended reading

These selections are starting points rather than a survey of Chomsky's whole output.

- *Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media* (1988) — Sets out the propaganda model's five filters, from concentrated ownership to anticommunism, explaining how dissident news is marginalised without formal censorship; the best starting point for his account of media power.
- "The Responsibility of Intellectuals" (1967) — Argues that people with access to information have a duty to expose government lies and that the "cult of the experts" keeps moral questions out of ordinary debate; the clearest entry into his case for intellectual responsibility.
- *Government in the Future* (2005) — Sets out his claim that capitalism and democracy are ultimately incompatible and defines libertarian socialism as direct popular control of institutions through workers' and consumer councils; the best starting point for both his critique of capitalism and his positive alternative.
- "Notes on Anarchism" (1970) — Argues that workers' organisations must build the facts of a future society before a revolution, praising the Spanish Revolution of 1936 and rejecting state socialism; the clearest entry into his anarchist socialism.
- *Hegemony or Survival: America's Quest for Global Dominance* (2003) — Argues that the United States has pursued a "grand imperial strategy" whose drive to "full spectrum dominance" endangers survival; the best starting point for his account of state power and foreign policy.

## Intellectual influences

Chomsky's own interviews provide the strongest evidence for influence. He names Zellig Harris as the person who drew him from a planned dropout into graduate linguistics and philosophy, and mentions C. West Churchman, Giorgio Levi Della Vida, Nelson Goodman, Morton White and the mathematician Nathan Fine as teachers who made his unconventional education productive. His uncle's radical newsstand and the Jewish working-class culture of New York, with its mixture of Freud, Marx, literature and chamber music, were in his words "the most influential intellectual culture during my early teens" ([Chomsky, "Personal influences"](https://chomsky.info/reader01/)). In the 1967 essay he explicitly credits Dwight Macdonald's articles on the responsibility of intellectuals, read as an undergraduate, with framing his own question about war guilt and intellectual duty ([Chomsky, 1967](https://chomsky.info/19670223/)).

In political theory the line of influence runs through Bakunin, Proudhon, Rudolf Rocker, Daniel Guérin, Karl Polanyi and the early Marx, with Pannekoek, Mattick, Luxemburg and the young Trotsky as anti-Leninist Marxists he cites approvingly. "Notes on Anarchism" quotes Rocker, Bakunin, Guérin, Pannekoek, William Paul and Rosa Luxemburg, and treats Guérin's project of rehabilitating anarchism as a contribution that can "enrich Marxism" ([Chomsky, 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/)). "Government in the Future" adds Wilhelm von Humboldt, Rousseau, Kant and Polanyi; Chomsky treats Humboldt's *The Limits of State Action* as a text whose premises lead, once industrial capitalism is understood, to libertarian socialism ([Chomsky, 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)). In the media work he credits Lippmann and Bernays, as noted above, in order to reject them ([Chomsky, 1997](https://chomsky.info/199710__/)).

The linguistic work matters here only because of what Chomsky and his interpreters make of it politically. In the 1971 debate he argues that the gap between the "small and rather degenerate" data given to a child and the rich knowledge the child acquires supports the postulate of innate "schematism", which he calls a constituent of human nature ([Chomsky–Foucault debate](https://chomsky.info/1971xxxx/)). Cohen and Rogers show how this connects to his politics: the human capacity for "normal creativity" underwrites both his moral claim that people need autonomy and his expectation that authoritarian systems must repress it ([Cohen and Rogers, 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky)). Chris Knight, a Marxist critic, makes the same connection in order to attack it; Chomsky rejects Knight's account in a detailed reply ([Knight, 2002/2010](http://radicalanthropologygroup.org/wp-content/uploads/pub_chomsky_politics_science.pdf); [Chomsky, 2019](https://chomsky.info/20190905-2/)). These are disputes about method, not settled facts about the mind.

## Influence and legacy

Chomsky's political influence is hard to measure because it is strongest in movements rather than in institutions. The documentary *Manufacturing Consent: Noam Chomsky and the Media* is one documented channel: Chomsky reports that it was shown on national television in many countries outside the United States, generated a large volume of reviews, and prompted letters from viewers who had organised screenings in their own communities. He also records the limits of that influence: he thought the film personalised collective work and turned viewers into would-be followers, and says the same problem appears in public speaking and book signing ([Chomsky, "An Exchange on Manufacturing Consent"](https://chomsky.info/power02/)). The New Press describes *Understanding Power* as a collection of previously unpublished talks in which he links imperial foreign policy to declining domestic living standards and "establishes a theory of social change" ([The New Press](https://thenewpress.org/books/understanding-power/)).

Academic reception is a different story. Edgley notes that Chomsky's political work remains marginal within the social sciences, that academics often dismiss it as "polemical" without demonstrating that it is, and that his work is "not even read to be rejected" in some fields ([Edgley, 2009](https://www.westminsterpapers.org/article/id/128/download/pdf/)). Mullen's peer-reviewed study of the propaganda model's reception reaches a similar conclusion from another angle: using counts of journal articles and textbooks, it argues that the model has been systematically marginalised within media and communication studies, as Herman and Chomsky had predicted ([Mullen, 2010](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/twenty-years-on-the-second-order-prediction-of-the-herman-chomsky/)). Mullen and Klaehn's companion article situates the propaganda model in the Marxist-radical tradition of media studies and sets out its three hypotheses and five operative principles ([Mullen and Klaehn, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-9020.2010.00275.x)). These studies support a claim about reception; they do not by themselves prove that the propaganda model is correct.

Cohen and Rogers's 1991 survey shows the same duality. They treat Chomsky's social thought as a coherent and optimistic perspective worth reconstructing, while stressing that its central claims rest on a contested view of human nature and that Chomsky himself offers no systematic social theory ([Cohen and Rogers, 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky)). For this collection his legacy is best described as a set of questions and methods: who benefits from a given policy, whose sources count as evidence, and how institutions teach people to think about their own interests.

## Criticisms and controversies

Criticism from within the Marxist tradition is substantial and takes several forms. Chris Knight's essay "Noam Chomsky: Politics or Science?", written in 2002, published in *What Next? Marxist discussion journal* in 2003 and revised in 2010, argues that Chomsky's linguistics and his anarchist politics had to be reconciled, and that he achieved this by presenting a formal, asocial theory of language as a liberating science. Knight points to the military funding acknowledged in the preface to *Syntactic Structures* — the Army Signal Corps, Air Force research command, Navy research, the National Science Foundation and Eastman Kodak — and argues that a "cognitive revolution" stripped of meaning and social content suited the needs of the computer age and its sponsors. He further claims that Chomsky's turn to "normal creativity" and an innate language capacity was designed to make his linguistics harmless to his political conscience ([Knight, 2002/2010](http://radicalanthropologygroup.org/wp-content/uploads/pub_chomsky_politics_science.pdf)). Chomsky's 2019 reply calls this a "web of deceit and misinformation", points out that the formal work began in the 1940s and 1950s before MIT, cites interviews with students at MITRE who said their work was syntax rather than command and control, and argues that military funding of universities in that period was general support for higher education under a defence label ([Chomsky, 2019](https://chomsky.info/20190905-2/)). Both texts are polemical. The dossier records the dispute and does not adjudicate it.

A second Marxist objection concerns his treatment of the Russian Revolution. George Fish, reviewing *Chomsky on Anarchism* for the socialist journal *New Politics* in 2010, accepts that Chomsky's critique of Bolshevism has "elements of merit and political insight" but calls it "simplistic" where it treats Lenin and Trotsky as laying the foundations of Stalinism and traces some failures to Marx and Engels themselves. Fish also objects that Chomsky's account of intellectuals "deterministically reduces them to careerists, commissars, or apologists", and asks how Chomsky himself could emerge from such a class. At the same time Fish finds common ground, including Chomsky's defence of democracy against anarchist dismissals of it, his qualified support for state authority and his critique of incomprehensible left-wing jargon ([Fish, 2010](https://newpol.org/review/chomsky-anarchism-and-socialism/)). This is a review in a partisan journal, not a peer-reviewed study.

A third line of criticism is theoretical rather than Marxist. In the 1971 debate Foucault argues that "human nature" is not a scientific concept of the kind Chomsky claims but an "epistemological indicator" that marks off a domain of inquiry. On creativity, Foucault says his own concern is with collective rules and transformations of knowledge rather than the originating subject. He explicitly asks whether the regularities that make knowledge possible might be located "somewhere else, even outside the human mind, in social forms, in the relations of production, in the class struggles", and says he finds it difficult to accept Chomsky's placement of those regularities in human nature ([Chomsky–Foucault debate](https://chomsky.info/1971xxxx/)). Cohen and Rogers, though broadly sympathetic, make a related point: Chomsky's four key claims about moral nature, autonomy, social stability and hope are "neither concrete nor precise", and his political conclusions depend on a view of human nature that is "speculative and imprecise" ([Cohen and Rogers, 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky)). Edgley likewise calls his account of human nature "essentialist (yet unverifiable, so open to amendment)", while judging that it gives his social analysis a distinctive and coherent foundation ([Edgley, 2009](https://www.westminsterpapers.org/article/id/128/download/pdf/)).

Chomsky's writing on Cambodia is the sharpest dispute in this collection, and it can now be reported more precisely than the first version of this profile allowed. In the 1977 review "Distortions at Fourth Hand", written with Edward Herman, he gave weight to analyses that put executions "at most in the thousands", called one book based on refugee testimony a "third-rate propaganda tract", found François Ponchaud's book "serious and worth reading" but accused it of playing "fast and loose with quotes and with numbers", and concluded that the authors did "not pretend to know where the truth lies amidst these sharply conflicting assessments" ([Chomsky and Herman, 1977](https://chomsky.info/19770625/)). Chomsky's own article index dates the piece 25 June 1977 while the text page gives 6 June 1977, so only the year is asserted here.

The most detailed published criticism is Bruce Sharp's long, footnoted essay "Averaging Wrong Answers". Sharp argues that the underlying theme of the 1977 review and of *After the Cataclysm* was that Khmer Rouge Cambodia was not as bad as its detractors claimed, and that Chomsky's work on the subject is "marred by omissions, dubious statistics, and, in some cases, outright misrepresentations" ([Sharp](https://www.mekong.net/cambodia/chomsky.htm)). He objects in particular to the favourable treatment given to Hildebrand and Porter's book alongside the dismissal of books based on refugee testimony, and reports that 33 of the 50 citations in one chapter of that book come from Khmer Rouge sources and six from the Chinese news agency Hsinhua. The essay is self-published on a specialist Cambodia archive, is not peer reviewed, and is recorded here as a charge rather than a finding. Sophal Ear's 1995 political science honours thesis at Berkeley goes further, placing Chomsky and Herman in a "Khmer Rouge Canon" of works that supported the regime explicitly or implicitly ([Ear, 1995](https://phdn.org/archives/www.ess.uwe.ac.uk/genocide/cambear1.htm)); it is an undergraduate thesis, not a peer-reviewed study. In a 1991 exchange with Chomsky, the Oxford historian Jamie Metzl argued that the doubt created by the argument over Cambodia "somewhat inhibited decisive action", while conceding that Chomsky had written only one or two pieces on the subject ([Metzl correspondence](https://jamiemetzl.com/noam-chomsky-and-the-cambodian-genocide/)). That is Metzl's interpretation, not a demonstrated causal finding, and Chomsky disputes it.

The strongest independent scholarly evidence in the opened record comes from Ben Kiernan, a historian of Cambodia. In 1979 he told readers of the *Bulletin of Concerned Asian Scholars* that he had "changed my mind": he had been "late in realizing the extent of the tragedy in Kampuchea after 1975 and Pol Pot's responsibility for it", had been wrong to explain the violence after 1973 simply as a grassroots reaction, and had been "too willing to believe" that refugee testimony and the Phnom Penh radio could be reconciled ([Kiernan, 1979](https://gsp.yale.edu/sites/default/files/bkbcaoct.1979vietnam_kampuchea.pdf)). In a 2001 journal article he concluded that "Massive Khmer Rouge brutality in the repression of Cambodian 'treason' resulted in genocide", that more than one and a half million Cambodians perished under the regime, and that absolute control lay with a small party centre around Pol Pot ([Kiernan, 2001](https://gsp.yale.edu/sites/default/files/myth_nationalism_and_genocide.pdf)). Sharp reports that Chomsky and Herman drew on Kiernan's earlier, more qualified work; that link is Sharp's account, not a separately verified finding. Chomsky's own position is stated in his letter of 3 February 1991 to Metzl: that in 1977 the facts were "extremely sparse and uncertain", that he expressed no firm conclusion and never denied that atrocities were taking place, and that with hindsight he would "emphasize more strongly that atrocities were taking place on a massive scale" while retaining his criticism of double standards in Western reporting ([Chomsky, 1991](https://jamiemetzl.com/noam-chomsky-and-the-cambodian-genocide/)). What the opened sources support is a disagreement about the weight to give to uncertain reports and about the use Chomsky and Herman made of them. The stronger charge that Chomsky supported the Khmer Rouge or denied the genocide is a critic's characterisation which he rejects and which Sharp himself treats as unsettled; it is not adopted here.

The propaganda model has also drawn substantial academic criticism. The full text of the 2010 review by Andrew Mullen and Jeffery Klaehn records a first wave of hostile reviews in the late 1980s and 1990s whose authors charged that the model overstated the power of the propaganda system and downplayed popular opposition to elite preferences, presented a "conspiratorial" view of the media, was deterministic, functionalist and simplistic, and neglected journalistic professionalism ([Mullen and Klaehn, 2010, full text](https://aklatangbayan.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/mullen-klaehn-sociology-compass-propaganda-model.pdf)). A second wave included John Corner's six objections in 2003, among them doubt that a model built for the United States could apply to countries with different media systems, the complaint that the five filters were "assumed to function without much, if any, need for further specification or qualification", and the question whether filtering produces media messages or only modifies existing ones; Kurt and Gladys Lang's 2004 objections to the absence of sampling and coding information and to the assumption that information existed but had been screened out; and Oliver Boyd-Barrett's call for a way to weigh the relative power of the filters and for a sixth filter covering intelligence-agency influence over journalists. These are Mullen and Klaehn's summaries of other writers, whose original articles were not opened for this profile. The two authors' own evidence concerns reception rather than truth: in a sample of 3,053 articles from ten media and communication journals over 1988–2007, only 79 (2.6 per cent) attended to the model, and of 48 course textbooks surveyed over 1990–2007 only 11 engaged with it at all. That supports a claim of marginalisation within the field. It does not settle whether the model is correct.

## Related thinkers

Edward S. Herman is the indispensable partner for this profile. *Manufacturing Consent* is jointly authored, and Chomsky has said that Herman's name was placed first at his insistence because Herman did most of the book ([Chomsky, "An Exchange on Manufacturing Consent"](https://chomsky.info/power02/)). Herman also co-authored *Counter-Revolutionary Violence*, *After the Cataclysm* and *The Washington Connection and Third World Fascism*, all listed in the author's chronology ([Chomsky's book list](https://chomsky.info/books/)).

Michel Foucault is a related thinker through disagreement: both addressed power and knowledge, but Foucault rejected the appeal to a fixed human nature and located the conditions of knowledge in historical practices and social relations ([Chomsky–Foucault debate](https://chomsky.info/1971xxxx/)). Bakunin, Rocker, Guérin, Pannekoek, Mattick, Luxemburg and Polanyi are positive influences on the socialist side ([Chomsky, 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/); [Chomsky, 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)). Cohen and Rogers and Alison Edgley are scholarly interpreters whose work summarises and tests his social thought ([Cohen and Rogers, 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky); [Edgley, 2009](https://www.westminsterpapers.org/article/id/128/download/pdf/)). Chris Knight and George Fish are critics writing from Marxist or socialist positions ([Knight, 2002/2010](http://radicalanthropologygroup.org/wp-content/uploads/pub_chomsky_politics_science.pdf); [Fish, 2010](https://newpol.org/review/chomsky-anarchism-and-socialism/)). The connection to [Michael Parenti](/michael-parenti) and to Domenico Losurdo is one of political contrast in the framing of this collection; neither writer's works were opened for the dossier, so no claim about the content of their positions is made here.
