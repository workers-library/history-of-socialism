On 15 March 1921, with the revolt at Kronstadt still unresolved, Lenin told the Tenth Party Congress that the revolution had to give way to the peasantry. The New Economic Policy, or NEP, is the name later given to that retreat: a fixed tax in place of requisitioning, and permission for peasants to sell what remained in markets. "The question of substituting a tax for surplus-grain appropriation is primarily and mainly a political question, for it is essentially a question of the attitude of the working class to the peasantry." (Vladimir Lenin, speech at the Tenth Party Congress, 15 March 1921, English translation. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1921/tenth-party-congress/tenth-party-congress-texts/lenin-explains-the-tax-in-kind-and-the-new-policy)). He admitted that free exchange meant turning back towards capitalism and that the party had gone too far in nationalising local trade. Four days later the All-Russian Central Executive Committee made the concession law. "This tax must be less than what the peasant has given up to this time through requisitions." (Tax in Kind decree, 21 March 1921, English translation. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1921/new-economic-policy/new-economic-policy-texts/nep-in-the-countryside/))

## The country the policy had to lift

The change was made from weakness. J. P. Goodrich, a former governor of Indiana surveying the Volga famine zone for the American Relief Administration, slept in peasants' homes and checked communal records and warehouses, choosing villages away from the railways. "In making my hurried investigation I did not trust to Government statistics, to appearances in the bazaars or on the cities' streets" (J. P. Goodrich, letter to Herbert Hoover, 1 November 1921. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1921/famine-of-1921-22/famine-of-1921-22-texts/american-descriptions-of-the-volga-famine)). He found villagers gathering famine seeds, eating cabbage leaves and melon rinds and butchering dogs; in the German commune of Schilling he listed 25 deaths from cholera, 30 from typhus and 45 from starvation since January. Goodrich was arguing for congressional funds and discounted what he called sensational newspaper reporting, so his figures are a relief advocate's case, but the case rests on houses he entered and records he read. NEP had to work from this baseline.

## The market and its losers

In the cities the retreat was visible first in shop windows. Walter Duranty, the *New York Times* correspondent in Moscow, watched cafés reopen and traced a Finnish trader who started with a trestle table of relief food packets and within a year had a shop, four salesmen and enough profit to retire. "The first twelve months of NEP. were like the old Roman Saturnalia, when for three days each year slaves and underlings might usurp with impunity the pleasures and privileges of their masters." (Walter Duranty, writing in 1935 about 1921–22. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1921/new-economic-policy/new-economic-policy-texts/moscow-from-ara-to-nep/)). A Nepman, the period's word for a private trader, became a recognisable type within a year or two. The state advertised through avant-garde artists and lampooned the same figure as a tax evader; Kardovskii painted *NEPmen*, Lebedev a *NEP Girl*, Maliutin a set of *NEP Types*, and Kustodiev drew the city-and-village link, the *smychka*, as an official theme. Fedor Gladkov's novel *Cement*, published in 1925 and later revised, follows a soldier and factory worker back into a town where shops are opening while rations continue. "An ironical hand had obliterated the first ' not ' in the last of these sentences" (Fedor Gladkov, *Cement*, 1925, English translation of the 1929 edition. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/nepmen/cement/)). Fiction cannot prove an event, but it records how the return of trade looked to a Bolshevik worker.

Not everyone could profit from exchange. Markoosha Fischer, who first visited Russia in 1922 and later worked in child welfare there, recalled that NEP brought unemployment. "NEP brought unemployment. In order to become self-supporting, enterprises reduced the number of workers and got rid in the first place of young people without practical experience." (Markoosha Fischer, *My Lives in Russia*, 1944, recalling 1922. [Source](https://www.thetedkarchive.com/library/markoosha-fischer-my-lives-in-russia)). She also remembered former aristocrats hoping for their apartments back, and fathers who disapproved of Nepmen while hoping that NEP meant a return to the good old days. Her book was written after she left the Soviet Union in 1939 and after her husband's break with the regime, so the dialogue is recalled, not recorded at the time.

## The scissors

By 1923 the prices of manufactured goods and grain had pulled so far apart that the two lines were compared to an opened pair of scissors. Lewis Siegelbaum puts industrial prices at 276 per cent of their 1913 level by October 1923 against agricultural prices at 89 per cent, a historian's calculation rather than testimony. Trotsky's answer was to make goods cheaper and coordinate industry with the peasant market rather than repeat the party's slogan of the *smychka*, the bond between town and country. "What the peasant asks of us is not to repeat a correct historical formula of class relationships (smychka, etc.) but that we supply him with cheaper nails, cloth and matches." (Leon Trotsky, *The New Course*, ch. 6, 1923, English translation. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1923/newcourse/ch06.htm)). Bukharin replied after Lenin's death that the conflict was contingent rather than fixed. "Trotsky's mistake is to consider the conflict between the proletariat and the peasantry inevitable, when it is only possible." (Nikolai Bukharin, "The Theory of Permanent Revolution," 13 December 1924, English translation. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/scissors-crisis/scissors-crisis-texts/bukharin-on-the-worker-peasant-alliance/)). He spoke as the winner of that argument and was later removed from power; the price figures suggest a gap deeper than a failure of will.

The price crisis became an argument about the party itself. On 8 October 1923 Trotsky wrote to the Central Committee that secretaries were now appointed from above. "The bureaucratization of the party apparatus has developed to unheard-of proportions by means of the method of secretarial selection." (Leon Trotsky, letter to the Central Committee and Central Control Commission, 8 October 1923, English translation. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1921/workers-opposition/workers-opposition-texts/trotsky-protests-bureaucratization)). A week later forty-six members sent a secret declaration to the Politburo, connecting an economic and financial crisis that had begun in July to a "secretarial hierarchy" that chose delegates. "Free discussion within the party has in fact disappeared; the party's social mind has been choked off." (Declaration of the Forty-Six, 15 October 1923, English translation. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1921/workers-opposition/workers-opposition-texts/declaration-of-the-forty-six)). Both are opposition documents making a case against their rivals, but they agree on the atmosphere: members were afraid to speak at party meetings.

## The last writings and the end

Lenin's final articles belong to the same moment. In "On Cooperation," written in January 1923 while he was recovering from a stroke and out of office, he argued that cooperatives under NEP had become the road to socialism. "By adopting NEP we made a concession to the peasant as a trader, to the principal of private trade" (Vladimir Lenin, "On Cooperation," written 4 January 1923, English translation; "principal" is the translated text's wording. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1923/jan/06.htm)). A month later he attacked the "bustle" of repeated administrative reorganisation, calling the state apparatus deplorable and pleading for patience. "We must follow the rule: Better fewer, but better." (Vladimir Lenin, "Better Fewer, But Better," *Pravda*, 4 March 1923, English translation. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/lenin-enrollment/lenin-enrollment-texts/better-fewer-but-better)). Weeks before those articles he had dictated a private note on the men who might succeed him. "Stalin is too rude [Russian: grubyi] and this failing, which is quite tolerable in our midst and in relations among us Communists, becomes intolerable in the office of General Secretary." (Vladimir Lenin, postscript to "Letter to the Thirteenth Party Congress," dictated 4 January 1923, first published 1957; English translation. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/lenins-succession/lenins-succession-texts/lenins-testament)). That note, published thirty-three years later, is the centre of the so-called Lenin Enigma: the creator of the General Secretary's office warning against the man who held it. Stalin claimed the inheritance in public within days of Lenin's death, presenting himself as the executor of his behests. Neither the note nor the articles say plainly whether NEP was a temporary concession or a new route, and that remains disputed.

The end came through the same everyday machinery. Fischer recalled villagers in the Ukrainian countryside in 1927 and 1928 asking officials how many cattle or acres they could have before being called kulaks, the label for peasants deemed prosperous enough to be class enemies. After 1929 the Moscow unemployment exchange closed, and former Nepmen were disenfranchised. The trader who had appeared in Kardovskii's paintings was now a target of the tax inspector, a type photographed in 1930, well after NEP at its height. A policy that had begun by allowing peasants to sell a surplus ended with the state deciding who was allowed to own a cow.

## Limits of the record

Nothing here rests on physical archive access. The record is weighted towards Moscow, party leaders and English translations. The most vivid ordinary voices belong to outsiders, Duranty, Goodrich and Fischer, or to a novelist, Gladkov; no Nepman's memoir and no village petition or diary from the NEP years has been located. No Russian-language archive catalogue was searched. The scissors price series reaches us through a historian's essay, not the original tables. Duranty's later reporting on Ukraine is heavily disputed, so his NEP observations are used only for the texture of Moscow; Fischer wrote after leaving the country and her retrospections are labelled as such. The visual sources carry titles and dates but no repository provenance beyond the site that holds them. The suppressed material around the Lenin Enigma, including the full dictation record, the December 1922 articles on the dictatorship of the proletariat and the medical details of the strokes, is not part of this record, so the Testament cannot stand for the whole of Lenin's last position.

## How historians have read it

- **Alan M. Ball, *Russia's Last Capitalists: The Nepmen, 1921–1929* (University of California Press; paperback edition 1990).** Examines where the Nepmen came from, how they competed with the socialist sector and what followed their liquidation. [Source: ucpress.edu](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/russias-last-capitalists/paper)
- **Moshe Lewin, *Lenin's Last Struggle* (Pantheon, 1968; translation of *Le dernier combat de Lénine*).** The standard study of Lenin's final months and the "Lenin Enigma"; the work to test whether "On Cooperation" marks a strategic change. [Internet Archive: leninslaststrugg00lewi](https://archive.org/details/leninslaststrugg00lewi)
- **Robert V. Daniels, *The Conscience of the Revolution: Communist Opposition in Soviet Russia* (Harvard University Press, 1960).** A documentary history of inner-party opposition to 1924, the natural companion to the 1923 documents. [Internet Archive: conscienceofrevo0000dani](https://archive.org/details/conscienceofrevo0000dani)
- **E. H. Carr, *The Interregnum 1923–1924*, vol. 4 of *A History of Soviet Russia* (Macmillan, 1954).** A documentary narrative of the scissors crisis and the succession, strong on the mechanics of party decision-making. [Internet Archive: interregnumedwa](https://archive.org/details/interregnum192310000edwa)
- **Lewis Siegelbaum, subject essays "Lenin Introduces NEP," "Smychka and the Scissors Crisis" and "Nepmen," in *Seventeen Moments in Soviet History*.** Treats Nepmen as the embodiment of a policy that tried to build socialism "with bourgeois hands" and dates the peak of the scissors to October 1923. [Source: soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/scissors-crisis/)
- **Nicolas Werth, "The Soviet State and Peasants," in *The Cambridge History of Communism*, ed. Silvio Pons and Stephen A. Smith (Cambridge University Press, 2017), pp. 399–423.** A survey of state-peasant relations across the communist world, broader than the Russian case. [Cambridge University Press: B9CF388324C0CFC2B624438EBBE5510B](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-history-of-communism/soviet-state-and-peasants/B9CF388324C0CFC2B624438EBBE5510B)

Broad agreement: NEP was a retreat forced by crisis; Nepmen and kulaks reappeared because the state could not supply exchange; the worker-peasant alliance was the central political problem; and Stalin's victory ended the argument. Live disagreement: whether Lenin's last articles reorient strategy, whether the scissors was mainly a pricing failure or a structural imbalance, and how much weight to give Bukharin's "possible" conflict against Trotsky's demand for planning. Ball and Siegelbaum treat the Nepmen as a distinct social group; the memoir sources here do not.
