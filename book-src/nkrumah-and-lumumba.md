Kwame Nkrumah led the Gold Coast to independence as Ghana in 1957 and was overthrown in 1966 ([Official Ghanaian *Biographical Sketch*, 1962](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/nkrumah-bio.pdf); [Nkrumah, *The Dark Days in Ghana*, 1968](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1968/dark-days.pdf)). Patrice Lumumba became the first prime minister of the independent Congo in 1960 and was killed seven months later ([Patrice Lumumba, 1984 biography](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/lumumba-bio.pdf)). Both men argued that a flag and a national anthem were not enough. Both believed that political freedom had to be completed by economic control and by the unity of the whole continent. And both wrote their last major texts in exile or confinement, addressing the question of how their defeats would be remembered.

The idea that connected them was Pan-Africanism. In its simplest form this was the belief that people of African descent everywhere shared a common political destiny, and that the separate states of Africa should act together rather than compete. For Nkrumah it became a programme of continental institutions. For Lumumba it was a security doctrine: if the Congo fell, he argued, every independent African state would be exposed. Both were working during the Cold War, the long contest between the United States and its allies and the Soviet Union and its allies that shaped politics from the late 1940s until the early 1990s. Neither man wanted Ghana or the Congo to become a client of either side. Their speeches and letters, preserved in the movements' publications, show what that refusal meant in practice, and where their hopes ran up against the limits of their power.

## Kwame Nkrumah: from mission school to the Pan-African Congress

The official Ghanaian *Biographical Sketch* issued by the Ghana Information and Trade Center on 9 April 1962 records the basic facts. Nkrumah was born on 21 September 1909 at Nkroful, in what was then the Western Province of the Gold Coast, the son of a goldsmith. He attended mission schools and trained as a teacher at Prince of Wales College at Achimota. He went to the United States in 1935 to study at Lincoln University in Pennsylvania, majored in economics and sociology, and graduated in 1939. He then studied theology and education at the University of Pennsylvania and taught philosophy, Negro history and Greek at Lincoln while working towards a doctorate. To pay his way he worked in shipyards, went to sea as a steward during vacations and preached in Black churches in Pennsylvania and New York. ([Official Biographical Sketch, 1962](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/nkrumah-bio.pdf))

A movement biography published in Moscow in 1984 gives a compatible account, adding that he trained at Accra and Achimota after Catholic primary school and began his career teaching in Catholic schools at Elmina and Axim. It says that in the United States he studied Marx, Engels and Lenin alongside Black American writers and educationalists. The agreement between a government sketch and a movement biography on this point is significant, because it means the broad outline of his formation is not in serious dispute. He was a mission-educated Gold Coast teacher who had worked with his hands and acquired a wide reading in the United States before he entered politics. ([*Kwame Nkrumah*, in *Fighters for National Liberation*, 1984](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/nkrumah-bio-2.pdf))

In 1945 Nkrumah left the United States for Britain to study at the London School of Economics, joined Gray's Inn to read law and served from 1945 to 1947 as general secretary of the West African National Secretariat, an organisation set up to work for a united Africa. His own account of 1945 is the founding document of his Pan-Africanism. In *Africa Must Unite* he wrote that Pan-Africanism and African nationalism took concrete expression when the Fifth Pan-African Congress met in Manchester. More than two hundred delegates attended, and he and George Padmore had been joint secretaries of the organising committee. The congress addressed a declaration to the colonial peoples of the world. It called on intellectuals and professionals to awaken to their responsibilities, told them that liberties would be won through trade union rights, cooperatives, a free press and the right to organise, and ended with the claim that there was only one road to effective action: the organisation of the masses. Nkrumah identified himself as the author of the declaration. ([Nkrumah, *Africa Must Unite*, 1963, chapter 15, pp. 134 to 135](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf))

The declaration carried three commitments. Liberties had to be organised for, not petitioned for. The workers, farmers and trade unionists were the subjects of liberation rather than its beneficiaries. And the programme was both national and continental, since national independence was the road to African unity. Nkrumah recorded the organisational consequences. The congress set up a working committee with W. E. B. Du Bois as chairman and himself as general secretary, moved its headquarters to London, and established the West African National Secretariat there to carry out in West Africa the policies agreed in Manchester. An inner group called the Circle admitted only those working genuinely for West African freedom and unity and prepared for what he called revolutionary work anywhere on the continent. He also stated plainly why he had gone back: "When I returned to West Africa in 1947, it was with the intention of using the Gold Coast as a starting-off point for African independence and unity." ([*Africa Must Unite*, pp. 135 to 136](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf))

### Positive action and the founding of the CPP

Nkrumah returned to the Gold Coast in 1947 as general secretary of the United Gold Coast Convention, a nationalist body led by established professionals. In 1948 he was arrested in connection with unrest and exiled to the north of the country. In June 1949 he broke away and founded the Convention People's Party, the CPP, which demanded immediate self-government. The official sketch describes a campaign of strikes and boycotts and says that early in 1950 he was imprisoned on charges of sedition and fomenting an illegal general strike during what was called the positive action campaign. Positive action was his term for non-violent mass pressure: strikes, boycotts and demonstrations used to force political change. He defined it in a single sentence in the official sketch as the non-violent method by which Ghana fought for and won its independence. ([Official Biographical Sketch, 1962](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/nkrumah-bio.pdf))

The CPP won thirty-five of thirty-eight seats in the 1951 election while Nkrumah was still in prison. He was released on 12 February and invited by the governor, Sir Charles Arden-Clarke, to form a government as Leader of Government Business. In 1952 he became prime minister. The 1954 election gave the CPP seventy-two of 104 seats, with the Northern People's Party the largest opposition group. Opponents then combined as the National Liberation Movement, drawing support from Ashanti and from cocoa farmers after a 1954 bill about cocoa duty and development funds. The CPP won the 1956 election with 397,941 votes against 298,967 for the opposition. Independence followed on 6 March 1957. ([*The Dark Days in Ghana*, 1968, pp. 55 to 56](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1968/dark-days.pdf); [Official Biographical Sketch, 1962](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/nkrumah-bio.pdf))

Nkrumah's own retrospective shows how he understood positive action once in office. Released after the 1951 election, he challenged his opponents by name, including Danquah, Lamptey and the executives of the UGCC and the National Democratic Party, to join a nationwide campaign if the British government rejected a motion for self-government now. He recorded that not one of them answered. The episode mattered to him because it confirmed his view that a section of the nationalist leadership treated mass mobilisation as a ladder to be kicked away once office was reached. Positive action remained for him a permanent technique of popular pressure, not simply a pre-independence tactic. In the conclusion of *Neo-Colonialism* in 1965 he was still arguing that peace could be secured by positive action, provided the peoples concerned acted at once, with resolution and in unity. ([*Dark Days*, pp. 55 to 56](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1968/dark-days.pdf); [*Neo-Colonialism*, Conclusion](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/neo-colonialism/conclusion.htm))

### What independence meant

The sentence Nkrumah used on the day of independence became the standard of his whole project. The official sketch quotes it: the independence of Ghana was meaningless unless it was linked with the total liberation of the whole continent of Africa. He restated the same principle in *Africa Must Unite*, adding that while the celebrations were taking place he called for a conference of all sovereign African states to discuss the future of the continent. In *The Dark Days in Ghana* he repeated it a third time and then explained what it meant in practice. Ghana was a base, not an end in itself. Before February 1966 it had sheltered freedom fighters and political refugees from South Africa, Rhodesia, Mozambique, Angola, Portuguese Guinea and the Cape Verde Islands. It was a place where they could meet, organise and train, and Nkrumah remembered it as a symbol of hope. ([Official Biographical Sketch, 1962](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/nkrumah-bio.pdf); [*Africa Must Unite*, p. 136](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf); [*Dark Days*, p. 132](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1968/dark-days.pdf))

### Pan-Africanism becomes a method

In April 1958 Ghana hosted the first Conference of Independent African States. There were then only eight independent African states: Egypt, Ghana, Sudan, Libya, Tunisia, Liberia, Morocco and Ethiopia. Nkrumah listed the purposes: to exchange views, to consolidate independence, to strengthen economic and cultural ties, to plan help for peoples still under colonial rule and to consider how to secure peace. When he welcomed the delegates on 15 April he said that Pan-Africanism had at last moved to the African continent, and that free Africans were meeting in Africa to examine African affairs. He called it a signal departure from the assumption that African affairs were the concern of states outside the continent. ([*Africa Must Unite*, p. 136](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf))

The second conference of 1958 was the one that links Nkrumah directly to Lumumba. The All-African People's Conference met in Accra in December with delegates from sixty-two African nationalist organisations. Nkrumah recorded that the enthusiasm of those who returned home profoundly influenced subsequent developments, and that the Belgian Congo, Uganda, Tanganyika, Nyasaland, Kenya, the Rhodesias and South Africa were all affected. His point was organisational: recurring continental assemblies produced habits of cooperation, and habits of cooperation could become institutions. Lumumba was in Accra as the Congo's representative, and the 1984 biography says he was elected to the conference's permanent secretariat. Lumumba himself later dated the turning point to that December meeting. Three accounts, two of them movement publications and one a speech by Lumumba, converge on the same event. ([*Africa Must Unite*, p. 137](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf); [1984 biography](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/nkrumah-bio-2.pdf); [Lumumba, 25 August 1960](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/08/25.htm))

## Republic, one-party state and African socialism

In 1960 Nkrumah put the monarchy to a referendum as well as a parliamentary vote, because he considered it incompatible with full independence for Ghana to continue to owe allegiance to the British Crown. The official sketch records that on 1 July 1960 the country became a republic, with more than a million votes for the constitution and 131,425 against, and with Nkrumah elected president by 1,016,076 votes to 124,623. ([Official Biographical Sketch, 1962](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/nkrumah-bio.pdf); [*Africa Must Unite*, p. 79](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf))

A January 1964 referendum made Ghana a one-party state. Nkrumah's defence of this in *The Dark Days in Ghana* was conditional rather than celebratory. He said that visitors from non-socialist countries were often surprised to hear politics discussed freely in Ghana, and that under what he called democratic centralism, the Leninist principle that party members debate an issue freely and then act together once a decision is taken, criticism came from within and was constructive. The 1964 vote, he wrote, merely gave legal recognition to an established fact. He immediately added the qualification: a one-party state could work for the people only within a socialist state or a developing state with a socialist programme, because the government governed through the people rather than through competing class interests. In a neo-colonial client state, by contrast, he warned that the same system could become the most dangerous form of tyranny. ([*Dark Days*, pp. 63 and 66](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1968/dark-days.pdf))

His theoretical case was that newly independent states could not wait for the slow parliamentary evolution of the older countries. "Time is the essence of our problem," he wrote in *Africa Must Unite*, arguing that an overwhelming electoral mandate had to be used to the limit in securing the quickest possible economic and social development. He conceded that the result looked like a one-party regime to outsiders, and called the system a People's Parliamentary Democracy. A Soviet historian, Yuri Smertin, later quoted Nkrumah from *Consciencism* to the effect that a one-party system expressed the common aspirations of a nation better than a multi-party system, which he described as a device for concealing the struggle between the haves and the have-nots. Smertin also reported that Nkrumah made no distinction between party and state functions and repeatedly said that the CPP was Ghana and Ghana was the CPP. ([*Africa Must Unite*, pp. 72 and 78](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf); [Smertin, *Kwame Nkrumah*, 1987, p. 230](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/ussr/nkrumah.pdf))

### What Nkrumah meant by socialism

Nkrumah's most compact statement comes in chapter 14 of *Africa Must Unite*. After independence he and his colleagues listed their urgent priorities: to abolish poverty, ignorance and illiteracy and improve health services. Production for private profit, he argued, deprived many people of the goods and services they produced, so socialism was the only alternative. By socialism he meant public ownership of the means of production, the land and its resources, used to meet people's needs. But he was explicit that the transition would be mixed. There were many roads to socialism, he wrote, and in the circumstances of what he called their retardedness they had to employ all the forces at their disposal. He enumerated state enterprises, foreign-owned enterprises and joint ventures, so that socialism in Ghana meant a state sector within a mixed economy in transition, not the immediate seizure of everything. The CPP, he claimed, was Ghanaian in content and African in outlook while being informed by Marxist socialist philosophy. ([*Africa Must Unite*, pp. 118 to 129](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf))

His philosophical book *Consciencism: Philosophy and Ideology for Decolonization* appeared in 1964. The argument, stripped of its technical vocabulary, was that an ideology for newly independent Africa had to reconcile three inheritances: the traditional societies of the continent, Islam, and the Euro-Christian presence. Nkrumah called the resulting outlook materialist, by which he meant that he explained ideas and institutions by reference to material conditions, while rejecting what he called extreme materialism. He held that matter was in tension and therefore capable of self-motion, and that social change came from the clash of opposing forces. In *African Socialism Revisited* in 1967 he used plain language to reject the romantic claim that traditional African society had been classless. Such a conception, he wrote, made a fetish of communal society, and there was no historical or even anthropological evidence for an idyllic society without rich or poor. What should be recovered was the spirit of communalism, its humanism and its reconciliation of individual advancement with group welfare. His conclusion was that there was one true socialism, which he called scientific socialism, and that to suppose there were tribal, national or racial socialisms was to abandon objectivity for chauvinism. ([Nkrumah, *African Socialism Revisited*, 1967](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1967/african-socialism-revisited.htm))

The position changed further in exile. In *Class Struggle in Africa* in 1970 he wrote that the phrase African socialism was used to deny the class struggle and obscure genuine socialist commitment, and that class cleavages reappeared after independence in those states whose governments embarked on socialist policies. Smertin recorded that in exile Nkrumah came to recognise the existence of classes and class struggle in Africa. This development matters for how the earlier writings should be read. His African socialism was not a fixed ethnic doctrine but a project he revised in the light of what he saw happening after independence. ([Nkrumah, *Class Struggle in Africa*, 1970, quoted in Smertin, p. 260](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/ussr/nkrumah.pdf))

Scholars have argued about the same questions from a different direction. Steven Metz, surveying the socialist theories of Nkrumah and Julius Nyerere in the *Journal of Modern African Studies* in 1982, noted the broad agreement that none of the forms of African socialism was orthodox Marxism, though all were shaped by the intellectual influence of Marx and Lenin. He recorded that one earlier writer, A. Fenner Brockway, placed Nkrumah among the African Marxists, while others classified him differently. A Marxist critic, Jitendra Mohan, argued in 1967 that the CPP had followed a path of neo-colonial accommodation by keeping Western parliamentary institutions and tying Ghana's future development to Western finance capital, with no fundamental structural change in the economy. Matteo Grilli, in a 2019 survey of the historiography in the *Southern Journal for Contemporary History*, divides the writing on Nkrumah into three phases: partisan clashes during his lifetime, more detached but still limited work in the 1970s and 1980s, and a rehabilitation after the early 1990s made possible by the end of the Cold War and the appearance of new sources. Nkrumah's own account of his socialism is therefore one voice in a long argument, not the last word on it. ([Metz, 1982](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-modern-african-studies/article/abs/in-lieu-of-orthodoxy-the-socialist-theories-of-nkrumah-and-nyerere/D6D1E076129B59DD106949D5D0CA1DE9); [Grilli, 2019](https://journals.ufs.ac.za/index.php/jch/article/view/4176))

### The Volta River Project

Nkrumah's economic nationalism is clearest in his account of the Volta River scheme. Modern industry, he wrote in *Africa Must Unite*, required a large and reliable supply of power, and countries determined to catch up had to have abundant electricity. The scheme involved damming the Volta to produce hydroelectric power, using that power to turn Ghana's bauxite into aluminium, and electrifying other industries. He described the colonial administration's inability to finance it, the rejection by an aluminium consortium, President Eisenhower's invitation in 1958, and the eventual decision to build at Akosombo. The financing is the striking part. The main dam was funded by Ghana, Britain, the United States and the International Bank, while an agreement was reached with the Soviet Union for the design and construction of a power dam at Bui. Ghana, Britain, the United States, the World Bank and the Soviet Union were thus partners in one project. That was non-alignment in practice rather than a slogan. ([*Africa Must Unite*, pp. 114 to 115](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf))

Nkrumah drew a continental conclusion. He said Ghana would be willing to share the benefits of the Volta dam with its neighbours within a common economic framework, and compared it with the Inga scheme on the Congo, which he called a blueprint dream that no single new state could afford to build. A united, integrated economic policy would allow the cost of such a project to be spread across a far larger population. A loose confederation for economic cooperation, he argued elsewhere, would not provide the necessary unity of purpose; only a strong political union could develop natural resources for the benefit of the people. ([*Africa Must Unite*, p. 169](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf); [*I Speak of Freedom* excerpt, 1961](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1961/speak-freedom.htm))

## Neo-colonialism and the campaign for Union Government

Nkrumah's best-known theoretical contribution was defined in the opening paragraphs of *Neo-Colonialism, the Last Stage of Imperialism* in 1965. He argued that old-fashioned colonialism was retreating and could not be restored once a territory had become nominally independent. In its place had come something harder to see:

> The essence of neo-colonialism is that the State which is subject to it is, in theory, independent and has all the outward trappings of international sovereignty. In reality its economic system and thus its political policy is directed from outside.
>
> Source: [Nkrumah, *Neo-Colonialism, the Last Stage of Imperialism*, Introduction, 1965](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/neo-colonialism/introduction.htm)

The mechanisms he listed were concrete. An imperial power might garrison the territory with troops, but more often control was exercised through money and trade. A state might be obliged to take the manufactured products of the former colonial power to the exclusion of competitors. Control might be secured by paying the costs of government, by placing civil servants in positions where they could dictate policy, and by controlling foreign exchange through a banking system owned or directed from outside. He named the Congo explicitly: the control of the Congo by international financial concerns was a case in point. He insisted that the struggle against neo-colonialism did not mean excluding foreign capital altogether, but preventing the financial power of rich countries from being used to impoverish poorer ones. ([*Neo-Colonialism*, Introduction](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/neo-colonialism/introduction.htm))

The structural remedy was continental union. Nkrumah argued that former large colonial territories had been broken into small states that could not develop independently and relied on the former imperial power for defence and even internal security. Foreign firms had long understood the advantage of operating on a Pan-African scale, using interlocking directorships and cross-shareholdings to form what were in effect single monopolies. The only effective answer was for African states to act on the same scale, through a Union Government. The book's chapters on the Congo Republic and on the Union Minière du Haut-Katanga show that this was not an aside: the Congo was his case study. ([*Neo-Colonialism*, Introduction and Conclusion](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/neo-colonialism/conclusion.htm))

In a speech to the National Assembly on 8 August 1960 he reduced the choice to three alternatives: unite and save the continent, disunite and disintegrate, or sell out. He warned that the retreat of colonialism was worthless unless African peoples moved into the positions the colonialists had vacated, because a power vacuum was the greatest danger. At the founding of the Organisation of African Unity in Addis Ababa in May 1963 he argued that independence was only the prelude to a harder struggle for control of economic and social affairs. He proposed a political union based on defence, foreign affairs and diplomacy, common citizenship, an African currency, a monetary zone and a central bank, together with commissions to draft a union constitution, plan a common market and coordinate foreign policy and defence. The OAU that was founded did not adopt the Union Government, and this disappointment runs through his later work. ([*Africa's Challenge*, 1960](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1960/africas-challenge.pdf); [OAU speech, May 1963](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/oau-speech.pdf))

## The Congo as the test of African unity

Nkrumah placed the Congo inside the Pan-African story rather than treating it as a separate foreign policy problem. In *Africa Must Unite* he described how, while the 1958 Accra conference was taking place, Katanga was attempting to secede and Lumumba had asked for United Nations aid. Some of the dangers of neo-colonialism and what he called balkanisation had become realities. Foreign business interests and policies connected with the Cold War, he wrote, dominated the Congo political scene and prevented early UN action that might have preserved Lumumba's government's sovereignty. Had the independent African states been united in July 1960, with a joint military command and a common foreign policy, an African solution might have been found. Instead the situation worsened, and the neo-colonialists and their allies in South Africa and the Rhodesias used the crisis as an argument that Africans could not manage their own affairs. ([*Africa Must Unite*, pp. 138 to 139](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf))

He recorded the August 1960 Leopoldville conference as the attempted answer, and he stated what he took to be Lumumba's own position. In a world divided into hostile camps, Nkrumah wrote, Africa could not stand divided. Lumumba, he said, had seen and suffered from disunity in the Congo and had agreed in Accra to work for the closest association with other independent African states towards a Union of African States. This is Nkrumah's account of Lumumba's commitment, written three years after the events, and it should be read as his interpretation rather than as a neutral record of a conversation. ([*Africa Must Unite*, p. 148](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1963/africa-must-unite.pdf))

## The 1966 coup, exile and death

Nkrumah wrote *The Dark Days in Ghana* in Conakry, completing the author's note on 24 August 1967. He rejected the word coup for what happened on 24 February 1966. Ghana, he wrote, was captured by traitors in the army and police inspired and helped by neo-colonialists and reactionary elements at home, in an act of aggression planned for his absence. He was on his way to Hanoi at the invitation of Ho Chi Minh with proposals for ending the war in Vietnam, and had almost reached Beijing. He recalled the exact words of the official who told him: "Mr. President. I have bad news. There has been a coup d'etat in Ghana." When he said it was impossible, the official replied that these things happened, that they were in the nature of the revolutionary struggle. ([*Dark Days*, pp. 9 to 10](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1968/dark-days.pdf))

His reconstruction of the operation is detailed. He left Accra on 21 February, seen off by officials and service chiefs whom he later accused of treachery. On 23 February the Kumasi garrison was ordered south, and the convoy was halted by Colonels Kotoka and Afrifa. The nominal leader, Major-General Ankrah, was chosen, Nkrumah wrote, because the conspirators thought so little of his abilities that they had not troubled to consult him. The troops were told that Nkrumah intended to send them to fight in Vietnam and Rhodesia, that he had stolen eight million pounds, and that Russian planes were landing at a secret airstrip in northern Ghana. Nkrumah cited a later television appearance in which Kotoka and Afrifa joked that they had found no Russians and no tunnel, which he took as an admission that the story had been invented. He described the killing of Major-General Barwah at close range in front of his family and the resistance at Flagstaff House, where fewer than a hundred defenders fought some six hundred rebel troops until the rebels threatened to blow up the residence where his wife and children were sheltering. These are Nkrumah's charges, set out in his own book, and they remain his account of the event rather than an independent finding. ([*Dark Days*, pp. 20 to 25](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1968/dark-days.pdf))

The movement response appeared in *Labour Monthly*. The underground Committee for the Defence of the Revolution in Ghana called what had happened a counter-revolution and named the forces it held responsible: tribalism, personal vendetta, foreign religious missions, foreign business firms and traitors in the CPP leadership. It listed the policies it said the new National Liberation Council would follow, including a free hand for foreign investors, cuts in government spending and the removal of socialist technicians, and it offered a self-criticism, admitting that the top men in the party had paid lip service to Nkrumah while accumulating wealth and working to destroy the party. Thomas Hodgkin, in the same journal, called the coup a counter-revolution of a classic type, while noting that Ankrah was no Louis Napoleon. Other African leaders sent messages of protest. Modibo Keita of Mali called the authors of the coup guilty of high treason, and Sékou Touré of Guinea organised a day of solidarity. Students in the Soviet Union described the coup as a blow against the peoples of Africa as a whole. These statements document how the event was understood within the movement and among its allies; they are not a substitute for an independent history of the coup. ([Committee declaration, *Labour Monthly*, May 1966](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/cpgb/ghana-committee.pdf); [Hodgkin, April 1966](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/cpgb/counter-revolution-ghana.pdf))

In Conakry, Nkrumah rejected the image of isolation. He wrote that Sékou Touré, the Guinea Democratic Party and the people of Guinea had made him feel at home, and that he had been able to read, study and write. He claimed an efficient communications system that kept him in touch with events inside Ghana. From exile he published *African Socialism Revisited*, *The Dark Days in Ghana*, the *Handbook of Revolutionary Warfare* and *Class Struggle in Africa*. The conclusion of *Dark Days* drew two lessons: that independent African states had to pursue all-out socialism if they were to survive, and that the struggle for continental liberation and an All-African Union Government had to enter a phase of planned, centrally directed armed struggle. He was awarded the Lenin Peace Prize for 1961, announced in April 1962. The 1984 biography records that he died in Bucharest on 27 April 1972 and was buried at Nkroful on 9 July. ([*Dark Days*, pp. 147 to 159](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1968/dark-days.pdf); [1984 biography](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/nkrumah-bio-2.pdf))

## Patrice Lumumba: self-education and the Congolese National Movement

The Marxists Internet Archive preserves Lumumba's speeches, letters and statements, poems, reminiscences by movement writers and a biography, mostly transcribed from two collections published in Moscow in 1961: *The Truth about a Monstrous Crime of the Colonialists* and *Patrice Lumumba: Fighter for Africa's Freedom*. His closing statement at the Belgo-Congolese Round Table Conference comes from the official conference proceedings published in Brussels in 1960. The basic facts of his death come from the 1984 Progress Publishers biography. These are the sources through which Lumumba speaks, and they are movement publications for the most part. ([Lumumba Archive index](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/index.htm))

The 1984 biography says Lumumba was born on 2 July 1925 at Onalua in Kasai Province into a poor Tetela peasant family. Educated at a Catholic mission school, he worked as a clerk, a postal official and an employee of Belgian companies. Higher education was not available in the colonial Congo, so he relied on self-education. He moved to Kindu in 1943 and then to Kalima in Kivu, where he worked as a clerk for a tin-mining company and became acquainted with the life of Congolese workers. He read widely, wrote summaries of Aristotle and the French Enlightenment thinkers, and published his first poems in the local press at eighteen. He founded and led the Congolese National Movement, known by its French initials MNC, in 1958, and edited the newspapers *Uhuru* and *Indépendance*. ([*Patrice Lumumba*, in *Fighters for National Liberation*, 1984, pp. 135 to 136](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/lumumba-bio.pdf))

The same biography records an early hope for a shared future. In a 1957 text Lumumba wrote that his dearest wish, which some might call utopian, was to found in the Congo a nation in which differences of race and religion would melt away, a homogeneous society of Belgians and Congolese joined by a single attachment to the country's destiny. The wish is worth remembering because his later speeches are often read only as denunciations. He began as someone who wanted a multiracial community and moved toward armed anti-colonial politics as the colonial administration resisted. According to the 1984 account, the MNC was to mobilise people not only against the colonial regime but against exploitation of man by man. The movement writer N. Khokhlov, in a 1961 reminiscence, adds that Lumumba founded *Uhuru* in Stanleyville and directed *Indépendance*, and dates the MNC's public declaration to October 1959. The 1984 biography dates the founding of the party itself to 1958. The two statements are compatible: the party was founded in 1958 and its declaration was published in 1959. ([1984 biography, pp. 136 to 140](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/lumumba-bio.pdf); [Khokhlov, 1961](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/reminiscences/khokhlov/goal.htm))

The 1984 biography describes the MNC's first programme, written by Lumumba, as a pledge to use every means to liberate the Congo from imperialist colonialism. Its statutes obliged members to work for the unity and indivisibility of the country, to carry out the decisions of the party's governing bodies and to expose abuses by colonialists. Lumumba represented the Congo at the Accra conference of African peoples at the end of 1958 and was elected to its permanent secretariat. Khokhlov gives the party's slogan as "Independence Now!" and describes the October 1959 declaration as the organisational culmination of the work of building a single party out of the progressive forces in the liberation movement. ([1984 biography, p. 139](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/lumumba-bio.pdf))

## The Round Table and independence

At the closing session of the Belgo-Congolese Round Table Conference in Brussels on 20 February 1960, Lumumba spoke in the name of the MNC. He said that the demanded immediate and unconditional independence and had won it, and that Belgium had assured the conference that it would retain no control after 30 June. He described the delegates returning home with their independence in their baggage. But he insisted that independence was only the first stage. The four tasks he set out were to create confidence and calm, to eradicate every trace of colonialism and racial discrimination, to end oppressive measures against local people, and to consolidate independence by building a stable national economy. "Our independence will have no significance unless it contributes to the improvement of living standards of the worker and peasant classes," he said. ([Statement at the Closing Session of the Round Table Conference, 20 February 1960](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/02/statement.htm))

He also stated the territorial principle that later became the issue in Katanga: the Congo was to be fought for as a single political and economic entity, and any attempt to dislocate the national territory would be opposed. On outside help he said that the doors of the Congo were open to all people of good will, but that the country would not tolerate anyone with imperialist aims. "We prefer liberty with poverty to wealth with tyranny," he said. He invited Belgian technicians to come and serve the Congolese state. This was the position he maintained throughout the crisis: assistance was welcome, control was not. ([Round Table statement, 20 February 1960](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/02/statement.htm))

### The speech of 30 June 1960

The independence speech was delivered at the ceremony proclaiming the Republic of the Congo on 30 June 1960. It is preserved in the translation published in the 1961 Moscow collection. The speech begins by saluting the independence fighters and asking the audience to mark the date and pass its meaning to their children and grandchildren. It then refuses the idea that Belgium had simply granted freedom.

> Although this independence of the Congo is being proclaimed today by agreement with Belgium, an amicable country, with which we are on equal terms, no Congolese will ever forget that independence was won in struggle, a persevering and inspired struggle carried on from day to day, a struggle, in which we were undaunted by privation or suffering and stinted neither strength nor blood.
>
> Source: [Lumumba, "Speech at the Ceremony of the Proclamation of the Congo's Independence," 30 June 1960, in *The Truth about a Monstrous Crime of the Colonialists*, 1961, pp. 44 to 47](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/06/independence.htm)

What follows is a catalogue of the daily humiliations of colonial rule, and it is here that the speech gives voice to ordinary Congolese rather than to the leader alone. Lumumba recalled forced labour paid at a rate that could not satisfy hunger, clothe a family or provide decent lodging. People were jeered, insulted and struck because they were Black, and the polite form of address was reserved for white people. Land was seized under laws that recognised the right of might. The law was lenient for one group and cruel for another. People were persecuted and exiled for their political or religious convictions. In the towns, he said, the mansions were for whites and the tumbledown huts for Black people; Black people were excluded from cinemas, restaurants and shops reserved for Europeans, and travelled in the holds beneath the luxury cabins. He spoke of shootings, of cells into which those who would no longer submit were thrown, and of wounds too fresh to be forgotten. These are the words of a political leader addressing a crowd on the day his country became independent, and they are a record of what he wanted that crowd to remember. ([30 June 1960 speech](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/06/independence.htm))

The speech then turns from indictment to programme. The new Republic would establish social justice and a fair remuneration for labour, ensure that the land benefited its own children, revise unjust laws, stop the persecution of free thought and guarantee the freedoms in the Declaration of Human Rights. Peace would rest on concord rather than on guns. Foreign assistance would be accepted only when it was not aimed at imposing an alien policy. Lumumba asked citizens to sink their tribal quarrels and respect the lives and property of others. The closing section moves from national to continental: the Congo's independence was a decisive step towards the liberation of the whole African continent, and the speech ended with calls for independence and African unity. An alternative translation of the speech is preserved in the same archive; the differences are translational rather than substantive. ([30 June 1960 speech](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/06/independence.htm); [alternative translation](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/06/independence1.htm))

## The Congo crisis in Lumumba's documents

The documents preserved in the Lumumba archive are the speeches, letters and declarations of the Congolese government and of the movement that defended it. When a claim about Belgian or American or United Nations conduct appears in them, it is a claim made by Lumumba or his government, not an established finding.

In a letter of 1 August 1960 to the president of the Security Council, Lumumba's government charged that United Nations troops had been arriving since 16 July but that not a single Belgian soldier had left Congolese soil. The letter called this a deliberate refusal. A telegram of the same date named what the government saw as the mechanism behind the secession of Katanga: it said that the Belgian government was using Moïse Tshombe as its instrument to prevent UN troops from entering Katanga, and that all of Tshombe's actions were dictated by Belgian officers placed beside him as advisers. Those are allegations made to the UN by the Congolese government. In his opening speech at the All-African Conference in Leopoldville on 25 August, Lumumba made the economic version of the same argument. Katanga, with its uranium, copper and gold, and Bakwanga in Kasai, with its diamonds, had become, in his words, hotbeds of imperialist intrigue whose object was to recover economic control of the country. ([Letter to the President of the Security Council, 1 August 1960](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/08/pressec1.htm); [Telegram, 1 August 1960](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/08/pressec2.htm); [25 August 1960 speech](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/08/25.htm))

The Leopoldville conference, held from 25 to 31 August, was the occasion for Lumumba's fullest statement of continental politics. He described the crisis as a continuation of the struggle between the forces of pressure and the forces of liberation, said that his government had appealed to the United Nations at the outset of what he called Belgian aggression, and thanked the heads of state who had supported the Congo by name, including Nkrumah, Sékou Touré and Mohammed V. He argued that if the Congo perished, the whole of Africa would be plunged into defeat and bondage. His eight-point agenda called for unqualified support for a Pan-African bloc, a policy of neutralism, military cooperation and trade and cultural measures. His conclusion was a direct statement of what he called positive neutralism, meaning an active refusal to join either Cold War bloc. Africa was neither French, nor British, nor American, nor Russian, he said, but African. The great powers had once divided Africans by tribe, clan and village and now sought to divide them by states, creating antagonistic blocs and satellites in order to deepen the Cold War division. Positive neutralism was, in his words, the only policy that allowed Africans to preserve their dignity. ([Opening speech, 25 August 1960](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/08/25.htm); [closing speech, 31 August 1960](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/08/31.htm))

### Nationalist, not communist

Lumumba rejected the communist label in the terms preserved by the movement collections. "We are neither Communists, Catholics nor socialists. We are African nationalists. We reserve the right to choose our friends in accordance with the principle of positive neutrality," he is quoted as saying in Khokhlov's 1961 reminiscence. In another reminiscence, Romano Ledda recorded him as saying that he was not a communist although he was accused of being one, that America was on the side of the colonialists, and that in using the United Nations she had her eye on the Congo's riches. Ledda also related that Lumumba asked the Americans for a plane and waited two days without result, then asked the Russians and was given one within two hours. These are self-descriptions preserved by the movement press. They do not prove that Lumumba never worked with Soviet advisers, which he did. They show what he claimed for himself: an independent nationalism that chose friends by whether they actually helped the Congo. ([Khokhlov, 1961](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/reminiscences/khokhlov/goal.htm); [Ledda, 1961](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/reminiscences/ledda/meetings.htm))

### Dismissal, flight and imprisonment

On 5 September 1960, after President Joseph Kasavubu announced on the radio that the government was dismissed, Lumumba broadcast his rejection. He said the government had held no talks with the head of state, that it had been democratically elected and enjoyed the confidence of Parliament, and that it could only be dismissed if it lost the trust of the people, which it had not. He said the government would defend the rights of the people with honour and dignity and would continue its mission. In a solemn appeal to the UN Security Council on 10 September he argued that the crisis made the Congo's complete break-up imminent and that a regime of anarchy and dictatorship had replaced the democratic regime established on 30 June. ([Radio broadcast, 5 September 1960](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/09/radio.htm); [Solemn Appeal, 10 September 1960](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1960/09/appeal.htm))

The last weeks of his freedom are reconstructed in a movement account by Lev Volodin, written in 1961. It records Lumumba telling a gathering to put away their weapons but look after them, because the colonialists would not give up freedom peacefully. It records his appeal at Mweka on 1 December to a United Nations lieutenant for protection, and the lieutenant's silence as he crushed his cigarette and went into the house. Volodin ends with his own judgment: that whenever people said the UN could do nothing, he remembered that lieutenant's face and the boot crushing the cigarette. This is a movement witness writing shortly after the events. His account establishes how the UN's failure looked to those who sought its protection, but it is a single testimony, written for a movement collection, and it does not by itself establish what the UN command in the Congo could or could not have done. ([Volodin, "Last Days of Freedom," 1961](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/reminiscences/volodin/last.htm))

Lumumba's own letters from detention are the most immediate testimony of all, and they describe the experience of imprisonment in the words of the prisoner. On 4 January 1961 he wrote secretly to A. M. Dayal, the UN Secretary-General's representative. He said that he was held with seven other parliamentarians, the president of the Senate, a Senate employee and a driver, ten in all. They had been locked in damp cells since 2 December and had never been allowed out. The meals brought twice a day were very bad, and for three or four days he had eaten nothing but a banana. His clothes had not been washed for thirty-five days and he was forbidden to wear shoes. He said the criminal code and the prison rules were ignored, that theirs was a case of arbitrary imprisonment and that they had parliamentary immunity. He closed by saying that he remained calm, hoped the United Nations would help, and stood for reconciliation among all the children of the country. He added that he was writing on bad paper. ([Letter to A. M. Dayal, 4 January 1961](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1961/01/letter.htm))

### The last letter and the killing

The last letter, from Thysville prison to his wife, was written in January 1961 without knowing whether it would reach her or whether he would be alive when she read it. It moves from private uncertainty to a public instruction about memory. Lumumba wrote that he had never doubted the victory of their cause, that the only thing they had wanted for their country was the right to a worthy life, to dignity without pretence and to independence without restrictions, and that those who wanted something else were the Belgian colonialists and their Western allies, supported, he said, by highly placed officials of the United Nations. He wrote that his faith remained indestructible and that Africa, Asia and the free peoples would stand beside the Congolese. The passage that later became most quoted concerns history:

> The day will come when history will speak. But it will not be the history which will be taught in Brussels, Paris, Washington or the United Nations. It will be the history which will be taught in the countries which have won freedom from colonialism and its puppets.
>
> Africa will write its own history and in both north and south it will be a history of glory and dignity.
>
> Source: [Letter from Thysville Prison to Mrs. Lumumba, January 1961, in *The Truth about a Monstrous Crime of the Colonialists*, 1961, pp. 230 to 231](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1961/xx/letter.htm)

He ended by telling his wife not to weep for him and by entrusting to their sons the task of restoring the country's independence and sovereignty. The claim about Africa writing its own history is an instruction about which archives are legitimate, and its force comes from the circumstances in which it was written. ([Thysville letter](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/1961/xx/letter.htm))

The 1984 movement biography states the death in summary form and then gives the sequence. On 17 January, it says, secret police put Lumumba and two associates, Mpolo and Okito, on a plane bound for Elisabethville in Katanga. During the flight they were beaten, and on arrival they were taken to a farm and shot in the evening. This is a movement secondary account written more than two decades after the events. A Congolese journalist, Jean Bulabemba, wrote in the 1961 collection that those who considered freedom their exclusive prerogative had murdered Lumumba in an effort to strangle Congolese nationalism. Yuri Zhukov, in the same collection, described the international protest and concluded that even after death Lumumba remained in the ranks of his people. ([1984 biography](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/lumumba-bio.pdf); [Bulabemba, 1961](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/reminiscences/bulabemba/life.htm); [Zhukov, 1961](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/lumumba/reminiscences/zhukov/suchwas.htm))

## Who killed Lumumba: the scholarly dispute

The movement record gives a clear answer to the question of responsibility. Historians have given several different ones. In 2001 a Belgian parliamentary commission concluded that certain members of the Belgian government and other Belgian participants were morally responsible for the circumstances leading to Lumumba's death. Edouard Bustin, reviewing the affair in the *Review of African Political Economy* in 2002, judged that the commission could not be dismissed as a whitewash, while noting that witnesses were rarely challenged and that Cold War stereotypes survived in the reactions to its report. ([Bustin, 2002](https://web.archive.org/web/20241013101324/https://www.scienceopen.com/hosted-document?doi=10.1080/03056240208704637))

Ludo De Witte, a Belgian sociologist, published *The Assassination of Lumumba* in Dutch in 1999 and in English in 2001; it was reprinted by Verso in 2022. De Witte assigns most of the blame to Belgium, writing that it was Belgian advice, Belgian orders and finally Belgian hands that killed Lumumba on 17 January 1961, and that at the root of it lay the desire of colonial holding companies for profit. He also implicates the United States, the CIA, the United Nations and the British. Matthew Stanard, reviewing the reissue for H-Empire in March 2023, praised the book for forcing the question of Belgian responsibility onto the public record, but noted criticisms of its stark division between nationalists and anti-nationalists and of its tendency to treat Belgium, the United States and the UN as monolithic actors with more power in Central Africa than they actually had. Emmanuel Gerard and Bruce Kuklick, in *Death in the Congo: Murdering Patrice Lumumba* (2015), take a wider and more guarded view. They give more weight to African agency, treat the Belgian minister's cable calling for Lumumba's definitive elimination as less conclusive than De Witte does, and compare the number of people involved to the multiple stab wounds in Agatha Christie's *Murder on the Orient Express*. ([Stanard, 2023](https://networks.h-net.org/node/5293/reviews/12570531/stanard-de-witte-assassination-lumumba))

The American role has a separate documentary history. The United States Senate's Church Committee reported in 1975 that it had received solid evidence of a plot to assassinate Lumumba, that the CIA had delivered lethal substances to its station in the Congo, and that a station officer had been instructed to carry out the assassination. The same report stated that there was no evidence the substances were actually used, and no suggestion of a connection between the plot and the events that led to Lumumba's death. The plot and the killing are therefore two distinct things: one is established by American congressional investigation, the other remains disputed in its authorship. What is not in doubt is that Lumumba's own letters accused the United Nations and Western governments, that a Belgian parliamentary commission later found Belgian moral responsibility, and that the immediate killing took place in Katanga after his transfer there. ([Church Committee Interim Report, 1975](https://www.aarclibrary.org/publib/church/reports/ir/pdf/ChurchIR_3A_Congo.pdf))

## What the two lives share

Nkrumah and Lumumba reached similar conclusions from different positions. Both treated national independence as a stage in a continental process rather than an end in itself. Both held that political freedom was empty without control of resources and an improvement in the lives of workers and peasants. Both called for a disciplined mass party and for non-alignment as an active doctrine rather than a refusal to choose. Both put their hopes in the organisation of ordinary people: Nkrumah in the mass mobilisation of the CPP and the repeated continental conferences, Lumumba in the MNC and the Pan-African bloc. And both addressed the question of how they would be remembered in their last surviving texts. Nkrumah wrote from Conakry after the loss of power, Lumumba from a prison cell weeks before his death.

The difference is one of historical position. Nkrumah had fifteen years in office, from his appointment as Leader of Government Business in 1951 to the 1966 coup, nine of them as head of an independent government, and then interpreted their reversal from exile ([Official Biographical Sketch, 1962](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/nkrumah-bio.pdf); [*The Dark Days in Ghana*, 1968](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/nkrumah/1968/dark-days.pdf)). Lumumba had seven months of government and a few weeks of imprisonment before his death. Nkrumah left a large body of theoretical writing, much of it revised in the light of events, while Lumumba's surviving words are speeches, letters and a poem. Nkrumah's neo-colonialism thesis read the Congolese crisis as its central proof; Lumumba's Pan-Africanism was the lived version of the unity Nkrumah argued for at Addis Ababa. Their deaths marked the end of a particular moment in African politics. The argument they began, about what independence should mean and who would control the continent's resources, continued long after them in the work of the historians who have contested and revised their accounts.
