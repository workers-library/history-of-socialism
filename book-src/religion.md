On 2 November 1850 the first issue of a London periodical put a claim on the table that neither clergy nor radicals found comfortable. *The Christian Socialist*, edited by the barrister John Malcolm Forbes Ludlow and shaped by the clergyman Frederick Denison Maurice and Charles Kingsley, argued that Christianity and socialism were allies rather than enemies. Socialism was "the livery of the nineteenth century", the journal said, as Protestantism had been the livery of the sixteenth. The editors backed the claim with institutions: cooperative workshops and a college for working men. The opening number went further, insisting that "even the strangest and most monstrous forms of Socialism are at bottom but Christian heresies." So wrote the first issue of *The Christian Socialist*, 2 November 1850, as transcribed at [Source: spartacus-educational.com](https://spartacus-educational.com/REsocialism.htm)

## The starting point

Whatever one makes of that claim, the argument had to begin with a text written a few years earlier in Paris. In an introduction composed in December 1843 and January 1844 and first published on 7 and 10 February 1844, Karl Marx set down the line that would shadow every later discussion: "Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the soul of soulless conditions. It is the opium of the people." (Karl Marx, introduction written December 1843 – January 1844, first published 7 and 10 February 1844. [Marxists Internet Archive: intro](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1843/critique-hpr/intro.htm)) Marx's point was not simply that religion was an illusion. He argued that the criticism of heaven turns into the criticism of earth, and that the state and society which need religious consolation must themselves be examined. His own chapter in this book sets out the reasoning in full. What concerns us here is what other socialists did with the relation between faith and politics.

The Christian Socialists took the opposite course. They did not wait for religion to wither; they tried to build a just order inside it. That programme was contested from within socialism almost at once. In January 1884 the British Marxist E. Belfort Bax attacked Kingsley and Maurice in a polemic called "Unscientific Socialism": "It was undoubtedly some scheme of co-operation, we may observe, that the 'old', 'original' Christian Socialists in this country, Kingsley and Maurice, had in view." (E. Belfort Bax, "Unscientific Socialism", January 1884. [Marxists Internet Archive: unscisoc](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bax/1884/01/unscisoc.htm)) Bax argued that their practical programme was trade cooperation, or industrial partnership, and so stayed within the ideas of the bourgeois system he wanted to replace. He called their guild a red-herring and dismissed their ethics. His account is useful as evidence of a quarrel, not as a neutral description: he wrote to discredit a rival, and he expounded a prejudice about "Semitic ethics" that must be rejected rather than repeated. One further caution: Kingsley later described the Bible as an "opium-dose for keeping beasts of burden patient"; the two phrases should not be merged. Marx published his line earlier.

## Faith as a resource

The Victorian argument was one version of a wider attempt to use religious tradition for egalitarian ends. At Medellín in 1968, 145 Latin American bishops declared that "Peace is, above all, a work of justice. It presupposes and requires the establishment of a just order in which men can fulfill themselves as men, where their dignity is respected." (Latin American bishops, Medellín, 6 September 1968, English text at [Source: we-are-church.org](https://www.we-are-church.org/123/index.php/library/caring-not-scaring/264-medellin-conference-statement-1968)) They described underdevelopment as an unjust situation that bred tension, named institutionalised violence, and instructed pastors to defend the poor and to encourage grassroots organisation. They warned that those who held privilege by force would be answerable for "explosive revolutions of despair". The phrase that came to summarise this shift, the "preferential option for the poor", the choice to put poor people first, was still being formed. The Peruvian theologian Gustavo Gutiérrez recalled in 2003: "The precise term was born sometime between the Latin American bishops' conferences in Medellín (1968) and in Puebla (1979)." (Gustavo Gutiérrez, interview, 3 February 2003. [Source: americamagazine.org](https://www.americamagazine.org/faith/2003/02/03/remembering-poor-interview-gustavo-gutierrez)) He meant material poverty and what he called premature and unjust death, and he insisted the option was not optional. His memory, recorded more than thirty years later, is a participant's account of a word's origin, not a transcript of how small grassroots church groups lived it.

A similar claim, made from inside another tradition, came from S. Mushir Hosain Kidwai, a barrister and former secretary of the Pan-Islamic Society of London. In a book published in 1912 he argued that socialism was native to Islam and older than its European form: "The key-note of Muhammad's Socialism was the spiritual and moral advancement of the people. His Socialism was ethical, while modern Socialism is materialistic." (Mushir Hosain Kidwai, *Islam and Socialism*, 1912. [Internet Archive: islamsocialism00mushuoft djvu](https://archive.org/stream/islamsocialism00mushuoft/islamsocialism00mushuoft_djvu.txt)) Kidwai grounded his case in zakat, the religious duty of almsgiving, in the ban on usury or lending at interest, in state control of land and in the status of slaves. He criticised European socialists for supposing that transferring land and capital to the state was enough. His reading of early Islamic history is a believer's reconstruction, the text survives in an OCR scan with errors, and he wrote as one male author about "Muslim society", not as a voice from a workshop or a village.

The Jewish Bund shows the edge of this pattern. It was a secular working-class organisation, often at odds with religious Jewish institutions, and its 1907 electoral manifesto attacked Jewish bourgeois parties while declaring: "The Bund is the party of the working class. It is the party of the most oppressed of all the oppressed, the most irreconcilable, and the most determined of all, who are now carrying on the struggle." (Electoral manifesto of the Jewish Bund, *Justice*, 12 October 1907. [Marxists Internet Archive: electoral manifesto](https://www.marxists.org/subject/jewish/boris/electoral-manifesto.htm)) A sympathetic report by K. Boris two weeks later recorded strikes across the Jewish towns: "A storm of strikes and boycotts swept over the whole of the industrial area of the Jewish towns and even villages." (K. Boris, *Justice*, 26 October and 2 November 1907. [Marxists Internet Archive: bund](https://www.marxists.org/subject/jewish/boris/bund.htm)) Boris said organisers built unions "in the teeth of the Rabbis of the synagogues". The Bund belongs here as a case of Jewish working-class socialism that defined itself against religious authority, not as an example of religious socialism, and both sources are movement publicity written for a sympathetic audience.

## The total state

For Confessing Church pastors in Germany the question was different. It was not whether faith could carry a socialist order but how the church should answer a state that claimed to be a single and totalitarian order of human life. At a synod, a meeting of church representatives, held on 29 to 31 May 1934, the Theological Declaration of Barmen rejected those who wanted to align the church with the Nazi state: "We reject the false doctrine, as though the state, over and beyond its special commission, should and could become the single and totalitarian order of human life, thus fulfilling the church's vocation as well." (Theological Declaration of Barmen, 29–31 May 1934, EKD English translation. [Source: ekd.de](https://www.ekd.de/en/The-Barmen-Declaration-303.htm)) The declaration allowed that the state had a task in justice and peace; it refused to let the state absorb the church's vocation, and it equally refused a church that became an organ of the state. It was a statement by church leaders, contested inside Germany, and it exists in more than one English translation.

A decade later, writing from Tegel prison in 1944, Dietrich Bonhoeffer judged his own church from the inside: "We can be Christians today in only two ways: by praying and by practicing righteousness among our fellow men." (Dietrich Bonhoeffer, prison letter, 1944, USHMM English translation. [Source: perspectives.ushmm.org](https://perspectives.ushmm.org/item/letter-written-by-dietrich-bonhoeffer-in-prison)) He expected a church "melted down and recast" and spoke of a "quite nonreligious language". The letter was a private pastoral note for a godson's baptism, guarded because of prison censorship. It does not name the regime or the genocide, the museum notes his own troubling beliefs about Jews, and some words are restored from a published edition. It records a participant's indictment of institutional failure, not a political programme.

## Atheism as policy

In the Soviet Union the surviving record is overwhelmingly of the state acting against religion. A top-secret memorandum of 4 December 1920 by T. P. Samsonov, head of the secret department of the Cheka, argued that "no other apparatus is capable of destroying religion apart from the apparatus of the VChK." (T. P. Samsonov, secret memorandum, 4 December 1920, translated in Corley (ed.), *Religion in the Soviet Union*. [Source: soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/antireligious-propaganda/antireligious-propaganda-texts/on-the-need-to-destroy-religion-by-force/)) The Cheka was the secret police; VChK is its Russian initials. Samsonov proposed working through the lower clergy to break the church from within and suggested the method might be extended to Islam. On that paragraph F. E. Dzerzhinskii wrote in pencil: "This is dangerous and we should not undertake it." (F. E. Dzerzhinskii's pencilled annotation on Samsonov's memorandum, 4 December 1920, as reproduced in the same translated document. [Source: soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/antireligious-propaganda/antireligious-propaganda-texts/on-the-need-to-destroy-religion-by-force/)) The document therefore records both a plan and an objection to part of it, though it shows intent, not achievement.

Three years later Iakov Peters of the GPU, the state security police, reported on Islam: "Our task is to follow closely (and if possible to direct) these potential contacts which could be of great interest to us." (Iakov Peters, GPU report, 22 September 1923, translated in Corley (ed.), *Religion in the Soviet Union*. [Source: soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/antireligious-propaganda/antireligious-propaganda-texts/soviet-policy-toward-islam/)) He described steering a congress of Muslim clergy and using favourable resolutions abroad. This was an intelligence officer's account of what his agency wanted and claimed, not a report of what believers thought.

The party's own instructions admit how slow the work was. A circular of 15 September 1921 from the Bolshevik central committee explained that women remained more religious than men: "The principal reason for this is rooted in women's political backwardness. Women are less involved in the work of the Party, of the unions, of soviet establishments." (RKP(b) Central Committee circular, 15 September 1921, translated in Rosenberg (ed.), *Bolshevik Visions*. [Source: soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/antireligious-propaganda/antireligious-propaganda-texts/anti-religious-propaganda-among-women/)) It advised activists to refrain from a stand against religion without serious preparation. That is an outside diagnosis dressed as an explanation, and it tells us activists did not expect quick success. Meanwhile a Soviet newspaper reported that at Petropavlovsk more than six hundred railway workers had voted to close their church, with fifteen against; the building became a Komsomol club, the Communist Youth League's hall, the cross was replaced by a red flag and the bells handed over for airship construction. "The workmen saw no use in keeping a church which is not visited by anybody except a few old women." (*Moskovskaia pravda*, 21 September 1923, translated in Szczesniak (ed. and tr.), *Russian Revolution and Religion*. [Source: soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/antireligious-propaganda/antireligious-propaganda-texts/end-of-a-church/)) The "few toothless women" who wept were figures in the reporter's story, not witnesses. The legal machinery followed in 1929, when a decree required every religious association to register, denied religious bodies the rights of a person at law, barred them from charity and from youth and women's meetings, and forbade religious teaching in any school: "No teaching of religious faith of any sort shall be tolerated in state or private schools or other educational establishments." (RSFSR Law on Religious Organizations, 8 April 1929, translated in Matthews (ed.), *Soviet Government*. [Source: soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1929/churches-closed/churches-closed-texts/law-on-religious-organizations)) A law on paper does not show how it was enforced, and the decree left surveillance to local soviets.

## Limits of the record

The documents gathered here are programmes, declarations and administrative acts, and they leave the believer mostly silent. Bishops, theologians, party officials, police officers and movement publicists speak; peasants, parishioners and members of the small grassroots church groups known as base communities rarely do. Several texts reach us in translations of uncertain provenance. Barmen exists in at least two English versions, the Medellín host page names no translator, and the Soviet items are published translations of Russian originals. Kidwai's book survives as an OCR scan with visible errors, and Bonhoeffer's letter has gaps filled from a published edition. A Lenin letter of 19 March 1922 on removing church valuables, described in a Library of Congress exhibition, was not available for this account. Islamic socialism appears through one male author's book rather than through women or the ulama who opposed him; Jewish religious socialism and the "Death of God" theologians are absent; no first-hand testimony from a base community was located. What is needed, and what this record cannot supply, is a check of the Spanish CELAM and Puebla texts, archive work on the Soviet police files, critical editions of Bonhoeffer's letters, oral histories in Yiddish and Spanish, and sources in Arabic, Urdu and Indonesian. None of these documents supports a reconstruction of private motives or of scenes no one recorded.

## How historians have read it

1. **Edward R. Norman, *The Victorian Christian Socialists* (1987)**; [Cambridge University Press: 21E284CA045D1C134CBAE55358A32EF9](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/victorian-christian-socialists/21E284CA045D1C134CBAE55358A32EF9) . Norman treats the movement as a series of critiques by ethical and educational reformers rather than politicians, centred on Maurice, Kingsley, Hughes and Ruskin. This agrees with the sources' own emphasis on colleges and cooperatives, and differs from Bax's contemporary dismissal.
2. **Gustavo Gutiérrez, *A Theology of Liberation: History, Politics and Salvation* (Spanish 1971; English translation by Caridad Inda and John Eagleson, 1973)**; catalogue record: [avo Gutiérrez, A Theology of Liberation: History](https://archive.org/details/theologyoflibera0000guti) . The foundational text of liberation theology, defining theology as critical reflection on praxis and placing the poor at the centre. It should be read alongside, not instead of, the 2003 interview above.
3. **Maria Clara Bingemer, "Theology in Latin America", *St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology* (2023)**; [Source: saet.ac.uk](https://www.saet.ac.uk/Christianity/TheologyinLatinAmerica) . A scholarly survey that traces Medellín, Puebla, the base ecclesial communities and the later crisis of liberation theology, including Roman Curia objections to the use of Marxist categories. It gives the broad scholarly frame for the conflict that Gutiérrez's interview describes from one side.
4. **Felix Corley (ed.), *Religion in the Soviet Union: An Archival Reader* (1996)**; the edition from which the Samsonov and Peters documents were translated; verified through the source notes on the Seventeen Moments pages ([Source: soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1924/antireligious-propaganda/antireligious-propaganda-texts/)). Corley's collection supplies the archival documentary base; it does not itself settle how far policy was implemented.
5. **Boleslaw B. Szczesniak (ed. and tr.), *Russian Revolution and Religion: A Collection of Documents Concerning the Suppression of Religion by the Communists, 1917–1925* (1959)**; the edition behind the newspaper report above, verified through the same source notes. An older, explicitly anti-communist compilation: useful for the texts it preserves, to be checked against the original newspapers where possible.
