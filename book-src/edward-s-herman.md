Edward S. Herman was an American economist and media analyst who argued that news in the United States is shaped by the corporate interests that own and fund the media. His central idea, developed with Noam Chomsky, is the propaganda model: the claim that news is filtered before it reaches the public, not by one controller issuing orders, but by several pressures that reinforce one another. The model names five such filters. The first is the size, concentrated ownership, wealth and profit orientation of the dominant media firms. The second is advertising as the main source of income. The third is reliance on information supplied by government, business and experts funded or approved by them. The fourth is "flak", organised complaints that discipline media organisations. The fifth is anticommunism, later widened to include market ideology, treated as a national belief that marks some views as unacceptable. News has to pass through each filter, and what survives is the reporting that fits established interests. This profile sets out what Herman argued, what can be checked in his published texts, and where critics objected.

## Biography

Edward Samuel Herman was born in Philadelphia into a family of liberal Democrats. The University of Pennsylvania's own record says he studied economics at Penn, taking bachelor's and master's degrees, and found a mentor in Robert A. Brady, a Berkeley professor who studied the economic systems of fascist societies. He completed a doctorate in economics at Berkeley in 1953, joined Penn as a lecturer in 1958, became an associate professor in 1961 and a professor in 1970, and retired as professor emeritus in 1989. The same record describes him as an economist and media analyst specialising in corporate and regulatory issues and political economy. He died on 11 November 2017, aged 92 ([Penn Almanac, 5 December 2017](https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/edward-s-herman-wharton)). The precise birth date of 7 April 1925 appears in the author biography attached to his articles at ZNetwork and in an Open Library authority record; it was not confirmed from an institutional record opened for this profile and should be treated as unverified.

Before the media books his working life was that of a finance academic with a taste for regulatory conflict. In a 2012 interview with Paul Jay he said his doctoral work at Berkeley studied the Bank of America system and the Transamerica Corporation, that he moved from Penn State to Wharton after being recruited for a study of branch banking in Pennsylvania, and that at Wharton he worked on an early Securities and Exchange Commission study of mutual funds and on a Federal Home Loan Bank Board study of savings and loan associations. The conflict-of-interest paper from the second study, he recalled, was the one the industry hated and publicly denounced. A grant from the Twentieth Century Fund let him update Berle and Means's *The Modern Corporation and Private Property*; the result was *Corporate Control, Corporate Power* (1981). He placed the start of his collaboration with Chomsky in the early 1970s antiwar movement, their first joint article being on Saigon, and described both men's entry into media analysis as a natural extension of their work on corporate power ([The Real News Network interview, 1 July 2012](https://therealnews.com/eherman0403pt1); [RePEc/Cambridge book record](http://ideas.repec.org/b/cup/cbooks/9780521289078.html)).

Two details in the Penn obituary need care. It groups *The Washington Connection and Third World Fascism* and *After the Cataclysm* under 2015, although Herman's and Chomsky's own records and later publisher biographies place the original two-volume *The Political Economy of Human Rights* in 1979; the 2015 dates are best read as later editions, a point that was not independently verified. It also describes *The Myth of the Liberal Media* as being "with Robert W. McChesney", while the Peter Lang publisher page lists Herman alone as author. Neither discrepancy changes the central biography, but neither should be repeated as settled ([Penn Almanac](https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/edward-s-herman-wharton); [Peter Lang publisher page](https://www.peterlang.com/document/1090855)). The same notice records that Herman's wife of 67 years, Mary Woody, died in 2013, that he married Christine Abbott two years later, and that a brother, Harris, survived him. These are family facts from a single institutional notice and are not corroborated among the other sources opened here.

## Intellectual tradition

Herman belonged to the tradition of critical political economy rather than to a party or a school with a formal membership. His own account traced the propaganda model to "my economist studies of industrial structure", and in the 2012 interview he called it "a structural analysis of the media" that asks who owns the media, where their money comes from, how they obtain information, what negative feedback they face and what ideology they internalise. Chomsky confirmed this division of labour in 2018: "The basic framework of MC was Ed's", he wrote, referring to *Manufacturing Consent*, and he insisted on placing Herman's name first despite the usual alphabetic order; the opening investigation of the business structure of the major media was "mainly Ed's" ([The Real News interview](https://therealnews.com/eherman0403pt1); [Media Theory discussion, 2018](https://mediatheoryjournal.org/2018/12/20/discussion-the-propaganda-model-today/)).

The intellectual lineage he acknowledged runs through the study of corporate control. Brady's work on fascist economic systems and Leo Rogin's history of economic thought formed the Berkeley environment; Berle and Means supplied the question of who controls the large corporation; and the American tradition of industrial organisation supplied the method of deriving conduct from market structure. Later scholars place the propaganda model alongside ideology critique and the Frankfurt School's account of instrumental reason, alongside Robert K. Merton's functional analysis and Warren Breed's newsroom study, and alongside Thomas Ferguson's investment theory of party competition ([The Real News interview](https://therealnews.com/eherman0403pt1); [Penn Almanac](https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/edward-s-herman-wharton); [Media Theory discussion](https://mediatheoryjournal.org/2018/12/20/discussion-the-propaganda-model-today/)).

Tom Mills, one of the participants in the 2018 discussion, observed that *Manufacturing Consent* "wasn't that well integrated into existing work" and was "not a Marxist text in the narrower sense", though it overlapped substantially with Marxist media scholarship. That is a useful caution against labelling Herman a Marxist theorist on the strength of his political conclusions. He was, however, a combative participant in left politics, and his difference with Chomsky over Iran makes the point. In the 2012 interview Herman said Chomsky disliked "fighting on the left" and ad hominem argument, whereas he believed it important to criticise liberal and left organisations that he thought had followed Washington's line on Iran while ignoring the coup in Honduras. He also said he had never had a direct exchange with Chomsky on the matter. His tradition is therefore best described as structural political economy applied to media and foreign policy, together with an activist refusal to treat the liberal left as outside the field of criticism ([The Real News interview](https://therealnews.com/eherman0403pt1)).

## Major contributions

The propaganda model is the central contribution. In the book's own chapter the five filters are: "(1) the size, concentrated ownership, owner wealth, and profit orientation of the dominant mass-media firms; (2) advertising as the primary income source of the mass media; (3) the reliance of the media on information provided by government, business, and 'experts' funded and approved by these primary sources and agents of power; (4) 'flak' as a means of disciplining the media; and (5) 'anticommunism' as a national religion and control mechanism." The authors added that the filters "interact with and reinforce one another" and that "the raw material of news must pass through successive filters, leaving only the cleansed residue fit to print" ([Herman and Chomsky, "A Propaganda Model", excerpted from *Manufacturing Consent*, 1988](https://libcom.org/article/manufacturing-consent-propaganda-model-edward-s-herman-and-noam-chomsky)). In a retrospective essay, whose original publication year is uncertain, Herman explained that the phrase "manufacture of consent" came from Lippmann and that the model was "a decentralized and nonconspiratorial market system of control and processing", not a claim of direct orders or a single centre of command ([Herman, "The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective"](https://libcom.org/article/propaganda-model-retrospective-edward-s-herman)). Chomsky noted that the fifth filter was generalised in a later edition, and Herman's 2003 interview confirms that market ideology was added alongside anticommunism after the Soviet Union's collapse ([Media Theory discussion](https://mediatheoryjournal.org/2018/12/20/discussion-the-propaganda-model-today/); [David Ross interview, 2003](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Propaganda/Propaganda_Free_Press.html)).

A second contribution is the paired comparison of "worthy" and "unworthy" victims. Herman wrote that he and Chomsky used the terms for a dichotomisation "clearly related to political and economic advantage rather than anything like actual worth". He illustrated it with the heavy coverage of Solidarity's repression in Poland in 1980–81 beside the near-silence on the Turkish military government's attack on unions at the same time. The earliest version of the argument is in *Counter-Revolutionary Violence* (1973), which sorted killings into "benign", "constructive", "nefarious" and "mythical" bloodbaths according to the political interests served by reporting them ([Herman, "The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective"](https://libcom.org/article/propaganda-model-retrospective-edward-s-herman); [Chomsky and Herman, *Counter-Revolutionary Violence*, full text](https://chomsky.info/counter-revolutionary-violence)).

A third contribution is the analysis of the institutions that supply "terrorism" news. In *The "Terrorism" Industry* Herman and Gerry O'Sullivan described a public and private apparatus of government agencies, think tanks, security firms and experts that manufactures a preferred view of terrorism, with the mass media acting as a "transmission belt". Herman's retrospective says the book's aim was to expose the institutional roots of bias in mainstream perceptions of terrorism ([Herman, "Terrorism: the struggle against closure", 1996](https://documents.uow.edu.au/~bmartin/pubs/96ce/4_Herman.pdf)). In *Demonstration Elections* (1984), written with Frank Brodhead, he developed the idea of an election staged chiefly for a domestic American audience through the cases of the Dominican Republic, Vietnam and El Salvador ([East Carolina University catalogue](https://librarycatalog.ecu.edu/catalog/255052); [Scialabba review, 1984](http://georgescialabba.net/mtgs/1984/06/demonstration-election-usstage.html), seen only as a search-result endpoint).

A methodological point should be stated plainly, because it is often missing from summaries of his work. Herman insisted that the propaganda model explains "media behavior and performance" and not media effects: "We never claimed that the propaganda model explained everything or that it shows media omnipotence and complete effectiveness in manufacturing consent." He pointed to alternative media, grassroots information sources and public scepticism as limits, and to divisions within elites as openings for debate ([Herman, "The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective"](https://libcom.org/article/propaganda-model-retrospective-edward-s-herman)).

## Analysis of capitalism

For Herman the decisive feature of capitalism was not simply that owners held property but that ownership, advertising and institutional dependence produced a predictable alignment of news with elite interests. The news media are major corporations selling audiences to advertisers; advertisers want an affluent audience and a "supporting selling environment"; the media therefore compete for patrons rather than for readers alone ([Herman and Chomsky, "A Propaganda Model"](https://libcom.org/article/manufacturing-consent-propaganda-model-edward-s-herman-and-noam-chomsky); [David Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Propaganda/Propaganda_Free_Press.html)). In *Corporate Control, Corporate Power* the publisher's description says Herman found that managerial control had not changed the corporation's basic objectives of growth and profit, nor enhanced social responsibility, and that government regulation had "done surprisingly little to reduce the autonomy of the corporation" ([RePEc/Cambridge record](http://ideas.repec.org/b/cup/cbooks/9780521289078.html)). The full book was not opened for this profile, so this is the publisher's description rather than the book's own text.

In the 1997 interview with David Peterson Herman named the transnational corporation, the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank and the World Trade Organization as the institutions of global capital, and said they press for trade and investment rights over local preferences, lower welfare spending, lighter business taxes and weaker unions and environmental constraints ([ZNetwork, "The Global Media", June 1997](https://znetwork.org/zmagazine/the-global-media-by-david-peterson-1/)). In a 1996 essay he used "closure" for the process by which dissent on terrorism is kept out of public forums, and argued that official designations of "terrorist state" followed US diplomatic interests rather than a consistent standard: Nicaragua, Iran, Cuba, North Korea and Libya were named by Reagan, while South Africa, Israel and Iraq were variously exempted as allies or "constructively engaged" ([Herman, "Terrorism: the struggle against closure"](https://documents.uow.edu.au/~bmartin/pubs/96ce/4_Herman.pdf)).

He treated the political system as structurally similar to the media system. In 2003 he called the United States a "corporate pseudo-democracy" and a "plutocracy", and argued, following Thomas Ferguson, that when major party funders agree the parties do not compete on the issue; he used the 2000 election and the exclusion of Ralph Nader from the debates as examples ([ZNetwork, "Media Democratization", 17 April 2003](https://znetwork.org/zcommentary/media-democratization-by-edward-herman/)). He also drew attention to the non-reporting of worker insecurity, quoting Alan Greenspan's 1986 explanation that inflation was low because workers were "scared to death" and unable to ask for wage increases ([David Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Propaganda/Propaganda_Free_Press.html)). These are Herman's arguments; this profile does not re-verify his empirical counts or the causal weight he assigned to each filter.

## Analysis of socialism

Herman wrote no blueprint for a socialist economy and did not present the propaganda model as a theory of transition. His positive institutional preferences concern communication. In the 1997 Peterson interview he quoted the conclusion of *The Global Media*: the ultimate goal of media activists must be "the establishment of a global, nonprofit public sphere to replace, or at least complement, the global commercial media market". He defended public broadcasting where it existed, described the spread of the commercial American model as a weakening of the public sphere, and argued that the internet would not serve democratic ends if left to the market ([ZNetwork, "The Global Media"](https://znetwork.org/zmagazine/the-global-media-by-david-peterson-1/)). In 2003 he pointed to South Korea's OhmyNews as a small but real precedent for independent online journalism and wrote that "we desperately need our own" media, together with strong grassroots organisations ([ZNetwork, "Media Democratization"](https://znetwork.org/zcommentary/media-democratization-by-edward-herman/)). In the 2012 interview, asked what he would like to see, he answered: "Oh, I would like to see a good democratic socialist movement", and praised George Galloway's victory over Labour and Conservative candidates as evidence of a third force ([The Real News interview](https://therealnews.com/eherman0403pt1)).

His treatment of actually existing socialism was sharply asymmetrical, and this needs to be reported with the same care as his critiques of the United States. In the book's own chapter, state-bureaucratic media are described as openly serving a dominant elite through monopolistic control and censorship, while private media are said to make the same function harder to see ([Herman and Chomsky, "A Propaganda Model"](https://libcom.org/article/manufacturing-consent-propaganda-model-edward-s-herman-and-noam-chomsky)). In *Counter-Revolutionary Violence* he and Chomsky quoted a Rand analyst's favourable account of North Vietnamese agricultural cooperatives and described North Vietnam as genuinely popular, while arguing that the largest post-war bloodbaths were sponsored by the United States and its clients ([Chomsky and Herman, *Counter-Revolutionary Violence*](https://chomsky.info/counter-revolutionary-violence)). Those judgments are contested and are examined in the criticisms section below; they are not presented here as established history.

## Recommended reading

This list picks the books that best introduce Herman's argument, not the whole of his output.

- *Counter-Revolutionary Violence: Bloodbaths in Fact and Propaganda* (1973). This is the place to begin with his "worthy" and "unworthy" victims argument: it sorts killings into "benign", "constructive", "nefarious" and "mythical" bloodbaths according to the political interests served by reporting them.
- *Corporate Control, Corporate Power: A Twentieth Century Fund Study* (1981). Start here for the corporate political economy behind the media work, where his study found that managerial control had not changed the corporation's basic objectives of growth and profit and that government regulation had done surprisingly little to reduce its autonomy.
- *Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media* (1988). This is the essential first book because it sets out the five filters, from ownership and advertising to "flak" and anticommunism, and insists that they work together without a single controller giving orders.
- *The "Terrorism" Industry: The Experts and Institutions That Shape Our View of Terror* (1989). Read this to see how he extends the filter argument to one news field, describing the agencies, think tanks and experts that produce a preferred view of terrorism with the mass media as a "transmission belt".
- *The Global Media: The New Missionaries of Corporate Capitalism* (1997). This is the best starting point for his positive proposal, because it concludes that media activists need a global, nonprofit public sphere to replace or at least complement the commercial media market.

## Intellectual influences

Herman's own interview testimony supplies the reliable influences. He named Robert A. Brady, author of *The Spirit and Structure of German Fascism* and *Business as a System of Power*, and Leo Rogin, a historian of economic thought, as the Berkeley figures who drew him toward radical economics; he named Berle and Means's *The Modern Corporation and Private Property* as the work his Twentieth Century Fund study updated ([The Real News interview](https://therealnews.com/eherman0403pt1)).

Inside the media argument he drew on James Curran and Jean Seaton's history of the British working-class press, Ben Bagdikian's *The Media Monopoly*, Mark Fishman's "bureaucratic affinity", Warren Breed's newsroom study and Walter Lippmann's phrase "manufacture of consent" ([Herman and Chomsky, "A Propaganda Model"](https://libcom.org/article/manufacturing-consent-propaganda-model-edward-s-herman-and-noam-chomsky); [Herman, "The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective"](https://libcom.org/article/propaganda-model-retrospective-edward-s-herman); [Media Theory discussion](https://mediatheoryjournal.org/2018/12/20/discussion-the-propaganda-model-today/) for the Breed and Merton connection). Chomsky's role was that of co-author and joint developer rather than a one-way influence: the 2018 discussion records Chomsky saying the framework was Herman's and that he had urged Herman's name be placed first ([Media Theory discussion](https://mediatheoryjournal.org/2018/12/20/discussion-the-propaganda-model-today/)).

Herman also cited Thomas Ferguson's work on campaign finance and Benjamin Page's survey evidence on the "elite-mass gap" as corroboration, and used Alex Carey, John Stauber and Sheldon Rampton on the public relations industry in his retrospective ([Herman, "The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective"](https://libcom.org/article/propaganda-model-retrospective-edward-s-herman)). These are his stated sources, not an independent verification of the claims they support.

## Influence and legacy

The propaganda model became one of the most widely tested frameworks in media studies. In 2018 a group of scholars described it as "one of the most widely tested models of media performance in the social sciences", with studies in the United States, Britain, Canada, Australia, Japan, China, Germany and the Netherlands, and marked the thirtieth anniversary of *Manufacturing Consent* with an open-access collection from University of Westminster Press ([Media Theory discussion](https://mediatheoryjournal.org/2018/12/20/discussion-the-propaganda-model-today/)). The same discussion records both the model's continued use and its marginalisation in mainstream media scholarship, a double judgment worth preserving. Jeffery Klaehn's earlier review articles treated the model as a serious object for sociology and political economy, while Oliver Boyd-Barrett judged the five filters pedagogically powerful but "somewhat derivative and truncated" beside the rest of the book and the earlier work of Herbert Schiller ([Klaehn 2002](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267323102017002691); [Klaehn 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/14616700306487); [Media Theory discussion](https://mediatheoryjournal.org/2018/12/20/discussion-the-propaganda-model-today/)).

Beyond academia, the paired-case method and the "worthy/unworthy victims" vocabulary shaped media criticism and activist analysis of the Kosovo and Iraq wars, and the 1997 Media Education Foundation film carried the model into teaching ([Media Education Foundation transcript](https://www.mediaed.org/transcripts/Myth-of-The-Liberal-Media-Transcript.pdf)). The Penn obituary quotes FAIR's Jeff Cohen calling Herman one of the leading progressive media critics and notes that Herman received primary credit for the propaganda model ([Penn Almanac](https://almanac.upenn.edu/articles/edward-s-herman-wharton)). His later work with David Peterson extended the double-standard argument to the language of "genocide" and to wars and mass killings in Yugoslavia, Rwanda, Congo and Iran. That extension is the most disputed part of his legacy and is treated in the next section.

## Criticisms and controversies

The scholarly objections are substantial and should not be reduced to a single line. Herman's own retrospective lists them: that the model is a conspiracy theory; that it derives from Chomskian linguistics rather than industrial organisation; that he failed to ask reporters for their intentions; that it ignores journalistic professionalism; that it confuses media behaviour with media effects; and that it is deterministic, functionalist and leaves no room for contestation ([Herman, "The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective"](https://libcom.org/article/propaganda-model-retrospective-edward-s-herman)). These are Herman's summaries of his critics rather than the critics' texts read directly. The named critics include Robert Entman (1990), Carlin Romano (1989), Daniel Hallin (1994), Walter LaFeber (1988), Philip Schlesinger (1989), Peter Golding and Graham Murdock (1991), and John Eldridge (1993); their full works were not opened for this profile. Klaehn's 2002 and 2003 articles assess the model and its reception, and the 2018 discussion adds criticism from inside the critical camp: Tom Mills found the book poorly integrated with existing communication scholarship, and Boyd-Barrett argued that the filters overstate systemic explanation at the expense of journalists' agency ([Klaehn 2002](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267323102017002691); [Klaehn 2003](https://doi.org/10.1080/14616700306487); [Media Theory discussion](https://mediatheoryjournal.org/2018/12/20/discussion-the-propaganda-model-today/)).

The record of Herman's work with David Peterson has three verified parts. Peterson interviewed Herman and McChesney in 1997 on global media and democracy; that was an interview, not an adversarial debate. Peterson later became Herman's co-author on *The Politics of Genocide* and numerous articles. The pair replied jointly to critics. A publication titled *Democracy and the Media* as a Herman–Peterson debate could not be verified: the phrase appears in the record as a topic, not as the title of a joint work. The nearest verified items are the 1997 *Z Magazine* interview and the "Media Democratization" and "The Importance of a Left Media" articles Herman wrote for ZNetwork ([ZNetwork interview](https://znetwork.org/zmagazine/the-global-media-by-david-peterson-1/); [Media Democratization](https://znetwork.org/zcommentary/media-democratization-by-edward-herman/); [The Importance of a Left Media](https://znetwork.org/zcommentary/the-importance-of-a-left-media-by-edward-herman/), the last seen only as a search-result endpoint). No such debate is therefore listed here as a publication.

The sharpest published criticism came from within the anti-imperialist camp. Gerald Caplan's review of *The Politics of Genocide* in *Pambazuka News* (17 June 2010) argued that the book's Rwanda chapter inverted the evidence on the 1994 genocide, relied on a small circle of writers Caplan called deniers, ignored a large scholarly literature and eyewitness testimony, and did grave damage to the anti-imperialist case it sought to advance ([Caplan review](https://links.org.au/politics-denialism-strange-case-rwanda-review-herman-and-petersons-politics-genocide)). It is a review essay in an advocacy outlet, not a peer-reviewed article. Herman and Peterson replied in detail, accusing Caplan of selectivity, of defending Paul Kagame's government and of misreading the Rwanda numbers; the reply was posted under Peterson's byline but is explicitly headed "Edward S. Herman and David Peterson" ([Herman and Peterson response, 4 July 2010](https://znetwork.org/znetarticle/genocide-denial-and-genocide-facilitation-a-response-to-gerald-caplan-by-david-peterson/)). Adam Jones and others entered the same exchange; those further responses were located but not opened.

A related dispute over Iran in 2009 set Herman and Peterson against the Campaign for Peace and Democracy. *New Politics* summarised the campaign's objection that Herman and Peterson dismissed the Iranian election protests and withheld solidarity from movements in countries outside US control while demanding it for movements within it ([Thomas Harrison, *New Politics*, 27 August 2009](https://newpol.org/debate-about-iran-and-left)). This is a participant's summary of an advocacy exchange. Herman confirmed the substance in his 2012 interview while maintaining that he would always support Iranian workers' right to fight for their rights ([The Real News interview](https://therealnews.com/eherman0403pt1)). These controversies are facts about the reception of Herman's work, not findings that settle the underlying historical disputes. This profile distinguishes what Herman and Peterson argued, what their critics said and what the available evidence establishes. For the Rwanda and Cambodia claims in particular, only the poles of the argument were opened here, not the body of genocide scholarship Caplan says the authors omitted; no judgment on those claims is made here.

## Related thinkers

Noam Chomsky was Herman's closest collaborator, on *Counter-Revolutionary Violence*, the two volumes of *The Political Economy of Human Rights* and *Manufacturing Consent*, and he wrote the foreword to *The Politics of Genocide*. His 2018 account of the division of labour is set out above; his side of the partnership is covered in the [Noam Chomsky](/noam-chomsky) profile. Robert W. McChesney co-authored *The Global Media* and edited or introduced collections of Herman's work. Frank Brodhead co-authored *Demonstration Elections* and *The Rise and Fall of the Bulgarian Connection*; Gerry O'Sullivan co-authored *The "Terrorism" Industry*. David Peterson co-authored *The Politics of Genocide* and later articles and was the interviewer in 1997. Ben Bagdikian, Herbert Schiller and James Curran supplied the political-economy of media framework Herman drew on; Daniel Hallin, Philip Schlesinger, Graham Murdock and Peter Golding were the critics he engaged; Thomas Ferguson and Benjamin Page supplied adjacent empirical work he regarded as corroborating. Jeffery Klaehn and the contributors to the 2018 *Media Theory* discussion represent the later scholarly reception.
