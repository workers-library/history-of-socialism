In the early hours of 1 October 1965 a group of officers led by Lieutenant Colonel Untung seized radio and communications facilities in Jakarta, killed six senior generals, and announced a "30 September Movement" that claimed to be forestalling a pro-American coup by the generals ([FRUS 1964–1968, vol. XXVI, docs. 149 and 161](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d149); [CIA, Office of Current Intelligence, 6 October 1965](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d161)). The CIA's Office of Current Intelligence, writing days later from broadcasts and reports, recorded that President Sukarno told the generals to "leave the political settlement to me." Within a day the army under General Suharto had crushed the movement. The destruction of the Communist Party of Indonesia, the PKI, followed over the next months and was still under way in 1966. It was the largest communist party outside the Soviet bloc and China, with millions of members, a trade-union federation, a peasant front, a women's organisation and a press. The surviving record consists chiefly of the papers of the people who planned, watched and encouraged that destruction, because the party's own documents are almost entirely missing.

## Three weeks of silence

The movement collapsed in a day, but the campaign against the PKI began weeks later. The CIA memorandum of 6 October was still listing Sukarno's whereabouts and motives as open questions, and recording Suharto's public implication that air-force elements were connected: "But it is impossible that there is no connection with this affair among elements of the air force." By 26 October Ambassador Marshall Green was reconstructing where the party leader Aidit and the air-force commander Omar Dani had been. His telegram concluded that "odds seem overwhelming that, at very least, Sukarno knew what was afoot and had given tacit blessing to seizure of the generals". A footnote recorded that the Embassy saw the key question as how to help the army win without revealing that assistance. The army was still manoeuvring politically in early November while the killings had begun. Both documents argue a case as well as report, and Green's reconstruction of PKI involvement has been contested, but the sequence is not in doubt.

## What the Americans wrote

The declassified record is unusually direct about outside support. A State Department memorandum of 3 November reported that the army knew the Pentagon, the CIA and the State Department all approved what it was doing, and concluded: "In the life and death struggle which has finally been joined with the PKI, the Army deserves our support." A CIA paper of 9 November discussed how covert credits, small arms and medicine might reach officers and selected civilian auxiliaries, and described the army's determination to break the party's organisational back and prevent any crypto-communist successor ([FRUS 1964–1968, vol. XXVI, doc. 172](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v26/d172)). These were proposals, not a receipt for every item delivered, and several lines remain withheld. In the field the reports were less guarded. A consular telegram from Medan on 16 November said the leaders of Pemuda Pantjasila, a Muslim youth organisation, intended to kill every PKI member they caught and would not hand captives over until they were dead or near death: "Attitude Pemuda Pantjasila leaders can only be described as bloodthirsty." A Surabaya cable of 24 November reported that "we continue receive reports PKI being slaughtered by Ansor many areas East Java", with missionaries describing bodies in rivers. By 30 November the Embassy's weekly summary could state the logic plainly: "Many provinces appear to be successfully meeting this problem by executing their PKI prisoners, or by killing them before they are captured, a task in which Moslem youth groups are providing assistance." Its estimate of 5,000 to 10,000 executed leaders and activists was described in the document itself as probably grossly exaggerated.

## Sukarno's own words

Sukarno remained president for a time and used the speeches he was still allowed to make to defend himself. On 11 March 1966 he signed Supersemar, an order authorising Suharto to take whatever measures he judged necessary for security, calm and stability, in the President's name. The circumstances of the signing are disputed, and Sukarno later implied the order had been extracted under pressure. In his accountability speech of June 1966, the Nawaksara, he defended Guided Democracy and self-reliance but gave no account of 1 October or the killings that followed; the assembly rejected the speech for that omission. In the supplement of January 1967 he finally addressed it, calling the movement a complete surprise: "G.30.S adalah satu 'complete overrompeling' bagi saya." He distributed responsibility among the party leadership, foreign subversion and unnamed individuals, and asked why he alone was held to account. The speech was addressed to a body already dominated by his rivals, and its evasiveness is itself evidence.

## Fifty years of silence

The killings did not end with Suharto's rise; the ability to speak about them did. The International People's Tribunal on Crimes Against Humanity in Indonesia, a non-governmental body that held hearings in The Hague in November 2015, opened with a prosecutor's account of the taboo at home: "the matter related to 1965 atrocities is still taboo, not subject to discussion and deliberation". Films had been banned, a writers' festival session cancelled, and witnesses who travelled to The Hague took risks. The judges found allegations of mass murder, detention without trial, torture, sexual violence and enforced disappearance well founded and held the Indonesian state responsible, noting that the story of the generals' castration had long been disproved by autopsy reports known to successive governments. Their statement came before the final reasoned judgment, and no state participated. A survivor named Leo Mulyono, an art student who spent fourteen years in prison, described being reduced to a number: "Starting then, we had no names, we were just numbers. There was no Leo." Adi Rukun, whose brother was abducted and killed, confronted the men responsible and found none would apologise: "Fifty years is already too long."

## How historians have read it

- **Benedict Anderson and Ruth McVey, *A Preliminary Analysis of the October 1, 1965, Coup in Indonesia* (Cornell Modern Indonesia Project, 1971).** Written in January 1966 from Indonesian newspapers and radio monitoring, it argued that the coup was an internal army affair and that the PKI and Sukarno were drawn in afterwards. Its thesis has been revised but not discarded.
- **Harold Crouch, *The Army and Politics in Indonesia* (Cornell University Press, 1978).** Used the special military court testimony of PKI leaders to argue that the party was involved, while leaving the extent unclear; this is the main early challenge to the Cornell thesis in its original form.
- **Robert Cribb, "Unresolved Problems in the Indonesian Killings of 1965–1966," *Asian Survey* 42(4), 2002, pp. 550–563.** Argues that considerable evidence shows the US encouraged the killings with funds and names, but that intervention did not significantly increase their scale. [Source: hdl.handle.net](https://hdl.handle.net/1885/71422)
- **John Roosa, *Pretext for Mass Murder: The September 30th Movement and Suharto's Coup d'État in Indonesia* (University of Wisconsin Press, 2006).** Reopens the question of who planned 1 October using the testimony of the accused; closer to the Cornell position than to Crouch on the army's responsibility.
- **Bradley R. Simpson, *Economists with Guns: Authoritarian Development and US–Indonesian Relations, 1960–1968* (Stanford University Press, 2010).** Places the killings inside the history of US support for the Indonesian army; his chapter 7 covers the destruction of the PKI.
- **Geoffrey B. Robinson, *The Killing Season: A History of the Indonesian Massacres, 1965–66* (Princeton University Press, 2018); Jess Melvin, *The Army and the Indonesian Genocide: Mechanics of Mass Murder* (Routledge, 2018).** Robinson argues the killings were a deliberate army campaign facilitated by the US, Britain and others, and writes explicitly to end the silence around them. Melvin uses 3,000 pages from the former Indonesian intelligence agency's archive in Banda Aceh plus the testimony of 70 witnesses to prove centralised military agency and to make the genocide case. They agree on army agency; they differ on whether the legal term genocide fits, a question on which Cribb and Coppel also dissent.

There is broad scholarly agreement that the killings were organised rather than spontaneous, that the army was the principal agent, that Western states gave material and political support, and that the toll ran to hundreds of thousands ([Cribb, "The Indonesian Massacres," 2012](https://asset.routledge.com/rt-media/pdf/9780415871921/chapter7_the_indonesian_massacres.pdf); [IPT 1965, *Final Report*, 2016](https://www.concernedhistorians.org/content_files/file/le/681.pdf)). Disagreement continues over who planned the 1 October operation, over the appropriate legal label, and over how much Western support changed the scale of killing.

## Limits of the record

Who planned the 1 October operation, and how far Sukarno and the PKI leadership were involved, is still disputed. The 6 October memorandum leaves Sukarno's role open; Green's telegram concludes he at least gave tacit blessing; the Cornell Paper, written in January 1966, argued that the initiative came from middle-ranking army officers in Central Java. The first-hand record cannot settle this, because the decisive Indonesian military and party records have not been opened. No full text of the movement's radio proclamation has been verified. There is no verified PKI document here: Aidit's writings, the party newspaper's editorial of 2 October 1965 and the letter read by Njoto in the Bogor cabinet are known only through secondary descriptions, so none is quoted.

The death toll is likewise unresolved. Contemporary estimates ranged from the Embassy's 5,000 to 10,000, which it called probably grossly exaggerated, to the one million reported by the security command Kopkamtib in 1966. Robert Cribb's review of the evidence concludes that a scholarly consensus has settled on 400,000 to 500,000, while the correct figure could be half or twice as much; the International People's Tribunal adopted that range ([Cribb, "The Indonesian Massacres," 2012](https://asset.routledge.com/rt-media/pdf/9780415871921/chapter7_the_indonesian_massacres.pdf); [IPT 1965, *Final Report*, 2016](https://www.concernedhistorians.org/content_files/file/le/681.pdf)). Whether to call the killings genocide, politicide or crimes against humanity remains a live interpretive question. The official record is rich on high politics and almost silent on the victims. Diplomats and intelligence officers learned of deaths through missionaries, railway officials and informers, not from the killed. The survivor testimony here arrives fifty years later and from unusual people: an art student who survived the prison system, a man who chose to confront his brother's killers. We cannot recover what an ordinary villager, a member of the women's organisation Gerwani or a trade unionist understood as the army arrived. Women's testimony is especially thin, though the judges referred to a 2007 report on sexual violence by Indonesia's national human rights commission that has not been opened. The Indonesian Wikisource transcriptions should be checked against scans, and Acehnese and Javanese material needs translation. The National Security Archive files contain redactions, and several hundred pages from the 1964 to 1968 Embassy collection were still classified in 2017. A physical visit to the US National Archives, the Johnson Library and the Indonesian archives would be needed to close these gaps. Neither film made about the period has been viewed. The tribunal's original website now serves an unrelated gambling site; every document cited from it here comes from an archived capture, a reliable preservation copy that should be identified as such.
