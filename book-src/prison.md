In an Odessa prison, a blacksmith welded iron rings onto Leo Deutsch's ankles. Deutsch was a founder of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party, and he was being prepared for the long journey to Siberia. "Every stroke of the hammer made my heart sink, as I realised that a new existence was beginning for me." ([Leo Deutsch, *Sixteen Years in Siberia*, describing the welding of his fetters, translated by Helen Chisholm (1903)](https://archive.org/stream/cu31924023036142/cu31924023036142_djvu.txt)) His translator noted the quiet tone and the absence of sensationalism, and advised reading between the lines. Deutsch was marched through Moscow in chains, with half his head shaved; the convict dress and chains were meant to mark him as "deprived of all rights". This chapter asks what a text like that can establish. Prison writing is the book's richest store of first-hand evidence, but a memoir proves that one person experienced something; it does not give a rate. Four kinds of writing are used here: retrospective memoirs, letters smuggled out at the time, poems written in custody, and interviews recorded long after release. Each tells us something different, and each has its own limits.

## The nineteenth-century inheritance

A literature of confinement existed before Marx. Alfred Dreyfus, a French artillery captain convicted of treason in 1894, described his public degradation on 5 January 1895: "Soldiers, they are degrading an innocent man. Soldiers, they are dishonoring an innocent man. Vive la France, vive l'armée!" ([Alfred Dreyfus, "The Degradation," 1895, translated by August F. Jaccaci (1901)](https://www.posenlibrary.com/entry/five-years-my-life)) He was held on Devil's Island in French Guiana. The Communards deported after 1871 went chiefly to New Caledonia, a different place which should not be confused with it. Dreyfus was not a socialist; he supplies the liberal case of a man wrongly convicted, and he had to reclaim his own words in writing after a gendarmerie captain turned his protests into a legend of confession. Louise Michel, a Communard, schoolmistress and anarchist, wrote her memoirs inside Saint-Lazare prison, using a rule that allowed attorneys to receive sealed letters to get her pages past the gate. "Many brave hearts are found among those unfortunate prisoners whom people despise. People must see things as they are, and only someone who has lived through such experiences knows." ([Louise Michel, memoirs written at Saint-Lazare, 1883–86, translated by Lowry and Gunter](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/louise-michel-the-red-virgin)) Her translators state that the words are hers and the arrangement is theirs, and they record her own inaccuracies about her age and her past. What these three accounts establish is the experience of punishment meant to mark the body and strip honour.

## Writing under censorship

Rosa Luxemburg wrote from Wronke to Sophie Liebknecht, the wife of Karl Liebknecht, under rigid censorship. She was allowed two letters a month, and delivery could take from four days to three weeks. Time itself changed in prison: "As during the siege of Sebastopol, every month seems a year." ([Rosa Luxemburg to Sophie Liebknecht, Wronke, 21 November 1916](https://www.marxists.org/archive/luxemburg/1916/11/21.htm)) She watched a wryneck, then the disappearance of songbirds, then a beetle eaten by ants; she said she felt more at home with birds than at a party congress, while holding to her purpose: "You know that I really hope to die at my post, in a street fight or in prison." ([Rosa Luxemburg, letter to Sophie Liebknecht, Wronke, 2 May 1917, translated by Eden and Cedar Paul](https://www.marxists.org/archive/luxemburg/1917/05/02.htm))

Antonio Gramsci, founder of the Italian Communist Party, wrote his notebooks in a Fascist prison between 1929 and 1935. Censorship entered the language itself. Where the Marxist meaning of "class" would be obvious, he substituted "social group"; his editors record the fact in a footnote: "Gramsci tends, for reasons of censorship, to avoid using the word class in contexts where its Marxist overtones would be apparent." ([Editors' footnote, Hoare and Nowell Smith, on Gramsci's terminology, not Gramsci's words](https://www.marxists.org/archive/gramsci/prison_notebooks/problems/intellectuals.htm)) His argument survived the substitution: "All men are intellectuals, one could therefore say: but not all men have in society the function of intellectuals." ([Antonio Gramsci, "The Intellectuals," written in prison 1929–30, translated and edited by Quintin Hoare and Geoffrey Nowell Smith (1971)](https://www.marxists.org/archive/gramsci/prison_notebooks/problems/intellectuals.htm)) Ho Chi Minh, held by Chiang Kai-shek's police in Guangxi in 1942–43, wrote his quatrains in classical Chinese because writing in Vietnamese would have looked suspicious. "Hauled o'er thirteen districts of Guangsi; / Kept in eighteen prisons successively" ([Ho Chi Minh, "At the Political Bureau of the Fourth War Zone," written in Chinese in prison, 1942–43, in the Hanoi English edition](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.220231/2015.220231.Prison-Diary_djvu.txt)) The odd language is itself evidence of the conditions under which a witness could speak.

## The body and the clock

Other witnesses record the small physical facts that a policy statement cannot supply. Arthur Koestler, a journalist in Spain in 1937, inventoried his cell and then noticed the guard at the spyhole: "It is an eye without a man attached to it, and for a few moments the prisoner's heart stops beating." ([Arthur Koestler, *Dialogue with Death*, Spain, 1937](https://www.thesunmagazine.org/articles/25143-this-prison-where-i-live)) Zhang Xianliang, a Chinese novelist who spent twenty-two years in prisons and labour camps, described the daily sorting of grass: "Here in the camps, the trick was to see whether or not a convict's mouth was green." ([Zhang Xianliang, "Grass Soup," China, 1960, translated by Martha Avery](https://www.thesunmagazine.org/articles/25143-this-prison-where-i-live)) Green lips meant a convict had found something to eat. Jacobo Timerman, an Argentine journalist held by the military dictatorship in 1977, described a wet mattress and a slit of light that removed the sense of time. Through a peephole he exchanged glances with a stranger until a guard said the other man had been "cooled out". He refused to accept the explanation: "Tenderness is indeed a consolation, whereas love is a need." ([Jacobo Timerman, "The Eye," Argentina, 1977, translated by Toby Talbot](https://www.thesunmagazine.org/articles/25143-this-prison-where-i-live)) Albie Sachs, a law professor detained under South Africa's ninety-day law, which allowed detention without trial, was handed a release order on the ninetieth day and then arrested again: "'I am placing you under arrest,' he says." ([Albie Sachs, *The Jail Diary of Albie Sachs*, South Africa, 1963–64](https://www.thesunmagazine.org/articles/25143-this-prison-where-i-live)) Primo Levi, deported to Auschwitz, described a recurring dream in which he tells his sister about the camp and she walks away without listening: "Why is the pain of every day translated so constantly into our dreams, in the ever repeated scene of the unlistened-to story?" ([Primo Levi, "Dreams in Auschwitz," 1944–45, translated by Stuart Woolf](https://www.thesunmagazine.org/articles/25143-this-prison-where-i-live)) These accounts agree on the texture of confinement: the body and the schedule are taken over, information is rationed, and the witness is left with a story no listener seems to want.

## A prison of consent

Not every "prison" in this record has bars. Miklós Haraszti, a Hungarian dissident and editor of the samizdat journal *Beszélő*, argued in 1987 that cultural control under post-Stalinist socialism worked by co-option rather than silencing. The state housed, paid and published artists, and censorship became a final glaze on work already approved: "A new aesthetic culture has emerged in which censors and artists alike are entangled in a mutual embrace." ([Miklós Haraszti, "The Velvet Prison," Partisan Review, 1987, translated by Katalin and Stephen Landesmann with Steve Wasserman](https://www.bu.edu/partisanreview/books/PR1987V54N3/HTML/files/assets/basic-html/page21.html)) His "velvet prison" is a metaphor for a system of incentives, not a cell, and it cannot establish that anyone was imprisoned. It belongs here as a different use of the word, not as a rival report from the same institution.

## The demand to renounce

Some confinement was preventive rather than punitive. Irini Papatsaroucha-Missiou, a member of the Lambrakis Youth, was arrested in Athens on the night of the colonels' coup in April 1967 and held on Gyaros and at Alikarnassos for about three and a half years without charge or trial. She was pressed to sign a declaration renouncing communism. "Because I wouldn't renounce even Buddhism, any -ism. I wouldn't renounce anything under dictation." ([Irini Papatsaroucha-Missiou, Istorima interview, recalling Gyaros, 1967, translated by Eva Kesserling](https://www.istorima.org/en/podcasts/609/rinio-missiou-exile)) She said she declared herself a communist only once, to stop the interviews. Carles Vallejo, a Catalan trade unionist detained in 1970, described beatings on the way to the station, twenty days of interrogation and torture, and then La Model prison, which he called a relief because there was no direct torture there. He was required to subscribe to the prison paper *Redención* to reduce his sentence. In these cases the aim was to change a person's statement, not simply to keep them shut away.

## What the record cannot establish

The record cannot supply numbers. A memoir proves one experience, not a death toll or the frequency of a practice, and the surviving voices are heavily literate and elite: party founders, novelists, a future president, a prominent journalist, a professor. Peasants, ordinary criminals and the illiterate appear only as figures in someone else's narrative. Most witnesses wrote after release and for a public, so memory, editorial selection and political purpose all intervene. The Gramsci notebooks say little about his cell, and their arrangement into a coherent book is the editors' work, not Gramsci's. The published record does not include the Michel correspondence or the Dreyfus diary manuscript; the Greek junta's own files and the Spanish prison administration's records are absent. Margarete Buber-Neumann's *Under Two Dictators*, which would set Stalin's and Hitler's camps in one life, is not available in an accessible text, and Gramsci's letters and Luxemburg's other prison letters remain leads rather than witnesses here. Greek and Spanish testimony reaches English through late interviews and translation, the English edition of Ho's poems names no translator, the Theodorakis diary is in Greek, and Carlota O'Neill's Spanish memoir is known only through a secondary study. The "Bastille and civil disobedience tradition" is the thinnest part of the record. Above all, testimony cannot speak for the person who did not survive. Timerman's neighbour across the peephole remains an eye and a rumour; Levi's sister walks away without listening. These sources document suffering under states of very different kinds. Comparison helps ask what testimony can establish. It is not a claim that the institutions were the same.

## How historians have read it

- **Shoshana Felman and Dori Laub, *Testimony: Crises of Witnessing in Literature, Psychoanalysis and History* (Routledge, 1992).** Argues that extreme events break the ordinary relation between event, witness and listener; the chapter "An Event Without a Witness" bears directly on the silence around Timerman's neighbour and Levi's unlistening sister. [Source: routledge.com](https://www.routledge.com/Testimony-Crises-of-Witnessing-in-Literature-Psychoanalysis-and-History/Felman-Laub/p/book/9780415903929)
- **Lynne Ann Hartnett, *The Defiant Life of Vera Figner: Surviving the Russian Revolution* (Indiana University Press, 2014).** Uses archives and Figner's memoirs to follow a Narodnik revolutionary from Schlüsselburg to accommodation with the Soviet regime; it treats the prison memoir as a political artifact as well as a record. [Source: iupress.org](https://iupress.org/9780253012845/the-defiant-life-of-vera-figner/)
- **Leona Toker, *Return from the Archipelago: Narratives of Gulag Survivors* (Indiana University Press, 2000).** The first broad survey of Soviet camp narrative as a genre, distinguishing memoir from fiction and treating repetition and reticence as evidence; its method supports reading Ho's poems and Gramsci's coded language as shaped forms. [Source: iupress.org](https://iupress.org/9780253337870/return-from-the-archipelago/)
- **Frank Rosengarten (ed.) and Raymond Rosenthal (trans.), Antonio Gramsci, *Letters from Prison*, 2 vols. (Columbia University Press, 1994).** A scholarly edition of more than four hundred letters which supplies the prison experience the *Notebooks* omit. [Source: cup.columbia.edu](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/letters-from-prison/9780231075527/)
- **Derek Boothman, "The New Edition of Gramsci's *Lettere dal carcere*," *International Gramsci Journal* 4, no. 2 (2021), 177–193.** Reviews the 2020 Einaudi edition of 489 letters and stresses the coded political messages and the editorial work needed to read them, against treating the letters as simple self-expression. [Source: journals.uniurb.it](https://journals.uniurb.it/index.php/igj/article/view/4116)

There is broad agreement that prison testimony is indispensable for experience and insufficient for scale. The live interpretive disagreement concerns how far the constraints on writing, censorship, self-censorship, translation and later editing, distort the witness. Toker and Boothman treat the constraints as legible and interpretable; Felman and Laub are more sceptical about whether the event can be transmitted at all.
