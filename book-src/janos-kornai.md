János Kornai was a Hungarian economist who was born and schooled under capitalism, lived through four decades of socialism and then observed its collapse. He built his reputation by asking which constraint actually stopped a firm from expanding, and by arguing that in state socialist economies the answer usually lay in physical bottlenecks and in managers' expectation that the state would cover their losses. He later turned the same comparative method on capitalism. This profile sets out what he argued, which passages and dates can be checked in the opened sources, and where other economists objected.

## Biography

The existing chapter on [planning, incentives and shortage](/planning-incentives-and-shortage) assembles contemporary documents on raised quotas, worker letters, empty shelves and contested explanations of why goods remained scarce; this profile adds the incentive mechanisms and the comparison of whole economic systems that Kornai built from such evidence.

Kornai was born in Budapest on 21 January 1928. The date is stated in his own memoir, where he writes that his father was forty-seven on that day, and it is corroborated by the title of a 2019 *Acta Oeconomica* editor's preface, "János Kornai Turns 90 on January 21", recorded in the reference list of the in memoriam by Péter Mihályi. He grew up in a Jewish professional family: his father was Dr. Pál Kornhauser, a lawyer, and he changed the family surname from Kornhauser to Kornai in 1945. He attended a German-language school in Budapest and lived through the anti-Jewish laws, forced labour and the siege of Budapest; the memoir describes his father's refusal to join any political party and his own adolescence under racial persecution. Mihályi, a long-time disciple and the editor of *Acta Oeconomica*, says Kornai completed his schooling in Budapest, became a convinced communist after the fall of the fascist regime in 1945 and became disillusioned with Marxist-Leninist thought between 1953 and 1956 ([Kornai, *By Force of Thought*, 2006](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/konyvek_cikkek/w20825/Kornai2007-By-force-of-thought--MITPress.pdf); [Mihályi, in memoriam, 2021](https://akjournals.com/view/journals/032/71/4/article-p507.xml)).

He joined the staff of the Communist newspaper *Szabad Nép* in June 1947 and left eight years later, in 1955; his memoir treats that period as a first-hand education in how the party-state actually worked. In August 1953 he was admitted to prepare for a "candidacy" degree, the Soviet-style qualification treated as equivalent to a PhD; the dissertation became the research published as *Overcentralization in Economic Administration*. He began work at the Institute of Economics of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences in June 1955, just before the Hungarian Revolution of October 1956. He did not take a conventional university degree in economics. The memoir is explicit that he taught himself modern Western economics from books in 1957, starting with Samuelson in German translation and then reading Arrow, Hicks, Hayek, Kalecki, Pigou and others.

Mihályi records that he was elected to the Hungarian Academy of Sciences in 1976, took an occasional professorial appointment at Harvard in 1986, the Allie S. Freed Professorship there in 1991 and retired as emeritus in 2002, while spending half the academic year in Budapest and half in Cambridge, Massachusetts. He was a founding fellow of Collegium Budapest and Honorary Professor Emeritus at Corvinus University of Budapest. After a long illness he died at home, surrounded by his family, on 18 October 2021, aged ninety-three; the same date appears in the abstract and opening paragraph of Mihályi's article, and Kornai's homepage now carries a mourning notice. The in memoriam contains an apparent typographical error, giving the Collegium Budapest fellowship as running "from 1912 to 2011"; that is not repeated here as a fact.

## Intellectual tradition

Kornai wrote from the tradition of comparative economic systems rather than from Marxism, and he described his method in his own terms. In a 2013 Oxford University Press Q&A he said, "My whole career has shaped my thinking 'comparatively.'" He added that he was born and schooled under capitalism, lived through four decades of socialism and then observed its collapse, living alternately in Budapest and Cambridge and feeling "at home in both". His position toward mainstream economics was, in his phrase, "one foot in the mainstream and one foot out": he used mathematical models and econometrics himself but chose verbal argument when comparing "great systems", citing Tjalling Koopmans's trade-off between relevance and rigour ([OUP interview, 2013](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/about/interviews/Kornai2013_OUP_interview.pdf)).

His early political identity was communist, and the break is documented in his own writing. A memoir chapter is titled "How I Became a Communist, 1945–1947" and a later one "Breaking with Marxian political economy"; Mihályi dates the disillusionment to 1953–1956. In 2009 Kornai wrote that he had expunged Marxist prejudices against "bourgeois economics" during his self-education and had come close to calling himself a neoclassical, held back by a determination to remain intellectually independent. He nevertheless kept Marx as an object of study. In "Karl Marx through the Eyes of an East-European Intellectual" he made the contested claim that "the plan of Marx was indeed implemented by the socialist system (not some fine utopia, but what existed and I lived through)". That claim rests on his reading of Marx's own passages on public ownership and the "anarchy" of the market; it is not a claim that all Marxists accept ([Kornai, *Social Research*, 2009](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/konyvek_cikkek/w20825/KORNAI2009_Marx_through_the_eyes_-_Social_Research.pdf)).

His mature "system paradigm" was explicitly a comparison of ideal types. In the 2016 *Acta Oeconomica* article he said his "type" had the same meaning as what Max Weber calls an "ideal type", but that he avoided Weber's term because "ideal" sounded normative. The paradigm asks which features are system-specific and how elements of a political and economic edifice fit together, rather than describing one organisation or institution; in 2012 he wrote that most such partial work examined a single institution while his books examined whole systems. He placed his work near institutional and behavioural economics and connected the soft budget constraint to moral hazard and time inconsistency while insisting it was not reducible to either. No opened source records him accepting a label such as "neoliberal" or "Marxist" in his mature period; "one foot in, one foot out" is his own description ([Kornai, *Acta Oeconomica*, 2016](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/konyvek_cikkek/w20825/Kornai2016-System-paradigm-revisited--Acta.pdf)).

## Major contributions

Five arguments recur in the opened texts, with a sixth term that entered the transition debate.

The first was the distinction between resource-constrained and demand-constrained systems, set out in the 1979 *Econometrica* presidential address. Kornai identified three upper constraints on a firm's attempt to expand: physical resources, demand at given prices and the budget. In a demand-constrained system buyers' demand binds even though available resources would permit more output; he called classical capitalism the demand-constrained case, connecting it both to Marx's contradiction between the drive to expand production and limited purchasing power and to Keynes. In a resource-constrained system physical bottlenecks bind instead, and he classed the "classical" socialist economy as this type. This was a framework for comparing systems by which constraint actually bit, not a claim that every firm in either system behaved identically ([Kornai, *Econometrica*, 1979](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/konyvek_cikkek/w20825/Kornai1979_Resource-constrained_vs._demand-constrained_systems_-_Econometrica.pdf)).

His second contribution was the soft budget constraint: the expectation, built up over time, that a firm's losses will be covered by the state, so that its spending is not finally limited by its own earnings. He introduced the idea in the 1979 article and elaborated it in *Economics of Shortage* (1980) and the 1986 *Kyklos* paper. In 1979 he wrote: "A budget constraint is hard if it is asserted with iron discipline: the firm can spend only as much money as it has." A soft constraint appeared when losses were compensated: "The state is a universal insurance company which compensates the damaged sooner or later for every loss. The paternalistic state guarantees automatically the survival of the firm." He insisted this was an ex ante behavioural regularity and a matter of expectation, not a bookkeeping identity, and that profit sharing was compatible with a soft constraint. The 1986 restatement made the expectations explicit: "The 'softening' of the budget constraint appears when the strict relationship between expenditure and earnings has been relaxed, because excess expenditure over earnings will be paid by some other institution, typically by the State", and "the higher the subjective probability that excess expenditure will be covered by external assistance, the softer the budget constraint." He listed soft subsidies, soft taxation, soft credit and soft administrative prices as distinct means, warned that "hardness of the budget constraint is not a synonym for profit-maximization", and called the concept a metaphor for the relaxation of financial discipline ([Kornai, *Kyklos*, 1986](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/konyvek_cikkek/KornaiJanosEletmuve_Cikkek_0480.pdf)).

The third contribution was the theory of chronic shortage: a persistent, self-reproducing scarcity of goods and labour, rather than a run of isolated failures. In 1979 he wrote that consumer-goods shortage, housing shortage, supply disturbances, investment-goods shortage and labour shortage sprang from common causes: "Certain social conditions, and certain properties of the economic mechanism ... constantly reproduce shortage." A soft budget constraint made a firm's demand as buyer "almost insatiable"; as seller it faced queues and pressed to produce more; bottlenecks led to hoarding, so "shortage breeds shortage"; and managers' identification with their firms produced "investment hunger", reinforced because "in the case of the soft budget constraint, nothing keeps the firm from investment." In his 2014 retrospective he revised the causal weight: in 1980 he had treated the soft budget constraint as the "prime mover", but he came to regard it as "a necessary but not a sufficient condition" and "a most important intermediate explanatory factor" in the second block of a longer causal chain ([Kornai, *Acta Oeconomica*, 2014](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/konyvek_cikkek/w20825/Kornai2014_SBC_-_Acta.pdf)). The full text of *Economics of Shortage* was not read for this dossier; these formulations come from Kornai's own later articles and his archive.

The fourth was the critique of general equilibrium theory in *Anti-Equilibrium* (1971), which he later called "the most ambitious enterprise of my career as a researcher" while admitting that he had "undertaken something bigger and more difficult than what I was able to accomplish". Vahabi's 2018 study records the shift: Kornai first rejected general equilibrium theory and advocated disequilibrium, then in *Economics of Shortage* distinguished Walrasian equilibrium from the "normal state" or Marshallian equilibrium, and in the 2006 memoir described *Anti-Equilibrium* as a "failure" while accepting general equilibrium theory as a benchmark of an ideal competitive market, but not as a description of reality ([Vahabi, 2018](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/about/reviews/Vahabi2018-Kornai-general-equilibrium-theory--Acta.pdf)). The memoir's year is not stable across Kornai's own records: his publication database gives 2006 and his online-publications page gives 2007 for the downloadable e-book, and no choice is made here.

The fifth was the "system paradigm" together with the affinity thesis: bureaucratic coordination has a natural affinity to state ownership, and market coordination to private ownership, so markets cannot be used as neutral instruments to prop up state ownership. Mihályi's in memoriam credits Kornai with a "supermarket metaphor" against third-way hybrid schemes: social systems are not assembled like goods in a trolley. His last major analytical book, *Dynamism, Rivalry, and the Surplus Economy* (2013), extended the comparison to capitalism as a surplus economy, with chronic excess capacity, stocks and unemployment alongside innovation and rivalry. He also coined or popularised "transformational recession" for the post-1989 output collapse, a term recorded in the title of a 2014 *Journal of Comparative Economics* article in his own archive.

## Analysis of capitalism

Kornai's account of capitalism was comparative and deliberately unsentimental. In 2012 he summarised the two pure models: "One is a shortage economy, the other a surplus economy; one is marked by signs of excess demand, the other by those of excess supply. One displays a chronic shortage of labour, the other chronic unemployment. The main mechanism of allocation in one is bureaucratic coordination and in the other market coordination. One has public ownership as its dominant property form and the other private ownership." In the same article he wrote that "Capitalism is not a shortage economy, but a surplus economy. It is marked not by overly tight production plans that force enterprises to produce more than they are really capable of, but by chronic unutilized capacity. Retail shelves are not empty, but packed." He contrasted years-long waits for a telephone line with phone companies competing for subscribers ([Kornai, *Acta Oeconomica*, 2012](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/konyvek_cikkek/w20825/Kornai2012_ActaOeconomica.pdf)).

The 2016 system article gives the mature taxonomy. Capitalism's primary characteristics are the ruling group's ensuring the dominance of private property and market coordination, private property as the dominant form of ownership, and market coordination as the dominant mechanism. Secondary characteristics are a surplus economy or buyers' market; labour surplus as the dominant state of the labour market; fast technical progress and frequent revolutionary innovation; high income inequality; hard budget constraints for organisations in a broad sphere; and corruption flowing mostly from seller to buyer. Kornai explicitly denied that repression or incompetence could be system characteristics, because they appear under both systems. The symmetry is a theoretical construct: he said real structures are never pure and elements of one system creep into the other.

In the 2013 interview he stated plainly: "I stand by capitalism." He qualified it at once: "mine is no blind enthusiasm or uncritical admiration", and capitalism has "more than one inescapable drawback, intrinsic to the nature of the system", which its advocates must accept soberly by limiting damage. In 2012 he acknowledged that the shortage economy was "ultimately more egalitarian than the surplus economy", a point Nuti also reports from the 2013 book, and he treated unemployment and inequality as inseparable from capitalist dynamism rather than as policy errors.

His view of the relation between capitalism and democracy is restrictive and politically contested. In 2016 he wrote: "As said earlier, capitalism can operate without democracy, but the statement cannot be reversed. Democracy cannot operate without capitalism – 'democratic socialism' is impossible." He immediately added that this depends on the definitions being used and that capitalism is a necessary but not sufficient condition for democracy. He cited Weimar Germany and Putin's Russia as evidence that democracy can collapse into dictatorship, and argued that China had become capitalist while remaining a one-party dictatorship.

Kornai also argued that the soft budget constraint was not confined to socialism. In 2012 he wrote that such phenomena in countries that never had communist rule were "capitalism's own product", not a legacy of socialism, and he invoked Hayek's *The Road to Serfdom* while conceding that Hayek "may have gone too far". He applied the syndrome to hospitals, schools, banks, local governments, agriculture, "rustbelt" industries and national governments, and used the 2008 financial crisis as the contemporary test case, contrasting a European tendency toward hardening with an American tendency toward softening. In 2009 he published "The soft budget constraint syndrome and the global financial crisis: Some warnings from an East European economist", listed on his own site.

## Analysis of socialism

Kornai's socialism was the actually existing party-state system, not an ideal. In 2012 he explained that whereas *Economics of Shortage* had deliberately avoided the word "party", *The Socialist System* opened with the party's role, political power and ideology: "the origin and operation of the socialist system can only be understood truly by starting from the role of political power and official ideology." In 2016 he defined socialism by the ruling Communist Party's enforcement of public ownership and bureaucratic coordination, state ownership as the dominant property form, bureaucratic coordination as the dominant mechanism, a shortage economy or sellers' market, labour shortage, slow technical progress, low income inequality, soft budget constraints across a broad sphere, and corruption flowing mostly from buyer to seller. The question of what ownership meant in practice is treated separately in [Who Owned What: Legal Title, Control and the Meaning of Social Ownership](/ownership-and-control).

The causal story ran from political power through paternalism and the soft budget constraint to expansion drive and shortage. In 2012 Kornai described table 15.1 of *The Socialist System* at page 361 of the English edition: the undivided power of the Marxist-Leninist party and the official ideology occupied the first block, forced growth and the chronic shortage economy were in the fifth, and "paternalism and the soft budget constraint syndrome appear in the penultimate Block 4." That placement was his answer to readers who treated the soft budget constraint and paternalism as the deepest causes. He made the same point about the 1980 book in 2014: self-censorship had led him to hint at "certain social relations, institutional attributes" and Western readers took paternalism to be the whole explanation. The page reference is Kornai's own, given in the 2012 article; the full text of the book was not read for this dossier.

On reform, Kornai argued that partial marketisation could not remove shortage while state ownership and party power remained. The soft budget constraint persisted in reformed "market socialism" in Hungary, Yugoslavia and China, in his 1986 and 2014 accounts; only a legal or tolerated private sector with hard constraints, free prices and imports filled the gaps. In 2014 he wrote that private activity in 1970s and 1980s Hungary, namely household farming, sideline activities and *maszek* work, supplied food and personal services at free prices and ended shortage in those sectors, and that China had a large state sector with soft constraints but no general chronic shortage because private firms and imports flooded the market. The affinity thesis was his reason for rejecting market socialism as a stable hybrid. The wider question of whether allocation without markets was possible is covered in [Prices Without Markets: The Calculation Question](/economic-calculation-debate).

He did not write a blueprint for a desirable socialism. *The Road to a Free Economy* (1990) argued for transition to a capitalist market economy, and "Market Socialism Revisited" (1993) is a criticism rather than a design. What the opened sources contain is a normative preference for a system with hard budget constraints, private property, market coordination and democracy, plus an analysis of why the socialist system could not generate those outcomes by incremental reform. His 2009 claim that Marx's plan was actually implemented in Soviet-type socialism is a claim about intellectual and institutional continuity, not an endorsement.

## Recommended reading

These are the five works the research dossier selects as the best representation of Kornai's argument.

- *Economics of Shortage* (1980) — the book that made shortage a general theory of the socialist firm's incentives rather than a list of empty shelves; Kornai himself judged it his most influential work.
- "The Soft Budget Constraint" (1986) — the clearest short statement of his own definition and of the four ways a state rescue becomes built into managers' expectations.
- "Resource-Constrained Versus Demand-Constrained Systems" (1979) — the compact theoretical origin of the distinction between an economy limited by buyers and one limited by physical bottlenecks.
- *The Socialist System: The Political Economy of Communism* (1992) — his own choice as his most important book, adding party power and ideology to the economic analysis.
- *Dynamism, Rivalry, and the Surplus Economy* (2013) — the mature comparison that gives capitalism its own systemic diagnosis, not merely the absence of socialism.

## Intellectual influences

Kornai's own writing gives an unusually clear record of influences. In the memoir he lists the writers he studied systematically after 1957: Kenneth Arrow, Arrow-Karlin-Scarf, Kenneth Boulding, Walter Eucken, Ragnar Frisch, Gottfried von Haberler, Friedrich von Hayek, John Hicks, Michał Kalecki, Arthur Pigou, Paul Samuelson, Heinrich von Stackelberg and Jan Tinbergen. He began with Samuelson's textbook in German translation and a three-volume West German textbook by Erich Schneider, then moved to monographs and journals, taking notes and asking what each result would mean for a socialist economy. Keynes, he says, was read later. He describes his early attraction to neoclassical economics and his refusal to immerse himself uncritically in an "-ism" after his Marxist youth.

The Lange–Hayek debate shaped his thinking about property, incentives and knowledge. He read Oskar Lange's market-socialism model as a brilliant Walrasian synthesis, found Hayek's practical critique of centralised knowledge "thought provoking", and wrote that Hayek "directed my attention to the close tie between knowledge, incentives, and property". He was careful not to project his later rejection of Lange back onto 1957: at the time he could not adjudicate between the two positions. György Péter, a Hungarian reform economist whose ideas resembled Lange's, and the Yugoslav system were additional early influences, as was Abram Bergson's survey of the literature.

Marx remained a formative presence even after the break. Kornai read Marx, Engels, Lenin and Stalin in German before Hungarian translations were available, and the 2009 essay treats Marx as "the great pioneer and incomparable practitioner of the system paradigm", while rejecting what he saw as the practical outcomes of Marx's plan. Max Weber enters explicitly in the 2016 article as the source of the "ideal type" idea; Schumpeter appears in the 2012 and 2016 discussions of innovation and creative destruction. Nicholas Kaldor, a fellow Hungarian, is cited alongside the critique of equilibrium theory, and Mihályi's in memoriam pairs Kaldor and Kornai on economics without equilibrium. Among collaborators and younger economists, the archive and the 2003 and 2014 studies name Jörgen Weibull, Béla Martos, Tamás Lipták, Ágnes Matits, András Simonovits, Eric Maskin and Gérard Roland. Andrea Szegő is named in the memoir as a critic who "distanced herself" from his theories.

## Influence and legacy

The soft budget constraint is the clearest measure of influence. In the 2003 *Journal of Economic Literature* survey co-authored with Maskin and Roland, Kornai wrote that the expression had become "a familiar part of the economics lexicon", that there was "hardly ... a report on transition — by the World Bank, the EBRD, or other agencies" in the preceding decade without "soft-" and "hard budget constraint" appearing prominently, and that the concept was increasingly invoked beyond socialist and transition economies, including in the East Asian banking crises of the 1990s. The same survey records a formal literature growing out of Dewatripont and Maskin (1995), and distinguishes the policy and theoretical uses of the idea. Kornai's 2014 study traced a further "canonisation" of the concept and its relation to institutional, behavioural, moral-hazard and time-inconsistency theories ([Kornai, Maskin and Roland, 2003](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/konyvek_cikkek/KornaiJanosEletmuve_Cikkek_0651.pdf)).

Mihályi's in memoriam calls *The Socialist System* "the most adequate and convincing explanatory narrative of the worldwide communist system", reports that Kornai's books were translated into twenty languages, and records the ten-volume Hungarian collected works, of which *Economics of Shortage* and *The Socialist System* were the first two volumes. It also notes his role as a policy adviser around the Hungarian transition and as a commentator on China, whose "U-turn" under Xi Jinping Kornai publicly declared in 2018. The in memoriam states that Kornai was never awarded the Nobel Prize and that the reasons remain confidential. Chenggang Xu's 2017 *Journal of Economic Literature* review treats the 2013 book as filling a gap in the analysis of capitalism's dynamic nature, while posing theoretical and empirical challenges and raising the case of hybrid capitalism.

For this book, the useful legacy is analytical rather than a verdict. The existing chapter on [planning, incentives and shortage](/planning-incentives-and-shortage) assembles primary documents on raised quotas, worker letters, empty shelves, the 1953 SED retreat and later GDR shortage reports; it explicitly says the causes are contested and offers no single ranking of explanations. Kornai supplies a set of mechanisms, among them taut plans, investment hunger, soft rescue expectations, hoarding, queues and forced substitution, that can be set beside those documents. His own 2014 revision matters here: he no longer treated the soft budget constraint as sufficient for general shortage, and Nuti's objection gives the chapter a live scholarly dispute rather than a settled doctrine. The documents establish conflicts over norms, pay, rations and supply; Kornai's framework organises them, but it does not by itself prove that every enterprise expected a bailout, and no enterprise minutes from those cases were opened for this dossier. No source opened here establishes how many workers or firms shared the experiences described.

## Criticisms and controversies

The most substantive Marxist objection opened here is D. Mario Nuti's. Nuti is an emeritus professor at Sapienza University of Rome who identifies Maurice Dobb, a Marxist economist, as his teacher. In "Kornai: Shortage versus Surplus Economies" (2018) he states that he does not dispute the reality of chronic shortage in Soviet-type economies or its consequences, including repressed inflation, over-full employment, hidden inequality and the failure of market-socialist reforms. His "residual major disagreement" is causal. Kornai originally attributed shortage primarily to soft budget constraints; Nuti replies that "soft budgets can explain open inflation, but are neither necessary nor sufficient to explain shortages", because shortage requires prices below market-clearing levels. He credits Stanislaw Gomulka (1985) with showing that shortage depends on the degree of budget softness relative to the degree of price flexibility, and reports Kornai's reply. He argues that Kornai's later appeal to self-fulfilling expectations introduces a subjective element that cannot be generalised to a whole state-enterprise system, and that China's large state sector with soft constraints but no general shortage fits his price-based account better. He concludes that Kornai's claim of complete price inelasticity of excess demand "remains a debatable conjecture". Nuti also calls Kornai's preference for the surplus economy a value choice, while noting that Kornai acknowledges unemployment, greater inequality and corruption as costs ([Nuti, 2018](https://www.kornai-janos.hu/media/about/reviews/Nuti2018-Kornai-Shortage-versus-surplus-economies.pdf)).

A second critique from within heterodox political economy is Andrea Szegő's 1991 article, "The Logic of a Shortage Economy: A Critique of Kornai from a Kaleckian Macroeconomic Perspective", *Journal of Post Keynesian Economics* 13(3), pp. 328–336, DOI 10.1080/01603477.1991.11489852. The record was confirmed through Crossref and through Kornai's own memoir bibliography, which says she "later, in 1991, criticized *Economics of Shortage* from a Kaleckian point of view, and distanced herself from my theories". The article text was behind a publisher paywall and was not opened, so no claim is made here about the details of her argument beyond its title and Kornai's characterisation.

Kornai's own revisions concede part of this ground. In 2014 he wrote that in 1980 he viewed the soft budget constraint as "the prime mover", singled out Gomulka as a critic, and changed position under the pressure of debate and of what he observed in Hungary: the soft budget constraint became "a necessary but not a sufficient condition" and "a most important intermediate explanatory factor". He also admitted that self-censorship had prevented him from naming the two factors he later thought decisive, namely the socialist system's refusal to tolerate a free private producing sector and the role of unrestricted imports. In 2012 he wrote: "I wrote the truth, but not the whole truth", and said Western colleagues misread his veiled reference to "institutional attributes" as a claim about paternalism alone. These are retrospective admissions, not independent verification of the 1980 text, whose full text was not read here.

Other scholarly objections concern scope and method. Bernard Chavance's 2015 review questions "the polar opposition of capitalism and socialism": at a higher level of abstraction the two systems share monetary and wage-labour features and can be seen as species of one genus; at a lower level, the diversity of national capitalisms and the post-war "golden age" of quasi-full employment and limited inequality qualify the claim that surplus and unemployment are genetic traits of capitalism. Chavance also notes that Kornai criticises declining coherence and affinity under capitalism less readily than he does under socialism. Mehrdad Vahabi's 2018 study argues that Kornai's self-described "failure" with *Anti-Equilibrium* was really the cost of a "half-in, half-out" relation to mainstream economics: his heterodox soft budget constraint was later absorbed into formal microeconomics, while the institutionalist system paradigm remains a heterodox research project. Steve Keen's 2018 essay takes the opposite view, arguing that *Anti-Equilibrium* was ahead of its time and that system dynamics offers an escape from equilibrium-dominated modelling. These two readings of the same book disagree, and neither is treated here as settled.

Methodological criticism is partly self-reported. In the 2013 interview Kornai acknowledged that he could not formalise the comparison of "great systems" and chose verbal propositions and conjectures; Vahabi treats that choice as theoretically consequential. Mihályi's in memoriam says the hoped-for paradigm shift did not occur and that the new generation of economists has hardly heard of *Anti-Equilibrium*. The political claim that "democratic socialism" is impossible is another point of controversy: Nuti reports it as Kornai's personal value choice, and Kornai himself immediately qualified it as dependent on his definitions and on what "in time" means. Finally, the 2013 television interview records Kornai saying he was not against centralisation as such, that an army, a large firm and a state all need it, and that the question is what is centralised and by what means. That nuance is often absent from summaries of his position.

## Related thinkers

- **Karl Marx** — the categories Kornai first absorbed and later rejected; the 2009 essay treats Marx as the great pioneer of the system paradigm while rejecting the practical outcomes of his plan.
- **Léon Walras, Oskar Lange and Abba Lerner** — the market-socialism synthesis he admired; he could not adjudicate between Lange and Hayek in 1957.
- **Friedrich Hayek** — the critique of centralised knowledge that pushed him toward the link between knowledge, incentives and property.
- **Paul Samuelson, John Hicks, Kenneth Arrow, Michał Kalecki and Nicholas Kaldor** — the self-taught toolkit; Kaldor is paired with Kornai on economics without equilibrium.
- **Max Weber and Joseph Schumpeter** — the ideal-type method and the vocabulary of innovation and creative destruction.
- **György Péter** — the link to Hungarian reform economics.
- **Stanislaw Gomulka and Andrea Szegő** — early critics of the soft-budget account of shortage; Gomulka's 1985 point is credited by Nuti and Kornai, while Szegő's 1991 Kaleckian article was paywalled and not opened.
- **D. Mario Nuti** — locates shortage in below-market-clearing prices and calls Kornai's preference for the surplus economy a value choice.
- **Bernard Chavance** — questions the polar contrast between the two systems.
- **Mehrdad Vahabi and Steve Keen** — disagree about the significance of *Anti-Equilibrium*, and neither reading is treated as settled here.
- **Chenggang Xu** — engages the 2013 account of capitalism.
- **Eric Maskin, Gérard Roland and Sergei Guriev** — extended the soft-budget framework into formal theory and state capitalism.
- **Péter Mihályi and Ádám Kerényi** — documented his publication record and legacy.
