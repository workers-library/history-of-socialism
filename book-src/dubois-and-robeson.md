W. E. B. Du Bois and Paul Robeson left different kinds of records. Du Bois wrote books, essays, editorials and an autobiography; Robeson left articles, speeches, greetings to conferences and a long answer to a congressional committee ([Du Bois, *Dusk of Dawn*](https://archive.org/details/duskofdawnessayt00dubo); [MIA index of *The Crisis*](https://www.marxists.org/history/usa/workers/civil-rights/crisis/index.htm); [MIA Robeson archive](https://www.marxists.org/archive/robeson/)). Both men used what they wrote to argue that the liberation of Black people could not be separated from the struggle against capitalism, racism and war. This chapter follows those records in roughly the order their authors produced them, then asks what the two bodies of work share and where they differ. It treats each text as an argument made at a particular moment, not as a transparent report of what its author saw or did.

The chief witnesses are the two men themselves. Du Bois's published work spans more than six decades and changes markedly over that span, so a sentence from 1903 cannot simply be joined to a sentence from 1935 as if the author had never revised his views. Robeson's most vivid account of Soviet life is a newspaper article written within weeks of a reception in Moscow and published by a party newspaper, a document that tells us much about his reception and almost nothing independent about conditions in the Soviet Union. For both men, later memorial articles written by political associates are a third category: useful evidence of how the movement remembered them, not neutral biography. Independent scholarly work is used here to set these claims beside the findings of historians who were not participants.

## W. E. B. Du Bois, 1868 to 1963

### A scholar formed in New England and Berlin

Du Bois grew up in Great Barrington, Massachusetts, a New England village where racial inequality existed but had not yet hardened into the all-powerful system he would later study in the South (Lewis, *Biography of a Race*, 1993, ch. 1). That difference shaped the early work. He approached what contemporaries called the Negro problem as a question of citizenship and nationhood, and his first major book was not a memoir but a piece of empirical social research. His education took him from Fisk to Harvard and then to the University of Berlin, where he encountered German social science and the historical school of economics (Lewis, *Biography of a Race*, 1993, chs. 2–3). Those studies did not make him a Marxist in the 1890s, but they gave him the habit of treating racial inequality as a historical and economic problem rather than a moral failing of individuals.

The first decisive publication was *The Conservation of Races*, a paper delivered to the American Negro Academy in 1897. Its announcement described the educated minority's responsibility to "take concerted steps" for the uplift of the race, the idea later known as the Talented Tenth. The stress in that phrasing falls on agency and on a duty to act rather than on charity. It also locates the agency in a small educated stratum, and Du Bois spent much of the next thirty years testing and revising that claim.

*The Philadelphia Negro* (1899) was a door-to-door study of the city's Black population ([*The Philadelphia Negro*, 1899](https://archive.org/details/philadelphianegr00dubo)). Du Bois did not simply write about Black people; he counted, interviewed and mapped them, making their conditions visible to a discipline that had largely ignored them. The method established his reputation as a sociologist of Black urban life and prepared the way for the much larger historical argument of *Black Reconstruction*. David Levering Lewis's two-volume biography, *W. E. B. Du Bois: Biography of a Race, 1868–1919* (1993) and *W. E. B. Du Bois: The Fight for Equality and the American Century, 1919–1963* (2000), remains the standard scholarly account of how that reputation and that argument developed.

### *The Souls of Black Folk* and the color line

*The Souls of Black Folk* appeared in 1903. Its short "Forethought" ends with the sentence that gave Du Bois his lifelong theme: "the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color line." He was making a claim about which problem would define the coming century, not a complaint about individual prejudice. The argument linked the American South to Africa, Asia and the Caribbean, and it anticipated his later work against empire.

The architecture of the book carries the argument. It moves from the inner life of "Spiritual Strivings" through the history of emancipation and the strategic dispute with Booker T. Washington, then through education, the Black Belt, religion, family and biography, and ends with the spirituals, which Du Bois calls the Sorrow Songs and treats as the stored memory of a people. The sequence matters because it refuses to separate scholarship, political argument and music into distinct compartments. That refusal helps explain why Du Bois and Robeson, despite their different professions, could recognize one another as allies.

The opening chapter describes double consciousness as "a sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity." The paragraph that follows is less often quoted and more revealing about Du Bois's purpose. He writes of a longing "to merge his double self into a better and truer self," and insists that neither older self should be lost: "He would not Africanize America... He would not bleach his Negro soul in a flood of white Americanism." The aim was synthesis. The early Du Bois was neither a separatist nor a nationalist in the later sense, and the claim that Black people had a contribution to make to civilization rather than a grievance to be redressed became the seed of his Pan-African thought.

The chapter on Booker T. Washington contains the phrase that dogged him thereafter. Asking whether advanced education would be withheld from the gifted tenth of the race, he wrote that "by refusing to give this Talented Tenth the key to knowledge, can any sane man imagine that they will lightly lay aside their yearning and contentedly become hewers of wood and drawers of water?" This is an argument against an education designed to confine Black people to manual labour, not a claim that only ten percent are fully human. The later Du Bois saw the flaw in the elitist formulation. In *Dusk of Dawn* and in the 1930s he located the revolutionary subject in the Black masses rather than in a cultivated minority ([*Dusk of Dawn*, 1940](https://archive.org/details/duskofdawnessayt00dubo); [*Black Reconstruction*, 1935](https://archive.org/details/blackreconstruct0000dubo)). What did not change was the insistence that the right to knowledge was a right, not a privilege granted by others.

A later chapter answers the question "What have we done for this country?" with a reversal of the burden of proof. The nation, Du Bois writes, received from Black Americans "a gift of story and song," "the gift of sweat and brawn," and "the gift of the Spirit," and he asks "Your country? How came it yours? Before the Pilgrims landed we were here." William L. Patterson's 1969 tribute to Robeson reports that Robeson quoted this passage, and the tribute prints it, with a slightly different wording, as part of his own political testament. The exchange tells us how the next generation read Du Bois: a claim about who had built the country, turned into a claim about who had the right to define its future.

### The institution builder

Du Bois helped convene the Niagara Movement in 1905 and was among the founders of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People in 1909 and 1910. He edited its magazine, *The Crisis*, until the mid-1930s. The [MIA index of *The Crisis*](https://www.marxists.org/history/usa/workers/civil-rights/crisis/index.htm) describes the magazine as pursuing "the world-old dream of human brotherhood" by bearing witness to "the danger of race prejudice" and to "inter-racial relations" at home and abroad. The same index records that circulation "peaked at about 100,000" within nine years, and that the magazine combined accounts of Black achievement with reports of discrimination and lynching. For a quarter of a century, an account of Black life appeared each month that did not pass through a white editorial filter. The color line was not only an idea Du Bois described; it was a publishing operation he helped sustain.

His Pan-African organizing supplied the institutional bridge between the 1903 claim and the anticolonial revolutions of the 1950s and 1960s. He worked through the 1900 London conference, the 1919 Paris Congress and the congresses of 1921, 1923 and 1927, and he convened the 1945 Manchester Congress, where the demand for African independence was framed in the language of mass struggle and where the young Kwame Nkrumah was present. Robeson's 1955 greeting to the Bandung Conference, discussed below, shows what that organizing looked like in its later phase: a gathering of Asian and African nations treated as a single political field. The two men's work converged most completely here.

### War, whiteness and the turn to Marxism

In May 1915, with Europe at war, Du Bois published "The African Roots of War" in the *Atlantic Monthly*. Its argument survives in an [MIA-hosted review](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/atc/4700.html) that quotes it. Du Bois wrote that "it was the competition for the labor of yellow, brown, and black folk" that caused the war, and compared the German invasion of Belgium with the Belgian record in the Congo: "what Belgium suffers now is not half, not even a tenth what she has done to the Black Congo." These quotations come from the review rather than from the 1915 magazine, a distinction that matters because the review selects and frames them. The argument is that violence Europe had exported to Africa and Asia had returned to Europe itself. It became the core of his later anti-imperialism.

The same year produced *The Negro*, which treated Black people as a people with a history rather than as a problem to be solved. Between *Souls* and the 1930s he also published the cotton-belt novel *The Quest of the Silver Fleece* (1911), the biography *John Brown* (1923) and *The Gift of Black Folk* (1924), carrying the same argument into fiction, biography and popular history. *The Negro* rejects both biological racism and the view that race does not matter because everyone is formally equal. Du Bois would radicalize that position into the theory of the wages of whiteness: the idea that white workers receive a material and psychological bribe from the subordinate status of Black workers.

*Darkwater: Voices from within the Veil* (1920) turned the gaze onto whiteness itself. In "The Souls of White Folk," Du Bois argues that whiteness was made in recent history: "The discovery of personal whiteness among the world's peoples is a very modern thing... The ancient world would have laughed at such a distinction." The passage matters because it treats a racial category as something produced by historical circumstances, not as a fact of nature. It also anticipates a point Robeson made before the House Committee on Un-American Activities in 1956, that his own success could not stand in for "thousands of Negro families" still trapped in poverty: "My father was a slave, and I have cousins who are sharecroppers, and I do not see my success in terms of myself."

### *Black Reconstruction* and the wages of whiteness

A central book of Du Bois's middle period is *Black Reconstruction in America: An Essay Toward a History of the Part Which Black Folk Played in the Attempt to Reconstruct Democracy in America, 1860–1880* (1935). The chapter titled "The General Strike" states the thesis: enslaved people, by withdrawing their labour and fleeing to Union lines, made emancipation their own act rather than a gift from others. The book appeared in 1935, before the Moscow Trials began in 1936, and it was ignored or dismissed by many historians on publication. A later generation reversed that verdict. The African American Intellectual History Society's essay ["When Slaves Go on Strike"](https://www.aaihs.org/when-slaves-go-on-strike) notes both the book's subsequent influence on American historians and the criticism that Du Bois relied too heavily on a Marxist expectation of how history must turn out. Lewis's biography likewise treats the book as a major intervention rather than as a settled triumph.

A [study guide to *Reconstruction*](https://www.marxists.org/history/usa/pubs/sojournertruth/reconstudy.pdf), published by the Sojourner Truth Organization and hosted by the Marxists Internet Archive, gives the book's Marxist frame in accessible form. The guide says Du Bois treated the Civil War and Reconstruction above all as a labour movement and calls his account "the kernel and meaning of the labor movement in the United States." That phrase is the guide's wording, not a quotation from Du Bois's book. The guide also quotes Du Bois's own description of Reconstruction as "one of the most extraordinary experiments of Marxism that the world, before the Russian revolution, had seen," and his account of how the experiment ended: "When white laborers were convinced that the degradation of Negro labor was more fundamental than the Uplift of white labor, the end was in sight." The last sentence states the theory of the psychological wage in compressed form. The book ends with a demand for "Land, Light and Leading for slaves black, brown, yellow and white, under a dictatorship of the proletariat," meaning political rule by the working class.

Du Bois did not apply Marxist categories mechanically. He shifted the revolutionary subject from the white industrial worker of orthodox Marxism to the Black worker, and he explained the defeat of Reconstruction as a betrayal by white labour, which chose the wage of racial superiority over class solidarity. That argument connects him to the Marxist tradition more deeply than a simple label would: he accepted the centrality of class while insisting that, in the United States, class had to be understood through race. The parallel with Robeson lies in the shared conclusion that racism was the main obstacle to working-class unity in the United States, and in the shared political cost of saying so.

### Prosecution, the peace movement and Ghana

In 1950 Du Bois became chairman of the Peace Information Center, which circulated the Stockholm Appeal for nuclear disarmament. In 1951 he and four colleagues were indicted in federal court for failing to register as a foreign agent, on the theory that the appeal was foreign propaganda ([*In Battle for Peace*, 1952](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.54190)). The prosecution collapsed when the government's own witness testified that Du Bois had not been paid by a foreign power. His passport was withheld for years, a restriction described both in his own *In Battle for Peace* (1952) and in Lewis's account of these years.

Robeson wrote from the same political position in "Thoughts on Winning the Stalin Peace Prize," published in *Freedom* in January 1953. "I cannot accept this award in a personal way," he wrote, and he described "the heroic efforts of the friendly peoples in Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Albania, Romania, Bulgaria, great, new China and North Korea." The two men were not only parallel figures. A 1958 *Political Affairs* review describes the Council on African Affairs as an organization "to which Robeson, along with Dr. W. E. B. Du Bois and Dr. Alphaeus Hunton, devoted many years popularizing the cause of African and colonial freedom in this country" ([*Political Affairs*, April 1958](https://www.marxists.org/history/usa/pubs/political-affairs/1958-04-37-4-political-affairs.pdf)). That shared institution is the concrete link between the Pan-African organizer of the 1920s and the singer who addressed Bandung in 1955.

In 1961, at the age of ninety-three, Du Bois joined the Communist Party USA. Young communists who took his name, the Du Bois Clubs, became the party's youth organization in the 1960s. Later that year he accepted Kwame Nkrumah's invitation to live in Ghana, where he worked on the *Encyclopedia Africana* until his death in Accra on 27 August 1963. The distance from the Talented Tenth of 1903 to the Communist Party of 1961 is real, but it is a distance travelled in one direction: from an educated minority as bearer of the race's destiny toward the Black masses as makers of their own emancipation.

## Paul Robeson, 1898 to 1976

### Fame and its use

Robeson achieved fame in three fields before he became a political figure. He was a scholar-athlete at Rutgers, earned a law degree at Columbia, and became a leading stage and concert performer, known especially for *The Emperor Jones* and *Othello*. The [MIA Encyclopedia of Marxism](https://www.marxists.org/glossary/people/r/o.htm#robeson-paul) describes him as the first world-famous singer and actor to become a political activist during his peak performing years. It is a movement reference work rather than an independent biography, so it is best read alongside Martin Duberman's *Paul Robeson* (Knopf, 1988) and Sheila Tully Boyle and Andrew Bunie's *Paul Robeson: The Years of Promise and Achievement* (University of Massachusetts Press, 2001). These scholarly biographies disagree about emphasis, but all three treat his politics as central to his life rather than as an accident of celebrity.

His 1956 testimony before the House Committee on Un-American Activities shows how he used his public standing. Asked about prejudice, the committee chairman pointed to Robeson's own success. Robeson answered with the athlete's retort, "We beat Lehigh," then turned the argument around: "the success of a few Negroes, including myself or Jackie Robinson can make up... for seven hundred dollars a year for thousands of Negro families in the South." He added, "I have sacrificed literally hundreds of thousands, if not millions, of dollars for what I believe in." Robeson did not renounce his fame; he spent it. The figure is his own estimate of what his positions cost him, not an audited sum.

The line most closely associated with his political turn came in 1938, during the Spanish Civil War: "The artist must elect to fight for freedom or for slavery. I have made my choice; I had no alternative." The wording survives through the MIA reference entry rather than through a recording or contemporary transcript. Read beside Du Bois's 1903 claim about the color line, it shows two men refusing the idea that scholarship or art could be neutral in a world organized by racial domination.

### Moscow, 1934

The vivid document in the Robeson archive is "I Am at Home," published in the *Daily Worker* on 15 January 1935. It reports a reception in Moscow and functions as a political conversion narrative. Its author was a visitor and a celebrated guest, writing for a party newspaper a few weeks after the event. What it establishes is how Robeson was received and how he interpreted that reception; it does not independently establish the conditions he describes.

The article quotes the film director Sergei Eisenstein introducing him as "the greatest American singer," and Robeson explains why he repeats it: to show "the wholehearted appreciation of these Russian sons of serfs who now are freed by their own efforts" toward "this visiting Negro singer, son of a slave in the United States." The parallel between the son of an American slave and the sons of Russian serfs is central to how Robeson framed his pro-Soviet position. Asked whether he had noticed a race question in the Soviet Union, he replied, "Only that it seems to work to my advantage!" He described his first impressions: "I was not prepared for the happiness I see on every face in Moscow." And he stated his position at its strongest: "anybody who lifts his hand against it ought to be shot," because "there is no terror here."

Those sentences were written in 1935, before the Moscow Trials of 1936 ([Robeson, "I Am at Home," *Daily Worker*, 15 January 1935](https://www.marxists.org/archive/robeson/1935/01/15.htm)). They are quoted here because they state the commitment clearly, not because they are comfortable. The MIA entry records that Robeson later had "private misgivings about the Stalinist purges of 1936–38," and that he remained publicly neutral on the Soviet Union's split with China in the late 1950s. His public praise should be read alongside those qualifications, not substituted for them. It is more accurate to say that Robeson reported finding no racial prejudice in the Soviet Union than to say that equality there was an established fact.

The article also records his plan to prepare a new form of drama and opera drawn from the folk arts of Africa, Asia and the Soviet peoples. That plan helps explain why he did not treat politics as a separate career. Artistic research and political commitment were one project in his mind: to demonstrate the equality and creative power of colonized peoples. He noted that on the way to Moscow he had spent a day in Berlin, "a day of horror," under Nazi threat. Robeson presented his commitment to the Soviet Union as a response to fascism, before the Cold War made that commitment costly ([Robeson, "I Am at Home," 1935](https://www.marxists.org/archive/robeson/1935/01/15.htm)).

### Spain and the 1949 Paris statement

Robeson's support for the Spanish Republic ran from 1936 into 1938. The MIA entry records that in 1938 he went to Spain "to sing and speak in support of the Spanish Republic in its civil war against General Francisco Franco's fascist rebellion." In his 1956 testimony he connected that struggle to his later positions: "I am for peace with the Soviet Union, and I am for peace with China, and I am not for peace or friendship with the Fascist Franco."

In 1949, at a world peace conference in Paris, Robeson said that Black Americans should not be drawn into a war against the Soviet Union on behalf of their own oppressors ([Gilbert King, "What Paul Robeson Said," *Smithsonian Magazine*, 2011](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/what-paul-robeson-said-77742433)). The exact wording became disputed in his later testimony, and he refused to retract the substance. Speaking to the committee in 1956, he recalled that students from the colonial world had asked him and the South African leader Dadoo "to say there that they were struggling for peace, that they did not want war against anybody." He continued: "I said it was unthinkable to me that any people would take up arms, in the name of an Eastland, to go against anybody. Gentlemen, I still say that." When confronted with an article he had published in July 1949, he insisted that the disputed phrase had appeared in the *Worker* in the United States, not in Paris.

### "We Charge Genocide"

The Civil Rights Congress prepared *We Charge Genocide* under the editorship of William L. Patterson and presented it to the United Nations in December 1951. Its introduction appeared in *Mainstream* in May 1951, several months before that presentation. The petitioners opened by saying that "the responsibility of being the first in history to charge the government of the United States of America with the crime of genocide is not one your petitioners take lightly." Their central claim was that "the genocide of which we complain is as much a fact as gravity." They argued that racial oppression could be measured in shortened lives and unequal access to health care, and they framed those conditions under the Genocide Convention adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 1946.

A [1970 *Political Affairs* retrospective](https://www.marxists.org/history/usa/pubs/political-affairs/PoliticalAffairs-1970-09-OCR-sm.pdf) records that the petition was presented in Paris by Patterson and, at the same time, to the United Nations Secretariat in New York by a committee headed by Robeson. This is one of the fullest indictments of American racism produced by the Black left in the Cold War years, and its importance for this comparison is structural: it moved the charge Du Bois had made about the color line into an international legal forum. The figures and conclusions in the petition are the petitioners' argument, advanced for a political and legal purpose, not uncontested statistical findings.

### The passport and the 1956 hearing

The MIA Encyclopedia states that Robeson's passport was revoked in 1950 and restored in 1958. In his 1956 testimony he gave his own account of why: "I should not be allowed to travel because I have struggled for years for the independence of the colonial peoples of Africa... The other reason... is that when I am abroad I speak out against the injustices against the Negro people of this land." He closed that passage with the genealogy the committee itself had invoked: "my ancestors in the time of Washington baked bread for George Washington's troops when they crossed the Delaware, and my own father was a slave. I stand here struggling for the rights of my people to be full citizens in this country."

The hearing, held on 12 June 1956, produced a long political statement. Robeson used it to challenge the committee's premises. Asked whether he was a Communist, he replied: "What is the Communist Party?... As far as I know it is a legal party like the Republican Party and the Democratic Party." Invoking the Fifth Amendment, he added that "wherever I have been in the world, Scandinavia, England, and many places, the first to die in the struggle against Fascism were the Communists." On the question of who would fight for independence, he told the committee that "the colored people are not going to die for anybody: they are going to die for their independence" (1956 HUAC testimony). He named Frederick Douglass and Eugene Debs as figures who would be at home in the company the committee condemned. Those names belong to this testimony; the 1969 tribute names Douglass and Du Bois instead.

On the Soviet Union he said: "In Russia I felt for the first time like a full human being. No color prejudice like in Mississippi, no color prejudice like in Washington." This is a witness's statement about his own experience, not an independent finding about Soviet society. When pressed about Stalin, he answered: "Whatever has happened to Stalin, gentlemen, is a question for the Soviet Union... You are responsible, and your forebears, for sixty million to one hundred million black people dying in the slave ships and on the plantations." The enormous figure was his own rhetorical claim. Scholars who have counted the transatlantic slave trade using surviving ship records reach a very different order of magnitude: the [Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database](https://www.neh.gov/project/transatlantic-slave-trade-database) estimates that about 12.5 million captives were embarked in Africa and about 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage. The two numbers are not measurements of the same thing, and the comparison is included here to show that Robeson's figure was a political summation rather than a demographic estimate.

The exchange shows how Robeson conducted himself before a hostile body. He answered the charge about his travel by naming his work for African independence, answered the charge about communism by naming what communists had done against fascism, and answered the charge about Stalin by returning the committee's attention to racial violence in the United States. That is a strategy, and it is more informative than an assessment of his tone.

### The later decades

In January 1953 Robeson published "Thoughts on Winning the Stalin Peace Prize," in which he named "great, new China and North Korea" among the peoples whose cause he would defend and described hearing "rumblings of the necessity of America's preparing for war against the Soviet Union" as early as 1945. Excerpts from *Here I Stand* appeared in *Political Affairs* in April 1958, including his declaration of "my deep conviction that for all mankind a socialist society represents an advance to a higher stage of life." A different passage from the book, not from those excerpts, states the domestic demand directly: "to walk the good American earth as equal citizens, to live without fear, to enjoy the fruits of our toil" (*Here I Stand*, Beacon Press, 1958). The two statements belong together. The argument for socialism abroad and the demand for equality at home were parts of one position.

Two complete messages from 1955 preserve that position in compact form. In his greeting to the Bandung Conference, Robeson called the meeting "an historic turning-point in all world affairs" and addressed "the peoples come from the shores of the Ganges and the Nile, the Yangtze and the Niger." He wrote that "the colored peoples of the world will no longer allow the great natural wealth of their countries to be exploited and expropriated by the Western world" (the online transcription reads "peopled," a scanning error), and closed with the line "Africa too shall shout in freedom and glory." At the Congress of the People in Kliptown in June, he quoted Frederick Douglass: "Power concedes nothing without a demand. It never did, and it never will." The Du Bois-Robeson convergence is visible in these texts without further commentary. Du Bois had argued since 1903 that the color line was a world problem; Robeson in 1955 addressed the world as a single political field. The "three gifts" of *Souls* had become a global exchange.

His passport restored in 1958, Robeson traveled abroad for five years to rebuild his career. The MIA entry records that he remained publicly neutral on the split between the Soviet Union and China, and that he returned to the United States in 1963 and retired there because of heart and blood-vessel disease. He continued to speak in the first person about his earlier life: "Though ill health has compelled my retirement, you can be sure that in my heart I go on singing" ([MIA Robeson glossary](https://www.connexions.org/CxArchive/MIA/glossary/people/r/o.htm)). He died on 23 January 1976, not abroad but at home.

### How he was remembered

Robeson's seventy-first birthday in 1969 was marked in *Political Affairs* by an address titled "In Honor of Paul Robeson," written and delivered by William L. Patterson. "Today we have gathered to celebrate the 71st birthday of Paul Leroy Robeson," Patterson wrote, and he placed Robeson "beside the deathless Frederick Douglas and the immortal W. E. B. Du Bois." The tribute also prints the Du Bois passage that Robeson had quoted: "Your country? How come its yours? Before the Pilgrims landed, we were here." The 1969 printing gives "How come its yours"; the 1903 text reads "How came it yours?" A 1970 *Political Affairs* retrospective on *We Charge Genocide* recorded Robeson's role in presenting the petition and called its documentation irrefutable. These pieces tell us what the movement chose to emphasize in 1969 and 1970: a lineage, a quotation and a petition.

## The joint record

The most concrete link between the two men is the Council on African Affairs, which the 1958 *Political Affairs* review describes as an organization to which Robeson, Du Bois and Alphaeus Hunton "devoted many years popularizing the cause of African and colonial freedom in this country" ([*Political Affairs*, April 1958, p. 3](https://www.marxists.org/history/usa/pubs/political-affairs/1958-04-37-4-political-affairs.pdf)). Around that institution can be placed Du Bois's Pan-African congresses, his 1915 essay on the African roots of war and the 1935 conclusion about land, light and leading; and Robeson's 1934 Moscow declaration, his 1949 Paris stand, his role in presenting the genocide petition, and his 1955 messages to Bandung and Kliptown. Both men were punished by their own government: Du Bois was prosecuted and restricted in his travel after 1950, and Robeson's passport was revoked in that year and restored only in 1958 ([*In Battle for Peace*, 1952](https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.54190); [MIA Robeson entry](https://www.marxists.org/glossary/people/r/o.htm#robeson-paul)).

Their differences matter as much as the overlap. Du Bois arrived at socialism late, through historical scholarship and the failure of liberal reform. Robeson arrived at it in his thirties, through the fight against fascism and through what he reported as his experience in the Soviet Union, while privately registering misgivings about the purges. Du Bois's writing relies heavily on documents and statistics and remains anchored in the American constitutional tradition even when it indicts that tradition. Robeson's writing is oratorical and personal, grounded in the conviction that culture itself is a political force. The Talented Tenth and the claim that the artist must choose freedom or slavery are answers to the same question from two professions: what does a gifted minority owe to the people from whom it has been separated?

By the 1950s both had answered that the question itself was wrong. The subject was the Black masses, in the United States and in the colonies. Du Bois's 1935 formulation, "Land, Light and Leading for slaves black, brown, yellow and white," and Robeson's 1958 formulation, "to walk the good American earth as equal citizens, to live without fear, to enjoy the fruits of our toil," express the same demand. Their later lives diverged. Du Bois died in Accra in 1963; Robeson returned to the United States in that same year and retired there ([*New York Times*, "W. E. B. Du Bois Dies in Ghana," 28 August 1963](https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/books/00/11/05/specials/dubois-obit.html); [MIA Robeson entry](https://www.marxists.org/glossary/people/r/o.htm#robeson-paul)). The record they left is not a set of statues or holidays but a body of arguments: a claim about the color line, a description of double consciousness, an indictment framed as genocide, a definition of the artist's duty and a repeated insistence that Black liberation and the struggle against capitalism and war could not be pulled apart.
