On 17 April 1975 the correspondent Sydney Schanberg stayed in Phnom Penh after the American staff left. The Khmer Rouge, as the soldiers and cadres of the Communist Party of Kampuchea were called, were entering the city. In testimony to the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia, the international tribunal set up to try the regime's leaders, Schanberg described more than two million people driven out, hospitals emptied and patients abandoned. The tribunal's summary of his evidence records: "[T]here was an air of panic and fear." The state these soldiers proclaimed, Democratic Kampuchea, lasted until 1979. Its revolution left an unusual archive: its own constitution, assembly minutes, prison rules, confessions and slogans. Read against the people who lived under them, those documents are the subject of this chapter.

## A programme on paper

The constitution of Democratic Kampuchea, adopted in December 1975 and promulgated on 5 January 1976, set out what the new state claimed it would deliver. Article 12 promised: "All workers are the masters of their factories. All peasants are the masters of the rice paddies and fields. All other labourers have the right to work. There is absolutely no unemployment in Democratic Kampuchea." The means of production were to be collective property, and a 250-member assembly held legislative power. Article 20 was equally plain: "Reactionary religions which are detrimental to Democratic Kampuchea and Kampuchean people are absolutely forbidden." The text said nothing about the Communist Party of Kampuchea, which still governed in secret through the body cadres called Angkar, the Organization. A reader of the constitution alone would have found a programme of collective ownership, full employment and equality, with no mention of the party that had written it.

## The meek tiger

Three months after the constitution, the party's Standing Committee discussed what to do with Prince Norodom Sihanouk, the former head of state left as a figurehead after the Khmer Rouge entered Phnom Penh. The minutes of 13 March 1976 call his differences with the party a class difference and conclude: "In reality, Sihanouk is a meek tiger, which only has its skin and bones left, without claws and the fangs." Keeping him in Cambodia was judged a gain for the revolution, but the minutes add: "But if he continues to resist us, we shall take measures to liquidate him." A month later the assembly recorded his retirement, elected Khieu Samphan president of the State Presidium and appointed Pol Pot prime minister, resolving to drive rice production to a maximum yield. In July 1978 the party's deputy secretary Nuon Chea told a friendly foreign party that the cities had been evacuated to frustrate CIA, KGB and Vietnamese agents, and that the party had kept its leadership secret because of the bloodstained experience of the Communist party of Indonesia. That is the leadership's stated reasoning, not evidence that the plots existed.

## Equality at a distance

The revolution could look different depending on where a person stood. A Cambodian student in France, separated from her parents, wrote in her diary on 17 September 1975: "To me, the evacuation of the people in Phnom Penh is correct. Its aim was to bring equality for every individual." By January 1977 she was recording an arranged marriage imposed by the organization, and in 1978 she described life in the northern region with a husband she had not chosen. At home, the language of production and the language of security ran together. Notebooks copied by cadres carried the line "Keeping you is no gain, losing you is no loss" alongside rice targets of three and a half and seven tons per hectare for 1978, and the instruction to purge CIA agents from the party and the country. The same vocabulary asked peasants to reach impossible yields and asked cadres to find enemies. The student's early judgment had been made in Paris; her later entries were written from inside the system she had welcomed.

## The prison and its paper

At S-21, the security prison at Tuol Sleng in Phnom Penh, the system acquired a routine. An undated circular from the prison office told guards: "Do not ask the prisoners their names." It specified shackles, patrols and four inspections in every twenty-four hours, and listed a staff that included 127 guards and seventy-six teenagers aged fifteen and sixteen. The registers record entry and execution dates for members of the royal house, including Sihanouk's daughter Noraknorin Dararith, and for nearly eighty foreign detainees. Hu Nim, a former information minister who signed a confession on 3 May 1977, wrote: "In the end I consented to their requirements. From that time I was a CIA agent." His doctorate and years of party work had been rewritten as spying. The tribunal that tried the prison's commandant explained why. Its judgment of 26 July 2010 found: "Given that detainees were considered guilty by reason of their presence at S-21, the role of interrogators was simply to 'validate the Party's verdict by extracting full confessions.'" It found that the commandant knew much of this was fabricated and that confessions were used to justify further arrests within a hierarchy reaching up to the Standing Committee. The killing was not simply local initiative.

## What the witnesses carried

The records are precise about procedure and silent about the people behind it. The survivor Chum Mey told the tribunal: "Whenever the word Tuol Sleng prison comes to my mind I could not hold my tears. It drops automatically." A former guard, Him Huy, said in a 2002 exhibition interview: "I didn't feel good about watching the people die one after another and the prisoners' miserable lives." Neither account was recorded at the time, and the guard spoke in a setting where former Khmer Rouge were invited to explain themselves. Schanberg, who was in Phnom Penh in April 1975, rejected the food-shortage explanation and described bodies on the roads two weeks later. By 1978 the regime was fighting Vietnam. A Phnom Penh radio broadcast of 10 May 1978 set the slogan "In terms of numbers, one of us must kill 30 Vietnamese" and spoke of a struggle that might last seven hundred years. The fall of Democratic Kampuchea followed in 1978 and 1979. For survivors, Chum Mey's words show, the event did not end with the regime.

## How historians have read it

- **David P. Chandler, "The Constitution of Democratic Kampuchea: The Semantics of Revolutionary Change," *Pacific Affairs*, Fall 1976.** A near-contemporary reading of the 1976 text as political language; DC-Cam used Chandler's translation as the basis of its own. His later work treats Pol Pot as a political leader whose revolution was shaped by war and secrecy. [Source: d.dccam.org](https://d.dccam.org/Archives/Documents/DK_Policy/DK_Policy_DK_Constitution.htm)
- **Craig Etcheson, *The Rise and Demise of Democratic Kampuchea* (Westview Press, 1984).** An early institutional history of the party and state; DC-Cam used it in preparing its English translation of the constitution. It is closer to Chandler than to the later refugee-testimony school in its emphasis on political structure. [Source: d.dccam.org](https://d.dccam.org/Archives/Documents/DK_Policy/DK_Policy_DK_Constitution.htm)
- **Ben Kiernan, *The Pol Pot Regime: Race, Power and Genocide in Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge, 1975–1979* (Yale University Press, 1996; revised 2002).** Built on interviews with several hundred refugees and argues that racial ideology and power helped produce genocide. His earlier sympathy for the Khmer Rouge has been criticised; the scale of killing he describes is broadly accepted, though his vocabulary and methods have been contested.
- **Michael Vickery, *Cambodia 1975–1982* (South End Press / Allen & Unwin, 1984; second edition, Silkworm Books, 1999).** The principal scholarly counterpoint to the orthodox account, sceptical of some received figures and interpretations; Vickery later served as a historical expert for Ieng Sary's defence at the ECCC, which indicates the political charge of the debate.
- **Documentation Center of Cambodia, *A History of Democratic Kampuchea (1975–1979)*, 2nd edition (2020).** A Cambodian institution's textbook, drawing on ECCC judgments and civil-party statements; it collects survivor testimony in translation and says "nearly two million" in its main text while listing estimates from 740,000 to 3.3 million in a footnote. It is a secondary compilation with first-hand excerpts, not a neutral account. [Source: thetedkarchive.com](https://www.thetedkarchive.com/library/documentation-center-of-cambodia-a-history-of-democratic-kampuchea-1975-1979)
- **ECCC Case 001 and Case 002 judgments (2010, 2014 and later).** Not historiography but the official legal record a writer must engage: Case 001 established how S-21 worked, and Case 002 found genocide of Vietnamese and Cham people and sentenced Nuon Chea and Khieu Samphan to life imprisonment. [Source: eccc.gov.kh](https://www.eccc.gov.kh/en/cases/case-001) and [Source: eccc.gov.kh](https://www.eccc.gov.kh/en/cases/case-002)

There is broad agreement that the evacuation was forced, the security apparatus was centralised, and mass death on a very large scale occurred. Disagreement continues over the exact toll, over the relative weight of racial ideology, class policy and state survival, and over the legal label for the killings.

## Limits of the record

The death toll is the largest open question. Cambodia's Documentation Center uses nearly two million, and 1.7 million is widely cited; these come from different methods and should be given as ranges. The tribunal itself could not count the dead at one prison: "Due to the inaccuracy of the existing record, it is not possible to quantify the precise number of the protected persons who died or were executed at S-21." Its findings are legal conclusions about selected defendants, not a total history. The motive for the evacuation is disputed: Schanberg rejected the food-shortage explanation, Nuon Chea offered a security plot, a student diary imagined equality, and the tribunal treated the forced transfer as a crime without adopting any of these justifications. Even the identity of the Vietnamese prisoners at S-21 is contested within the Documentation Center's own material.

The archive favours the state and the literate. It holds party minutes, prison rules and foreign correspondents, but almost no sustained first-hand account from an ordinary New Person, the city dweller sent to the countryside, in a cooperative. Confessions establish what the security system required a prisoner to say, not the conspiracies they describe. The two insider accounts here are a guard defending himself and a student writing from abroad, and Chum Mey's words reach us only through a judgment. Most documents are translations prepared by the Documentation Center, and the Standing Committee minutes were edited decades later by David Chandler; any phrase printed should be checked against the Khmer originals. The photographic catalogue has been read but the individual images have not been examined, and no film was watched. No Chinese or Yugoslav advisory records have been verified, so the international dimension of the security services remains unevidenced. A Pol Pot interview from August 1978 and the record of an Oslo hearing in April 1978 exist as PDFs whose text has not been extracted, so neither is used. The S-21 execution logs and confessions remain in Khmer, and Schanberg's underlying testimony transcripts have not been read. A visit to the Documentation Center, the Tuol Sleng museum and the tribunal's archive, with Khmer translation, would be needed to close these gaps.
