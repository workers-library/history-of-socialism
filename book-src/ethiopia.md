In November 1969 a fourth-year arts student at Addis Ababa University published an article in the student journal *Struggle* asking whether Ethiopia was one country or several. Walleligne Mekonnen argued that the unity proclaimed by the monarchy was a fiction maintained by the dominance of one people. "What is this fake Nationalism? Is it not simply Amhara and to a certain extent Amhara-Tigre supremacy?" he wrote. He named Oromo, Tigray, Amhara, Gurage, Sidama, Wolayta, Harari and Somali nations, held that secession could be progressive if workers and peasants led it, and rejected a military coup as the answer ([Walleligne Mekonnen, "On the Question of Nationalities in Ethiopia," 17 November 1969](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/ethiopia/nationalities.pdf)). Five years before the officers' Provisional Military Administrative Council, known as the Derg, took power, the national question was already being argued in the students' own words.

## The students and the claim to the revolution

Walleligne wrote for a student audience; his Leninist frame was his own, not every student's view. The internal history of the movement survives in a retrospective account by Alem Habtu, who sat on the executive of the Ethiopian Students Union in North America between 1965 and 1970. He wrote in 2015, recalling events forty-four years earlier: "My discussion will, therefore, rely on my first-hand or insider knowledge." ([Alem Habtu, "The Ethiopian Student Movement: My Experiences in ESUNA, 1964–1971," 25 February 2015](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/ethiopia/habtu-esuna.pdf)). Habtu dates the break from embassy funding to 1965, the shift from reform to anti-feudal politics to 1966 to 1968, and two occupations of the Ethiopian embassy in Washington to 1969. He defends his own part in the settlement, so the chronology is a participant's with an interest in it.

The Ethiopian Peoples' Revolutionary Party, or EPRP, formed from a merger of currents that included the Ethiopian People's Liberation Organisation, claimed descent from this student radicalism. Its clandestine programme of 1975 called the February uprising incomplete, accused the officers who had seized power of riding a revolutionary tide they had not made, and demanded a provisional peoples' government and a national congress. It promised the unrestricted right of nations to self-determination and declared that it would not bow to whoever held a gun. Its claim to represent the working class was stated in one sentence: "The E.P.R.P. is the party of the working class." ([Ethiopian Peoples' Revolutionary Party, programme, 1975](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/ethiopia/eprp-program.pdf)). The programme was written before the killings that put that claim to the test.

The February 1974 uprising itself appears in another participant's words. Senay Likke led the Waz League, an underground group that trained military cadres and worked with the Derg. He described the revolt in the vocabulary the officers adopted: "After age-old oppression under an archaic autocratic and monarchical rule, the Ethiopian people rose in revolt in February of 1974." ([Senay Likke, *The Ethiopian Revolution*, mid-1970s](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/ethiopia/likke-revolution.pdf)). Likke argued that the officer corps was carrying the same revolution forward. He was killed in a shootout with the Derg in February 1977, so his pamphlet cannot speak to what came after. Alongside the EPRP document it shows two incompatible accounts of who owned the revolution, written in the same language of feudalism, imperialism, the people and the vanguard, the revolutionary party that claimed to lead the working class. The All-Ethiopia Socialist Movement, known as MEISON, was another civilian current that worked with the Derg before the regime turned on it; its own programme survives only as an unreadable scan.

## The cleaning up campaign

Mengistu Haile Mariam, then chairman of the Derg, addressed a May Day crowd in Addis Ababa in 1977 in the language of the revolution's defenders. He announced that workers and peasants were being armed, that the neighbourhood associations known as kebeles were forming defence squads, and that the campaign against the EPRP and its allies would continue. "The cleaning up campaign will continue in a more organized and sophisticated way." ([Mengistu Haile Mariam, address to the May Day rally, 1 May 1977](https://www.marxists.org/archive/mengistu/1977/may-day.htm)). "Cleaning up" was the regime's own phrase. Read beside the human-rights investigations and the amnesties that followed, the speech records an intention to escalate political violence against opponents, whatever the phrase meant to the people in the square.

The legal ground had been prepared two years earlier. Article 27 of the Derg's Special Penal Code of November 1974 made it a crime to do anything that "creates within the country a grave state of misery, want or famine, epidemic or epizootic disease or distress" ([Article 27, Special Penal Code, November 1974, as quoted in *Evil Days*, 1991](https://www.hrw.org/sites/default/files/reports/Ethiopia919.pdf)). Punishment ran to rigorous imprisonment for life, and to death where a death resulted. Africa Watch, the human-rights organisation that reproduced the article, argued that such vagueness deterred legitimate grain trading and helped produce the shortages it was meant to prevent. That causal claim is the report's interpretation, not part of Article 27.

## Land, famine and the war for the union

Land nationalisation and the peasant associations that followed it changed the countryside, though much of the account of them comes from the regime. In a 1978 anniversary speech Mengistu listed the nationalisation of land and of the major industries in January 1975 among the revolution's achievements, and conceded acute urban food shortages, blaming merchant hoarding and peasant individualism. His famine reference on that occasion was to the Wollo famine of 1972 to 1974, not to the catastrophe of the mid-1980s. The later famine and the resettlement that accompanied it survive in other kinds of testimony. Alula Pankhurst, an anthropologist who lived in a resettlement village in 1987 and 1988 and collected settlers' accounts, recorded one man from Desé who remembered building the road out on which the buses later took him away: "We had to work for grain; we built the very road the buses used to take us away from our homeland. While we worked for food, others received aid free." ([settler from Desé, recorded by Alula Pankhurst, 1992](https://archive.org/details/famine-in-ethiopia-the-villagers-experience)). Pankhurst warned against reducing such motives to hunger alone or to force alone, and admitted that he had highlighted moving cases over ordinary ones.

Questioned by Jane Perlez in 1988, Mengistu defended the programme while conceding that the revolution had not met its goals. "It is wrong to consider us as full-fledged socialists. We are still on the democratic stage of the Revolution" ([Mengistu Haile Mariam to Jane Perlez, 26 November 1988](https://www.marxists.org/archive/mengistu/1988/nyt-interview.htm)). He said ninety per cent of farmland remained privately owned, called the displacement voluntary, and put the eventual number of people to be moved as high as seven million. He admitted that some aged and starving people died in transit. His figures were his own, and his account of voluntariness conflicts with the settlers' testimony and with the scale of the programme.

The national question Walleligne had raised in 1969 was never settled. The Tigray People's Liberation Front, or TPLF, adopted a programme in May 1983 that called national oppression the most widespread and acute contradiction in Ethiopia and dismissed the Derg's land reform as half-hearted. "The TPLF's aim is the elimination of national oppression." and "Nationalities shall have the right to self-determination including and up to secession." ([Tigray People's Liberation Front, People's Democratic Programme, May 1983](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/ethiopia/tigray-program.pdf)). The Eritrean People's Liberation Front, or EPLF, held its first congress in liberated areas in January 1977, with delegates from other Ethiopian movements present, and joined a planned economy, equality of nationalities and complete equality of women in its programme. After the Soviet Union shifted its support to the Derg, EPLF leaders said they were fighting Russian and Cuban forces as well as the Ethiopian army, and rejected Chinese aid that required them to imitate Chinese slogans. Mengistu's answer was the unitary state. From exile in Harare in 1994 he denied that he had been a Soviet satellite or a dictator, claimed gains in literacy, health and labour law, and blamed conspiracy and a fifth column in his own ranks for his fall. He denounced the EPLF and TPLF as one Tigrayan movement. "only history and the Ethiopian people have the right to judge me and my political leadership" ([Mengistu Haile Mariam, statement of 3 February 1994](https://www.marxists.org/archive/mengistu/1994/sunday-gazette-statement.htm)). Ethiopian readers wrote to the newspaper to dispute him at once.

## How historians have read it

Histories of the revolution rest on different kinds of evidence. Bahru Zewde's single-volume narrative links the student movement, the uprising and the national question in one chronology; Andargachew Tiruneh's institution-centred study reconstructs the regime's formal structure from proclamations and newspapers. Donald Donham's ethnographic history argues that the revolution must be understood through everyday practice in one region, and is more sympathetic to the revolutionary project than the human-rights literature. Africa Watch's *Evil Days* holds the Derg responsible for the deaths of at least half a million civilians and treats famine and relief as weapons of war. René Lefort wrote as a participant close to the civilian left, and his account of the factional politics of 1976 to 1978 is valuable and partisan. Gebru Tareke's later synthesis sets the revolution inside the regional and Cold War wars. There is broad agreement that the monarchy fell in 1974, that the Derg defeated the civilian left and then its own allies, and that land nationalisation, war and famine transformed the country. Disagreement concerns the weight of drought against state policy in the famine, the scale and legal character of the Red Terror, and whether the Derg carried out a social revolution or seized one. These bodies of work answer different questions, and should not be treated as equally supported.

## Limits of the record

The surviving record leaves large gaps. The Derg's founding proclamations are missing from it: the LawEthiopia scans return errors and the *Negarit Gazeta* has not been recovered, so Proclamation No. 31/1975 on rural land is known through citations and later scholarship rather than a primary text. MEISON's programme of 1975 and Babile Tola's participant account of the Red Terror survive as image-only scans with no extractable text, and Dawit Wolde Giorgis's memoir of 1989 has not been available as full text. Most of the material is in Amharic, with Eritrean sources also in Tigrinya and Arabic. The English record is weighted toward the diaspora, the anti-revisionist left, Western journalism and human-rights organisations. Testimony from Oromo, Somali, Afar and southern peasants is thin beside that from Amhara and Tigrayan students and officials. The record preserves the organisers and memorialists of the Red Terror far better than the young people most likely to be seized in a night raid. One survivor's account reached a wide audience through a 2022 documentary presented by *The New Yorker* in 2024, but what survives in print is the introduction, not a transcript of her words. Death estimates for the Red Terror run from Africa Watch's floor of "well in excess of 10,000" into the tens of thousands, and some go higher; they count different things, and none should be quoted as a measured figure ([Africa Watch, *Evil Days*, 1991, ch. 6](https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/e/ethiopia/ethiopia.919/c6terror.pdf)). Closing these gaps would mean working in the National Archives of Ethiopia, the Red Terror Martyrs' Memorial Museum in Addis Ababa and the archives of the Special Prosecutor's Office, and reading the proclamations in Amharic.
