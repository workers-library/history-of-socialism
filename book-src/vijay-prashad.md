Vijay Prashad is an Indian-born historian and journalist whose work explains how countries emerging from colonial rule tried to act together in world politics. Three terms need a plain definition before his arguments can be followed. "Third World" in his usage is not a place on a map but a political project: the countries of Africa, Asia and Latin America that asserted national liberation and non-alignment rather than joining either Cold War bloc. "Global South" is, in his publisher's formulation, not a geographical space but "a concatenation of protests against neoliberalism". "Internationalism" means the belief that working people and movements in different countries should cooperate across borders; Prashad calls the nationalism he studies "an internationalist nationalism". This profile sets out what he has argued, what can be checked in published and institutional records, and where historians and other socialists have objected.

## Biography

Tricontinental's own staff page, the strongest institutional record available for this profile, says Vijay Prashad was born in 1967 in Calcutta, India. It describes him as a historian and journalist and as the director of Tricontinental: Institute for Social Research, though elsewhere on the same page he is called executive director. The institute describes itself as an inter-movement research institute of the Global South associated with the International Peoples' Assembly, claims that his weekly newsletter reaches almost two million people, says he has written more than forty books, and says that two of his histories, *The Darker Nations* (2007) and *The Poorer Nations* (2013), won awards and that he has twice received the Muzaffar Ahmed Prize. These are the institute's own figures and claims, not independently counted here ([Tricontinental, "The Team"](https://thetricontinental.org/team/)).

The New Press author biography adds that Prashad is editor of LeftWord Books and chief correspondent for Globetrotter, that *The Darker Nations* was chosen as a Best Nonfiction Book of the Year by the Asian American Writers' Workshop and won the Muzaffar Ahmad Book Prize, and that he lives in Santiago, Chile, and Northampton, Massachusetts ([The New Press, *The Darker Nations*](https://thenewpress.org/books/the-darker-nations)). Monthly Review's author page calls him executive director of Tricontinental, chief editor at LeftWord Books and a regular writer for *Frontline*, *The Hindu*, *Alternet* and *BirGun* ([Monthly Review, "Vijay Prashad"](https://monthlyreview.org/author/vijay-prashad/)).

An older institutional page records an earlier stage of the same career. The University of Oklahoma says Prashad was the George and Martha Kellner Professor of South Asian History and director of the International Studies Program at Trinity College in Hartford, that he earned a PhD in history at the University of Chicago in 1994, and that he had previously taught at Syracuse and Cornell. It identifies him as the 2007 NCORE keynote speaker, lists board service with the Center for Third World Organizing, United for a Fair Economy and the National Priorities Project ([University of Oklahoma, NCORE page](https://ou.edu/jrcoe/edahdtan/apincore/prashad.htm)). Some commonly cited biographical details could not be verified here. A University of Chicago International House event listing described him as "PhD'94" and the George and Martha Kellner Chair, but its URL returned a 404 when opened. An UNCTAD notice gave a BA from Pomona College (1989), an MA from Chicago (1990) and a PhD from Chicago (1994), but that page returned HTTP 403, so the earlier degrees are not confirmed in this profile. The domain vijayprashad.org now serves unrelated casino material and cdn.vijayprashad.org returned a transport error; neither can be used as a first-party record.

## Intellectual tradition

Prashad works in the tradition that Tricontinental calls "national liberation Marxism". The institute's history page says its name joins two sources: the Tricontinental Conference held in Cuba in January 1966, which brought together revolutionary movements from Africa, Asia and Latin America with an orientation of national liberation, non-alignment and socialism, and the Institute for Social Research founded in Frankfurt in 1923. It states that national liberation Marxism "anchors the work of our institute", that its method starts from Marx's eleventh thesis on Feuerbach ("philosophers have hitherto only interpreted the world in various ways; the point is to change it"), and that a key concept is Gramsci's "new intellectual": an organic intellectual of working people who acts as "constructor, organiser, 'permanent persuader'" ([Tricontinental, "About Us"](https://thetricontinental.org/about/)). The same page says Tricontinental and the International Peoples' Assembly both emerged from a 2015 "Dilemmas of Humanity" meeting in Brazil, and that the institute has branches in Argentina, Brazil, India and South Africa. Its publications page defines weekly newsletters, monthly dossiers, studies, notebooks and "Red Alerts", and says the dossiers draw on a global network of research institutes ([Tricontinental, "Our publications"](https://thetricontinental.org/publications-overview/)).

His journal affiliation is substantive, not simply a place where his work appears. Monthly Review Press publishes *Washington Bullets* and lists *Washington's New Cold War*, co-authored with John Bellamy Foster, John Ross and Deborah Veneziale ([Monthly Review, *Washington Bullets*](https://monthlyreview.org/9781583679067/washington-bullets)). His contributions to the journal range from articles on India's alignment with the United States to a 2025 argument about socialism and dignity ([Monthly Review, search listing](https://monthlyreview.org/search-results/?keyword=Red%20Star%20Over%20the%20Third%20World)).

He does not accept that every state outside the West belongs automatically to a socialist camp. In a 2023 *Democracy Now!* interview he said of BRICS: "this is not a socialist bloc or an anti-imperialist bloc standing against the West", and argued that it was "a little absurd to judge them based on a socialistic standard". He proposed instead that the alignment of large Southern states indicated "a kind of new nonalignment", analogous to the Belgrade gathering of 1961, which included both Cuba and pro-Western Ceylon ([Democracy Now!, 22 August 2023](https://www.democracynow.org/2023/8/22/brics_summit_vijay_prashad)). That qualification matters when reading criticisms that label him a simple "campist": his own published position treats Southern states as contradictory actors rather than as socialist allies.

## Major contributions

The best verified statement of Prashad's central concept comes from reviews that quote the book directly. GVGK Tang gives the opening of *The Darker Nations* as "The Third World was not a place. It was a project", locating it on page xv ([Tang, "A People's Biography?"](http://gvgktang.com/research/a-peoples-biography-prashads-the-darker-nations)). Saswat Pattanayak quotes the same opening line and a second formulation: "The Third World form of nationalism is thus better understood as an internationalist nationalism" (page 12 in his citation) ([Pattanayak, Radical Notes review](https://radicalnotes.org/2007/03/19/a-review-of-the-darker-nations/)). Verso's description of the sequel restates the argument in the publisher's own words: "Just as *The Darker Nations* asserted that the Third World was a project, not a place, *The Poorer Nations* sees the Global South as a term that properly refers not to geographical space but to a concatenation of protests against neoliberalism" ([Verso, *The Poorer Nations*](https://versobooks.com/en-gb/products/2292-the-poorer-nations)). The full text of *The Darker Nations* was not opened for this profile: a university-hosted PDF endpoint returned an unsupported file type and the JSTOR preview page did not render, so the page references above are taken from the reviewers' citations rather than from direct inspection.

Prashad has continued to develop the idea in his own bulletins. In the fourth Tricontinental newsletter of 2025, dated 23 January, he wrote that the IMF and Washington Consensus recipes of "debt, austerity, structural adjustment" had failed, and argued that China was "the only exception to this trend" of growth without commensurate poverty reduction. He closed by invoking Cabral's claim that the goal of national liberation is "the freeing of the process of development of the national productive forces", and spoke of "the Promethean aspirations of the darker nations" ([Tricontinental, "The Promethean Aspirations of the Darker Nations"](https://thetricontinental.org/newsletterissue/a-new-development-theory/), 23 January 2025). In the 2025 *Democracy Now!* interview he explained Bandung's watchwords as "peace" and "development", and described the Third World as a "moral force" whose hope was attacked through the coups against Lumumba, Goulart and Sukarno ([Democracy Now!, 22 April 2025](https://www.democracynow.org/2025/4/22/1955_bandung_conference)).

## Analysis of capitalism

Tricontinental's first working document, *In the Ruins of the Present*, dated 1 March 2018, is the clearest extended statement of Prashad's economic analysis available in full text ([Tricontinental, working document 1](https://thetricontinental.org/working-document-1/)). It opens with an "international division of humanity": poor people know "the punishment" of hunger and war but not "the crime" for which they are punished. Neoliberalism, he writes, is not simply the work of Thatcher and Reagan; it was "brought to bear by these governments to solve practical problems produced by structural changes in the global mode of production". Capital had always sought a global market, but by the 1970s technological barriers had been overcome and working-class power "relatively depleted", allowing capital to look down on the planet from satellites and seek "the cheapest workers and the dearest markets".

The political hinge in that account is the Third World debt crisis. Prashad highlights the 1979 Volcker shock, the rise in dollar interest rates, and figures for fifteen heavily indebted countries whose external public debt rose from 17.9 billion dollars in 1970 to 402.2 billion in 1987. He quotes Nyerere's question, "Must we starve our children to pay our debts?", and argues that the crisis broke the bargaining power of postcolonial states. The new architecture rested on three things: new technologies such as container shipping and satellite communication; the delivery of millions of workers to capital through the weakening of earlier barriers, which he names as the October Revolution, the Chinese Revolution and the Third World Project; and the intellectual property settlement of the Uruguay Round, which created the WTO in 1994. From this he derives "disarticulated production", foreign-direct-investment outsourcing, "arms-length outsourcing", labour arbitrage and the power of brand firms that invest little in production. He calls the refusal of the wealthy to invest a "tax strike" and an "investment strike", writing that "There is no greater scandal than this structural constipation of capital, this 'investment strike'." Figures in the document, such as the claim that eight men hold as much wealth as the bottom half of humanity or that the total cost of violence equals 13.3 per cent of world GDP, are Prashad's own cited estimates and are reported here as his claims, not as independently verified statistics.

His later bulletins keep the same frame. The January 2025 newsletter cites the Maddison Project Database 2023 for a 689.9 per cent rise in global GDP at purchasing power parity between 1980 and 2022, contrasts that with poverty rates that "did not decrease at a commensurate pace", and cites an UNCTAD report putting global public debt at a record 97 trillion dollars in 2023. Its policy conclusion is deliberately concrete: "We must invest in order to build, build in order to grow, and grow in order to improve people's lives." In the 2023 interview he applied a similar analysis to the BRICS New Development Bank, describing it as capital of 100 billion dollars established in 2015 to lend for infrastructure rather than debt servicing, and quoting Dilma Rousseff's commitment to lend without conditionalities ([Democracy Now!, 22 August 2023](https://www.democracynow.org/2023/8/22/brics_summit_vijay_prashad)).

## Analysis of socialism

Prashad has not published a single blueprint for a socialist society in the material opened here, and it would be misleading to invent one. His preferred formulation is broad and political. Monthly Review's listing for his November 2025 article, "The World Wants to Advance to Socialism", summarises him as writing: "A politics to produce dignity is a socialist politics…. Capitalism inherently generates forms of inequality and indignity. Therefore, all undertakings that seek dignity for all are socialist projects." The full article body did not render when the page was opened, so these are the journal's published summary words rather than a passage read in the article itself ([Monthly Review, search listing](https://monthlyreview.org/search-results/?keyword=Red%20Star%20Over%20the%20Third%20World)).

Tricontinental's mission statement supplies the institutional version. It says the institute's work is "rooted in both a theory of exploitation and the reality of class struggle", that its research agenda includes tracking "the various forms of socialism that are being developed in our time", and that it seeks to archive and analyse histories of national liberation and theories of national liberation Marxism ([Tricontinental, "About Us"](https://thetricontinental.org/about/)). The point is to learn from actually existing projects rather than to legislate a model. The institute's dossier and newsletter titles in 2026 show the same method at work: "Socialism Is Slow to Mature" (the twenty-first newsletter, 2026), "The Future – Socialism – Is Possible and Necessary" (the twentieth, 2026), and the hundred-and-sixth dossier, "Socialist Strategy within the Architecture of Power", published 10 November 2026, which asks how movements building organised strength outside government can connect with governments that hold office without transforming the system ([newsletter index](https://thetricontinental.org/newsletter/) and [dossier index](https://thetricontinental.org/dossier/)). Dossiers are collectively produced, according to the institute's own description, so they are cited here as institute publications, not as solely Prashad's authorship.

In *Washington Bullets* the publisher's description says the book ends with Thomas Sankara's line about needing "a certain amount of madness" and "the courage to invent the future", and that the book is "infused with this madness" ([Monthly Review, *Washington Bullets*](https://monthlyreview.org/9781583679067/washington-bullets)). In the 2023 interview Prashad was explicit that BRICS should not be measured against a socialist standard, while insisting that India could simultaneously pursue "a full-scale capitalist project against its own people" and tell Western governments that "Your issues are not our issues" ([Democracy Now!, 22 August 2023](https://www.democracynow.org/2023/8/22/brics_summit_vijay_prashad)). That is the project-based reading he applies to socialist and postcolonial politics alike: judge what institutions do, not the label they claim.

## Recommended reading

These are starting points into Prashad's argument, not a measure of his whole output; the full bibliography stands above.

- *The Darker Nations: A People's History of the Third World* (2007) — This is the essential starting point for his central claim that the Third World was a project rather than a place and that its nationalism was internationalist.
- *The Poorer Nations: A Possible History of the Global South* (2013) — It is the best place to see how he carries that idea forward into the term Global South, which he uses for a concatenation of protests against neoliberalism rather than a geographical space.
- *In the Ruins of the Present* (2018) — This is the clearest available statement of his economic analysis, tracing neoliberalism to the debt crisis and the weakening of postcolonial bargaining power, then setting out his account of outsourced, disarticulated production.
- *Washington Bullets: A History of the CIA, Coups, and Assassinations* (2020) — It is the best starting point for his project-based reading of postcolonial and socialist politics, ending as it does with Sankara's call for the courage to invent the future.

## Intellectual influences

The influences that can be documented from Prashad's own texts are few but clear. Tricontinental's mission page names Marx's eleventh thesis on Feuerbach and Gramsci's "new intellectual", including the phrase "permanent persuader", as the basis of its method ([Tricontinental, "About Us"](https://thetricontinental.org/about/)). The January 2025 newsletter quotes Eduardo Galeano's line about where people earn the per capita income, cites Amílcar Cabral on national liberation and productive forces, and treats Nyerere's Arusha Initiative and *Ujamaa* as a serious, if ultimately unsuccessful, alternative ([Tricontinental newsletter](https://thetricontinental.org/newsletterissue/a-new-development-theory/)). In the 2025 interview Prashad invoked Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* for the point that newly independent African countries could not produce toothpaste and relied on multinational imports, and placed Sukarno, Nkrumah, Nasser, Nehru and Zhou Enlai inside the Bandung generation ([Democracy Now!, 22 April 2025](https://www.democracynow.org/2025/4/22/1955_bandung_conference)).

The title of *The Darker Nations* is linked to W. E. B. Du Bois. A JSTOR indexed excerpt said the title comes from Du Bois's 1915 essay "The African Roots of War", but the JSTOR page did not render when opened; Tang's review independently records that Prashad invokes Du Bois's 1900 address, and Pattanayak's review notes his use of Du Bois on Pan-Africanism ([Tang](http://gvgktang.com/research/a-peoples-biography-prashads-the-darker-nations); [Pattanayak](https://radicalnotes.org/2007/03/19/a-review-of-the-darker-nations/)). Frantz Fanon is present through the epigraphic use of *The Wretched of the Earth* noted by Pattanayak. Noam Chomsky is a co-author rather than merely an influence: *The Withdrawal* and *On Cuba* are dialogues published by the New Press.

## Influence and legacy

Publisher records support a limited, careful statement of reach. The New Press says *The Darker Nations* was named a Best Nonfiction Book of the Year by the Asian American Writers' Workshop, won the Muzaffar Ahmed Book Prize, and "has defined for a generation of scholars, activists, and dreamers" how to imagine a more just international order; it prints endorsements from Immanuel Wallerstein, Ruth Wilson Gilmore, Eduardo Galeano, Paul Gilroy and others ([The New Press](https://thenewpress.org/books/the-darker-nations)). These are publisher-selected judgments and endorsements, not independent measurements of influence. Tricontinental's own figures of almost two million newsletter readers, monthly dossiers, institutes in four countries and membership of the International Union of Left Publishers are likewise institutional claims ([Tricontinental team and about pages](https://thetricontinental.org/team/)).

What can be said without exaggeration is that the book became a reference point for later arguments about Bandung, non-alignment and the Third World. It was reviewed in *Journal of Global History* by Adnan A. Husain (volume 4, issue 3, November 2009, pages 497–499, DOI 10.1017/S1740022809990180) and in *Societies Without Borders* by Wesley Hiers (volume 4, issue 3, 2009, pages 452–455, DOI 10.1163/187188609X12492771031816). Both journal records were opened, but the review texts themselves were paywalled ([Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/darker-nations-a-peoples-history-of-the-third-world-by-vijay-prashad-new-york-the-new-press-2007-pp-xi-364-hardback-1899-isbn-9781565847859-paperback-1599-isbn-9781595583420/01F6DCF42A262E6895BE05B62DCA901A); [Brill](https://brill.com/view/journals/swb/4/3/article-p452_9.xml)). His later institutional work has made the phrase "global South" and the idea of a new non-alignment part of a weekly publication series, where dossiers and newsletters are translated into several languages.

## Criticisms and controversies

The most detailed scholarly critique opened for this profile is GVGK Tang's essay on *The Darker Nations*. Tang argues that despite a book titled "a people's history", "radical male intellectuals and politicians" are cast as those who act while "the subjects, the oppressed and downtrodden masses (indeed, the titular 'people') remain underrepresented". He faults Prashad for not dealing at length with race, colour or the nation-state boundaries that circumscribe the Third World, and for treating indigenous traditions as "insufficient against the problems posed by imperialism", a conclusion Tang reads as implicitly elevating European culture. His verdict is: "Still, 'a people's history' this book is not" ([Tang](http://gvgktang.com/research/a-peoples-biography-prashads-the-darker-nations)). This is a historian's review essay on a personal academic site, not a peer-reviewed article; it establishes that the criticism was made, not that every part of it is correct.

A contemporary review from the radical left made overlapping points. Pattanayak asked whether "it is not too early to declare the Third World a dead project?", argued that women's independent movements were confined to a single chapter and a few named speakers, and complained that the book underplayed CIA intervention, the Vatican's role and popular struggles in favour of "titans" and "giants" ([Pattanayak, Radical Notes, 19 March 2007](https://radicalnotes.org/2007/03/19/a-review-of-the-darker-nations/)). The Cambridge review by Husain, of which only metadata and a truncated search excerpt were available, reportedly identifies an "endemic flaw in the project" in the unity of the darker nations; that fragment is too slight to summarise his argument, and no fuller claim is made here.

On Syria, Evan Sandlin's 2016 *Los Angeles Review of Books* essay criticised Prashad for grouping "Salafists, the Muslim Brotherhood, and al-Qaeda in Iraq together as 'sectarian' Sunni forces, despite clear differences between the groups", as part of a wider argument that parts of the left minimised Assad's crimes. The same essay carries a correction: "The author originally referred to Vijay Prashad as 'pro-Assad.' Although Prashad no longer supports demands for Assad to step down he did issue his condemnation of the Assad regime in 2011." Both the criticism and the correction are reported here; describing Prashad simply as an Assad supporter would ignore the printed correction ([Sandlin, LARB, 30 October 2016](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/dereliction-duty-left-syrian-civil-war/)).

The sharpest recent dispute concerns Xinjiang. Writing for Critical China Scholars on 17 April 2026, the historian David Brophy replied to a *Monthly Review* article by Prashad and Tings Chak. Brophy alleges that a "landmark essay" attributed to Wang Hui was made up, and that the authors "had invented a book by Wang Ke 王柯, a scholar of Xinjiang who works in Japan, and a *Grayzone* article by Max Blumenthal". He reports that Prashad said no AI was involved and that the referencing flaws did not affect the substance, a claim Brophy disputes. Brophy's substantive case is that the article repeats official Chinese historical narratives, minimises detention and censorship, and substitutes whataboutism for a defence of national rights; he cites a UN review of judicial documentation and prosecution figures that rose from 41,305 in 2016 to 215,823 in 2017 ([Critical China Scholars](https://criticalchinascholars.org/interventions)). These are Brophy's allegations and are attributed to him. The same collective's 2023 statement criticised Tricontinental by name for failing to recognise "the many oppressive realities of the PRC state" while opposing McCarthyite attacks on the left ([Critical China Scholars, 16 August 2023](https://criticalchinascholars.org/interventions)).

From a different position on the left, the International Socialist Organisation Aotearoa argued in October 2024 that Prashad "has rebranded himself as an apologist for dictatorial forces of the Global South, most notably in China and the Middle East", and faulted him for saying little about Russia's invasion itself while opposing NATO ([ISO Aotearoa](https://iso.org.nz/2024/10/24/vijay-prashad-campism-and-anti-colonial-struggle)). This is a partisan political critique, useful as evidence of a dispute inside the socialist tradition, not as a neutral assessment of his positions.

## Related thinkers

Noam Chomsky is Prashad's most visible collaborator, with two New Press dialogue books verified here. John Bellamy Foster and Monthly Review Press form a second institutional relationship, and Eduardo Galeano, Immanuel Wallerstein and Ruth Wilson Gilmore appear as endorsers on the New Press page. Walter Rodney, Amílcar Cabral, Julius Nyerere, Frantz Fanon, W. E. B. Du Bois, Antonio Gramsci and Karl Marx appear in Prashad's own texts or in Tricontinental's statement of method. The disagreements are as instructive as the affiliations. Trevor Ngwane and Patrick Bond challenged the significance of BRICS in the same *Democracy Now!* programme, with Ngwane arguing that anti-imperialism is not necessarily anti-capitalism, and Prashad answering that BRICS was neither socialist nor anti-imperialist and should be understood on its own terms ([Democracy Now!, 22 August 2023](https://www.democracynow.org/2023/8/22/brics_summit_vijay_prashad)). David Brophy and the Critical China Scholars collective represent a scholarly-left challenge from the opposite direction, while the ISO Aotearoa critique represents a revolutionary-socialist challenge to his reading of state power.
