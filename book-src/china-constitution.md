A constitution is the fundamental law of a state. It sets out the institutions that hold power, the limits on that power and the rights the state claims to protect. China's Constitution has been rewritten four times since the People's Republic was founded in 1949 and amended five times since the current text was adopted in 1982. Each version belongs to a distinct political moment. The 1954 text accompanied the transition to socialism, the 1975 text was written during the Cultural Revolution, the 1978 text belongs to the uncertain interval after Mao's death, and the 1982 text was part of the reform era's institutional settlement. The amendments, especially that of 2018, record later shifts in the balance between Party and state.

## Where the official text comes from

The official English text is published on the State Council's portal. Two official hosts carry different amended versions, one reflecting the 2004 amendments and the other the 2018 amendments: the [full text at english.www.gov.cn](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/lawsregulations/201911/20/content_WS5ed8856ec6d0b3f0e9499913.html) and a [second amended text on the same portal](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/laws_regulations/2014/08/23/content_281474982987458.htm). The promulgating body is the National People's Congress (NPC), the national legislature that the Constitution calls the highest organ of state power. The text is republished by the State Council's official English portal with attribution to npc.gov.cn ([official text](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/lawsregulations/201911/20/content_WS5ed8856ec6d0b3f0e9499913.html)). The document currently in force was adopted in 1982 and amended in 1988, 1993, 1999, 2004 and 2018.

## Four constitutions and the politics behind them

Before there was a constitution, the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference, an advisory body that represented the Communist Party and allied groups, adopted the Common Programme of 1949. It served as a provisional constitution while the new state was being organised. The [NPC's own overview](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/china_abc/2014/08/23/content_281474982987272.htm) states that the Constitution "was adopted at the First Session of the First NPC in 1954 and comprehensively revised by the NPC in 1982. The current Constitution is the one promulgated and put into effect after this revision." That sentence compresses four constitutions into two dates.

The first Constitution was adopted on 20 September 1954 by the First Session of the First NPC. It had 106 articles and was drafted in the period when the leadership was moving from the mixed economy of New Democracy toward socialist transformation. Its Preamble says that "from the founding of the People's Republic of China to the attainment of a socialist society is a period of transition" and that the state's task was "step by step, to bring about the socialist industrialization of the country and, step by step, to accomplish the socialist transformation of agriculture, handicrafts and capitalist industry and commerce" ([1954 Constitution, Preamble](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China_%281954%29)). Mao Zedong chaired the drafting committee and a small drafting group worked in Hangzhou; Soviet constitutional experience, including the 1936 Soviet constitution, was an important reference. Official accounts report that about 150 million people took part in the subsequent discussion, though the discussion was organised by the Party and government ([official account of the 1954 Constitution](http://www.npc.gov.cn/zgrdw/npc/zt/qt/gjxfz/2014-12/03/content_1888059_2.htm); [Byron Weng on the 1982 draft](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/abs/some-key-aspects-of-the-1982-draft-constitution-of-the-peoples-republic-of-china/B9773E5744B8D799463BF08BDAD34E54)). Jerome Cohen's study of China's changing constitution treats the 1954 document as the first serious attempt to give the new regime a working legal framework, and asks whether such documents are merely propaganda or carry legal weight ([Cohen, "China's Changing Constitution," *The China Quarterly* 76, December 1978, 794-841, DOI 10.1017/S0305741000049584](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/abs/chinas-changing-constitution/3E88396D1C6BB354FAD6F54B5E24B0F8)).

The second Constitution was adopted on 17 January 1975 by the First Session of the Fourth NPC, while the Cultural Revolution was still under way. It had only 30 articles. Its Preamble says that socialist society covers a long historical period in which classes and class struggle persist, and that "These contradictions can be resolved only by depending on the theory of continued revolution under the dictatorship of the proletariat and on practice under its guidance" ([1975 Constitution, Preamble](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Constitution_of_the_People%27s_Republic_of_China_%281975%29)). The "dictatorship of the proletariat" means rule by the working class, exercised in practice by the Communist Party. The text abolished the presidency, gave the Party chairman command of the armed forces, and replaced ordinary local government with revolutionary committees. Zhang Chunqiao, a leading radical, told the NPC that the revision was needed to "persist in continued revolution under the dictatorship of the proletariat" ([Zhang Chunqiao, "Report on the Revision of the Constitution," 13 January 1975](https://www.crteducazione.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/CHN_Constitution_1975_EN.pdf)). The Chinese constitutional scholar Mo Jihong describes the 1975 and 1978 texts as shaped by extreme leftist ideas of continuing revolution that were at odds with ordinary constitutional principles ([Mo Jihong, "The Constitutional Law of the People's Republic of China and Its Development," *Columbia Journal of Asian Law*](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/cjal/article/view/3287)).

The third Constitution was adopted on 5 March 1978, after Mao's death and after the fall of the Gang of Four, but before the political line of the reform era had been settled. It had 60 articles, twice as many as the 1975 text but fewer than the 106 of 1954. It restored the procuratorate, a state office that supervises prosecutions and legal oversight, and restored open trials and the right to defence. Its rights chapter grew from four articles to sixteen. Yet it retained the language of "continuing the revolution under the dictatorship of the proletariat" and the official account of constitutional development links this language to the "two whatevers" slogan, the claim that policy should follow whatever Mao decided and whatever he said. It was amended in 1979, when county-level and higher people's congresses were given standing committees and revolutionary committees were turned into people's governments, and again in 1980, when the right to "big controversy, big debate, big-character posters" was removed ([NPC account of constitutional development](http://www.npc.gov.cn/npc/c12434/dzlfxzgcl70nlflc/202108/t20210824_313204.html)). By 1982, with the reform programme under way, the 1978 text no longer fitted the political settlement.

The current Constitution was adopted on 4 December 1982 by the Fifth Session of the Fifth NPC. It was drafted by a Constitution Revision Committee in which Peng Zhen played the leading role, after the Third Plenary Session of December 1978 had shifted the Party toward reform and after the [1981 Resolution on Party history](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm) had settled how the Mao era would be judged. The connection matters: the Resolution repudiated the Cultural Revolution while defending Mao's historical place, and the Constitution gave that new settlement institutional form. The draft was more carefully prepared than its two predecessors and involved scholars and legal professionals alongside politicians and Party cadres. A contemporary study in *The China Quarterly* noted that the 1982 draft resembled the 1954 text in style and conception, unlike the "revolutionary, erratic" texts of 1975 and 1978 ([Byron Weng, "Some Key Aspects of the 1982 Draft Constitution of the People's Republic of China," *The China Quarterly* 91, September 1982, 492-506, DOI 10.1017/S0305741000000692](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/abs/some-key-aspects-of-the-1982-draft-constitution-of-the-peoples-republic-of-china/B9773E5744B8D799463BF08BDAD34E54)). Zhai Zhiyong's study of the making of the 1982 Constitution shows that the drafters consciously responded to the Cultural Revolution, including its destruction of historical relics, and restored the presidency and limits on its tenure, while moving explicit Party leadership from the main text back into the Preamble as it had been in 1954 ([Zhai Zhiyong, "The Making and Structure of the 1982 Constitution of China," *Tsinghua China Law Review* 8, 2016, 141](https://www.tsinghuachinalawreview.law.tsinghua.edu.cn/issues/info/10189)). The official white paper notes that "the Fifth Session of the Fifth NPC adopted the present Constitution, establishing the fundamental system of the country", and records amendments in 1988, 1993, 1999 and 2004 ([official white paper](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/white_paper/2014/09/09/content_281474986284659.htm)). The 2018 amendment is recorded in the official text.

The five amendments extended the constitutional settlement in stages. In 1988 the text was changed to allow private enterprise within limits; in 1993 it described China as being in the "primary stage of socialism" and renamed the goal a "socialist market economy"; in 1999 it added "rule of law" and gave a constitutional place to non-public ownership; in 2004 it added "the state shall respect and protect human rights" and protection of private property. Mo Jihong describes these changes as the incorporation of constitutionalist principles into the 1982 text ([Mo Jihong, "The Constitutional Law of the People's Republic of China and Its Development"](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/cjal/article/view/3287)). Each amendment is recorded in the [publication note appended to the official text](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/lawsregulations/201911/20/content_WS5ed8856ec6d0b3f0e9499913.html).

## How the document is organised

The Constitution has a Preamble and four chapters. Chapter I, General Principles, covers Articles 1 to 32. Chapter II, Fundamental Rights and Obligations of Citizens, covers Articles 33 to 56. Chapter III, State Institutions, covers Articles 57 to 140 in eight sections: the NPC; the President; the State Council; the Central Military Commission; local people's congresses and governments; autonomous organs of ethnic autonomous areas; Commissions of Supervision; and the people's courts and procuratorates. Chapter IV covers the national flag, anthem, emblem and capital, in Articles 141 to 143 ([table of contents in the official text](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/lawsregulations/201911/20/content_WS5ed8856ec6d0b3f0e9499913.html)).

## What the Preamble says

The Preamble combines a historical narrative with the statements of principle that the articles elaborate. Several phrases need explanation before the text is read. The "primary stage of socialism" is the doctrine that China will remain for a long time at an early level of socialist development, needing markets and material growth before a fuller socialism is possible. The "Scientific Outlook on Development" is the doctrine associated with Hu Jintao, and the "new development philosophy" is the formula associated with Xi Jinping. Both name changes in emphasis within the ruling party's programme. "Democratic centralism" means that organs are elected from below but must carry out decisions made at higher levels.

>
> After 1840, feudal China gradually became a semi-colonial, semi-feudal country. The Chinese people, wave upon wave, waged heroic struggles for national independence and liberation and for democracy and freedom.
>

>

>
> In 1949, after engaging in protracted, arduous and tortuous struggles, armed and in other forms, the Chinese people of all ethnic groups led by the Communist Party of China with Chairman Mao Zedong as its leader finally overthrew the rule of imperialism, feudalism and bureaucrat-capitalism, won a great victory in the New Democratic Revolution, and founded the People's Republic of China. The Chinese people thus secured power and became masters of their own country.
>

Our country will long remain in the primary stage of socialism. The fundamental task for our country is to concentrate on achieving socialist modernization along the road of socialism with Chinese characteristics. ## The key articles in context

The articles turn the principles of the Preamble into legal rules. They were written by a committee that included Party leaders, legal scholars and other advisers, then revised by successive NPC amendments. To read them is to read the outcome of political argument as well as legal drafting. In 1954 the principles of a planned transition were written into law. In 1975 the radical language of continuing revolution displaced much of the earlier framework. In 1978 some of it was restored while the old slogans remained. In 1982 the drafters built a more detailed structure for a reforming state, and later amendments adjusted it to a market economy and to the Party's changing view of its own role. The Communist Party of China (CPC) is the party that has governed the People's Republic since 1949.

The rights chapters show the same layering. Article 35 lists freedoms of speech, press, assembly and association, while Article 33 declares equality before the law and, after the 2004 amendment, that the state respects and protects human rights. Article 42 makes work both a right and an obligation, and Article 48 asserts equality for women. These are legal claims, and their practical force depends on institutions and political conditions. Mo Jihong notes that the amendments added rule of law, democracy and human rights to the constitutional text, while the ultimate authority over interpretation remains with Party and state organs rather than an independent court ([Mo Jihong, "The Constitutional Law of the People's Republic of China and Its Development"](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/cjal/article/view/3287)). A procuratorate is a state office that supervises prosecutions and legal oversight; Article 134 defines it as a legal oversight organ.

All quotations below are from the [official English text](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/lawsregulations/201911/20/content_WS5ed8856ec6d0b3f0e9499913.html).

### Chapter I: General Principles

**Article 1**: The socialist state and the leadership of the CPC:

> "The People's Republic of China is a socialist state governed by a people's democratic dictatorship that is led by the working class and based on an alliance of workers and peasants.
>
> The socialist system is the fundamental system of the People's Republic of China. Leadership by the Communist Party of China is the defining feature of socialism with Chinese characteristics. It is prohibited for any organization or individual to damage the socialist system."

**Article 2**: Popular sovereignty:

> "All power in the People's Republic of China belongs to the people.
>
> The organs through which the people exercise state power are the National People's Congress and the local people's congresses at all levels.
>
> The people shall, in accordance with the provisions of law, manage state affairs, economic and cultural undertakings, and social affairs through various channels and in various ways."

**Article 3**: Democratic centralism:

> "The state institutions of the People's Republic of China shall practice the principle of democratic centralism.
>
> The National People's Congress and the local people's congresses at all levels shall be created through democratic election and shall be responsible to the people and subject to their oversight.
>
> All administrative, supervisory, adjudicatory and procuratorial organs of the state shall be created by the people's congresses and shall be responsible to them and subject to their oversight.
>
> The division of functions and powers between the central and local state institutions shall honor the principle of giving full play to the initiative and motivation of local authorities under the unified leadership of the central authorities."

**Article 5**: Rule of law:

> "The People's Republic of China shall practice law-based governance and build a socialist state under the rule of law.
>
> The state shall safeguard the unity and sanctity of the socialist legal system.
>
> No law, administrative regulation or local regulation shall be in conflict with the Constitution.
>
> All state organs and armed forces, all political parties and social organizations, and all enterprises and public institutions must abide by the Constitution and the law. Accountability must be enforced for all acts that violate the Constitution or laws.
>
> No organization or individual shall have any privilege beyond the Constitution or the law."

**Article 6**: Socialist public ownership and distribution:

> "The foundation of the socialist economic system of the People's Republic of China is socialist public ownership of the means of production, that is, ownership by the whole people and collective ownership by the working people. The system of socialist public ownership has eradicated the system of exploitation of man by man, and practices the principle of 'from each according to his ability, to each according to his work.'
>
> In the primary stage of socialism, the state shall uphold a fundamental economic system under which public ownership is the mainstay and diverse forms of ownership develop together, and shall uphold an income distribution system under which distribution according to work is the mainstay, while multiple forms of distribution exist alongside it."

**Article 7**: The state sector:

> "The state sector of the economy, that is, the sector of the socialist economy under ownership by the whole people, shall be the leading force in the economy. The state shall ensure the consolidation and development of the state sector of the economy."

**Article 11**: Non-public sectors:

> "Non-public economic sectors that are within the scope prescribed by law, such as individually owned and private businesses, are an important component of the socialist market economy.
>
> The state shall protect the lawful rights and interests of non-public economic sectors such as individually owned and private businesses. The state shall encourage, support and guide the development of non-public economic sectors and exercise oversight and regulation over non-public economic sectors in accordance with law."

**Article 12**: Socialist public property:

> "Socialist public property is sacred and inviolable.
>
> The state shall protect socialist public property. It is prohibited for any organization or individual to seize or damage state or collective property by any means."

**Article 15**: Socialist market economy:

> "The state shall practice a socialist market economy.
>
> The state shall strengthen economic legislation and improve macro regulation.
>
> The state shall, in accordance with law, prohibit disruption of the socioeconomic order by any organization or individual."

**Article 24**: Socialist cultural-ethical advancement and core socialist values:

> "The state shall promote socialist cultural-ethical advancement through widely accessible education on ideals, morality, culture, discipline and law, and through the formulation and observance of different forms of rules of conduct and public pledges among different urban and rural populations.
>
> The state shall champion core socialist values; advocate the civic virtues of love for the motherland, for the people, for work, for science and for socialism; educate the people in patriotism and collectivism, in internationalism and communism, and in dialectical and historical materialism; and combat capitalist, feudal and other forms of decadent thought."

**Article 29**: The armed forces:

> "The armed forces of the People's Republic of China belong to the people. Their missions are to strengthen national defense, resist aggression, defend the motherland, safeguard the people's peaceful work, participate in national development, and work hard to serve the people.
>
> The state shall make the armed forces more revolutionary, more modernized and better regulated in order to strengthen national defense capabilities."

**Article 31**: Special administrative regions:

> "The state may establish special administrative regions when necessary. The systems instituted in special administrative regions shall, in light of specific circumstances, be prescribed by laws enacted by the National People's Congress."

### Chapter II: Fundamental Rights and Obligations of Citizens

**Article 33**: Citizenship, equality, human rights:

> "All persons holding the nationality of the People's Republic of China are citizens of the People's Republic of China.
>
> All citizens of the People's Republic of China are equal before the law.
>
> The state shall respect and protect human rights.
>
> Every citizen shall enjoy the rights prescribed by the Constitution and the law and must fulfill the obligations prescribed by the Constitution and the law."

**Article 35**: Freedoms:

> "Citizens of the People's Republic of China shall enjoy freedom of speech, the press, assembly, association, procession and demonstration."

**Article 42**: The right and obligation to work:

> "Citizens of the People's Republic of China shall have the right and the obligation to work.
>
> The state shall, in various ways, create employment opportunities, strengthen worker protections, improve working conditions and, based on the development of production, increase remuneration for work and work-related benefits.
>
> Work is an honorable duty for every citizen who is able to work. All working people in state owned enterprises and in urban and rural collective economic organizations should approach their own work as masters of their country. The state shall encourage socialist work contests and commend and award model workers and advanced workers. The state shall encourage citizens to participate in voluntary work.
>
> The state shall provide necessary pre-employment training for its citizens."

**Article 48**: Equality of women:

> "Women in the People's Republic of China shall enjoy equal rights with men in all spheres of life: political, economic, cultural, social and familial.
>
> The state shall protect the rights and interests of women, implement a system of equal pay for equal work, and train and select female officials."

### Chapter III: State Institutions

**Article 57**: The National People's Congress:

> "The National People's Congress of the People's Republic of China is the highest state organ of power. Its permanent organ is the National People's Congress Standing Committee."

**Article 62**: Functions and powers of the NPC (excerpt):

> "The National People's Congress shall exercise the following functions and powers:
> (1) amending the Constitution;
> (2) overseeing the enforcement of the Constitution;
> (3) enacting and amending criminal, civil, state institutional and other basic laws;
> (4) electing the president and the vice president of the People's Republic of China;
> ...
> (15) deciding on issues concerning war and peace; and
> (16) other functions and powers that the highest state organ of power should exercise."

**Article 64**: Constitutional amendment procedure:

> "Amendments to the Constitution must be proposed by the National People's Congress Standing Committee or by one-fifth or more of National People's Congress deputies and be adopted by a vote of at least two-thirds of National People's Congress deputies.
>
> Laws and other proposals shall be adopted by a majority vote of the National People's Congress deputies."

**Article 79**: The presidency:

> "The president and the vice president of the People's Republic of China shall be elected by the National People's Congress.
>
> Citizens of the People's Republic of China who have the right to vote and stand for election and who have reached the age of 45 are eligible for election as president or vice president of the People's Republic of China.
>
> The president and the vice president of the People's Republic of China shall have the same term of office as that of the National People's Congress."

**Article 134**: The procuratorate:

> "The people's procuratorates of the People's Republic of China are the legal oversight organs of the state."

## The 2018 amendment

The most recent change came on 11 March 2018, when the First Session of the Thirteenth NPC adopted an amendment containing 21 articles, numbered 32 to 52. It was the fifth amendment to the 1982 Constitution. The best-known changes were the removal of presidential and vice-presidential term limits from Article 79 and the insertion of "Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era" into the Preamble. Article 36 also added the sentence to Article 1 that "The defining feature of socialism with Chinese characteristics is the leadership of the Communist Party of China." Other changes are less visible but consequential. Article 37 altered Article 3 so that supervisory organs were named alongside administrative, adjudicatory and procuratorial organs. Article 40 required state functionaries to take a public oath of allegiance to the Constitution when taking office. Article 44 renamed the NPC Law Committee as the NPC Constitution and Law Committee. Article 46 added environmental protection to the duties of the State Council ([Library of Congress, "China: 2018 Constitutional Amendment Adopted," 18 May 2018](https://web.archive.org/web/20260204135431/https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2018-05-18/china-2018-constitutional-amendment-adopted/)).

The largest institutional change was the creation of a new branch of state organs. Article 52 added Section 7, "The Supervision Commissions," with Articles 123 to 127, establishing the National Supervisory Commission and local commissions. A supervisory commission is a state body that investigates corruption and misconduct by public officials. It operates alongside the Party's discipline inspection system. James W. C. Lee argues that this is the most far-reaching part of the amendment, because it alters a state structure that had been largely unchanged since the first Constitution of 1954. He also notes that the reform reverses earlier reform-era ideas of separating Party and state, and raises questions about the rights of people under investigation ([Lee, "Anti-Corruption in a Party-State: Constitutional Implications of China's Supervisory Reform," *Asian Journal of Comparative Law* 18, no. 3, December 2023, 389-406, DOI 10.1017/asjcl.2023.25](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/asian-journal-of-comparative-law/article/anticorruption-in-a-partystate-constitutional-implications-of-chinas-supervisory-reform/B412DDAEAFD9579CF24F11DFDD0AE002)). Other scholars disagree about how much practical difference the changes make. A 2019 study in *China Perspectives* argues that the three headline amendments are unlikely to alter constitutional practice in a substantial way, while warning that they signal a constitutionalised fusion of Party and state ([The 2018 Constitutional Amendments, *China Perspectives*, 2019](https://journals.openedition.org/chinaperspectives/8646)). The disagreement turns on whether one measures constitutional change by the text, by institutional behaviour or by political signal.

An official commentary, "Xi stresses writing new chapter in China's Constitution practice in new era", states that "After the current Constitution was enacted in 1982, the National People's Congress had conducted five amendments to some individual articles and contents, which were necessary and very important" ([english.www.gov.cn](https://english.www.gov.cn/news/topnews/202212/20/content_WS63a0f6dbc6d0a757729e4946.html)). The publication note below records the adopted text.

> "(Adopted at the Fifth Session of the Fifth National People's Congress and promulgated by the Announcement of the National People's Congress on December 4, 1982; amended in accordance with the Amendment to the Constitution of the People's Republic of China adopted at the First Session of the Seventh National People's Congress on April 12, 1988, the Amendment ... adopted at the First Session of the Eighth National People's Congress on March 29, 1993, the Amendment ... adopted at the Second Session of the Ninth National People's Congress on March 15, 1999, the Amendment ... adopted at the Second Session of the Tenth National People's Congress on March 14, 2004, and the Amendment ... adopted at the First Session of the Thirteenth National People's Congress on March 11, 2018)"
>
> Source: [Official text, english.www.gov.cn](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/lawsregulations/201911/20/content_WS5ed8856ec6d0b3f0e9499913.html)

The complete document has 143 articles and is published in the [official text](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/lawsregulations/201911/20/content_WS5ed8856ec6d0b3f0e9499913.html).

The Constitution is both a legal text and a record of political choices. Its Preamble tells a story of revolution and rehabilitation; its articles define institutions whose meaning has shifted with each amendment. Reading a clause without asking when and why it entered the text can give a false impression of stability. The 1954 assembly, the 1975 radicals, the 1978 compromisers, the 1982 reformers and the 2018 amenders each left marks on the same document.
