In October 1943 T. G. Narayanan, a correspondent for *The Hindu*, tried to get an ambulance for an old woman lying near Ballygunge station in Calcutta. He recorded what happened: "Hundreds of people passed by her. Not one cared enough to telephone for an ambulance. Before the ambulance I asked for came she was gone." Narayanan had been sent to the famine areas of Bengal, and his reports, later collected as *Famine Over Bengal*, were written by a man who watched destitutes arrive in the city. The Bengal famine of 1943 killed between one and two million people, according to the official inquiry, and left about six million seriously affected. It was the first of three events that shaped Indian communism in this period; the others were the peasant revolts of 1946 to 1951 and the uprising at Naxalbari in 1967. This chapter follows the witnesses to all three.

## Counting the hunger

Narayanan was not the only person taking note in Calcutta in 1943. Tarakchandra Das, a lecturer in anthropology at Calcutta University, organised a survey of 820 destitute family units, many interviewed within two weeks of leaving home. His team tabulated the reasons people gave; lack of food and work accounted for 65.73 percent of cases. Das wrote: "The Government and private organisations for relief were not adequate for the purpose at any stage." He described people eating refuse and recorded complaints that the millet gruel supplied by the government caused bowel complaints. The Famine Inquiry Commission, which heard witnesses in Calcutta and rural Bengal in 1944, put the scale in numbers: "Between one and two million people died as a result of the famine and the outbreaks of epidemic disease associated with it." An unnamed witness told the commission: "There was a feeling of tenseness and expectancy in Calcutta. Calcutta was largely empty. Houses were vacant." The commission found that most of the poor stayed in their villages and died there, and that high prices and a failure of distribution, not a single failed harvest, produced the starvation. Reporter, survey and official report differed over blame but broadly agreed on that point.

## Two-thirds

Three years later the politics of food moved from the relief kitchen to the field. Tebhaga was the demand of sharecroppers in Bengal that two-thirds of the crop, rather than half, be stored in their own granaries. In December 1946 the Communist Party of India sent Somnath Hore, a second-year art student, to document the movement in Rangpur district. Hore walked into Badgachha village with organisers from the peasant association and attended a night meeting of nearly 200 sharecroppers. He recorded the slogan: "We want Tebhaga. No one's afraid of the jotedars. They've lost their nerve after the hat hartal." A jotedar was a rural landlord or moneylender; the hat hartal was a strike at the local market. Hore noted a jotedar's attempt to divide Hindu and Muslim sharecroppers, and on 25 December wrote of a police threat to arrest the local leader Harikanta Sarkar: "The jotedars are obviously in a state of panic." The diary covers twelve days and a few villages, but it shows a movement of local decisions: meetings, granaries, a threatened arrest, and women directing visitors to the red flag camp.

## Telangana, and what could not be verified

In Telangana, in the south, the struggle turned on land and on the rule of the Nizam, the Muslim ruler of the princely state ([Sundarayya, *Telangana People's Struggle and Its Lessons*, 1972](https://www.etelangana.org/uploads/ebooks/Dr_Puchalapalli_Sundaraiah_2014-06-16_102752/2014-06-16_103038_S_STRUGGLE_AND_ITS_LESSONS_-_Pucchalapalli_Sudaraiah.pdf); [Dhanagare, 1974](https://www.marxists.org/subject/india/dhanagare-74.pdf)). P. Sundarayya, a leader of the revolt who later became general secretary of the Communist Party of India (Marxist), wrote its history in 1972. He traced it from the Andhra Mahasabha and the killing of Doddi Komarayya in 1946 through land distribution, village councils known as gram raj, the armed Razakar bands, the entry of the Indian army in 1948, the concentration camps in which Koya people died, and the withdrawal of 1951. The book carries appendices of named martyrs and a chapter on women, and is candid about its own limits: "We have not tried to edit the narration of the events, as given by some of the chroniclers, or as recorded in the available reports from the lower units to higher centres." Sundarayya conceded that exact numbers could no longer be verified, estimating about 4,000 killed by police, military and landlord terror between 1946 and 1951, with some investigators putting the figure nearer 6,000, excluding the Koya who died of disease in camps. A participant's history written for a party publisher is not a neutral account, but its admissions are part of its value.

## Siliguri, May 1967

Naxalbari, in the Darjeeling district in the north, began with evictions from vested land and tea-garden land ([*Naxalbari and Indian Revolution*, pp. 62–63](https://archive.org/download/naxalbari-and-indian-revolution/Naxalbari%20and%20Indian%20revolution_djvu.txt)). Jangal Santhal, chairman of the Siliguri Subdivision Peasant Samiti, gave his account on 24 May 1967: "But apart from attacks by jotedars, there is no instance of peasant violence". He described courts and police rather than guerrillas. Promode Sengupta, a supporter turned internal critic, later argued that the movement was an open economic struggle radicalised by police violence, and quoted Santhal in his book. That account competes with the one Charu Mazumdar gave in June 1968: "This is the first time that the peasants have struggled not only for their partial demands but for the seizure of state power." Each comes from a participant with an interest in the outcome; none offers independent evidence of what the peasants wanted.

## Spring thunder

The argument over Naxalbari was conducted in public by rival communist organisations. On 5 July 1967 the editorial page of *People's Daily*, organ of the Chinese Communist Party's central committee, opened: "A peal of spring thunder has crashed over the land of India. Revolutionary peasants in the Darjeeling area have risen in rebellion." It praised the seizure of grain, land and weapons and set out the Maoist prescription of rural base areas and protracted armed struggle. In August the central committee of the Communist Party of India (Marxist), or CPI(M), rejected that endorsement at its Madurai session, complaining that Chinese comrades were applauding expelled extremists: "We are at a loss to understand how the comrades of the Chinese Communist Party, in utter violation of every Marxist-Leninist tenet ... are advocating armed struggle, as seen in the case of Naxalbari peasantry." Mazumdar, by then a leader of the separate revolutionary committee, defended the rising in its own paper. In March 1969 he described visiting the insurgent area of Srikakulam: "Here, in the midst of a jungle surrounded by hills, I am sitting in a room on a hill-top and before me are seated about a score of young men." He asked whether Srikakulam would become the Yenan of India, invoking the Chinese revolutionary base as an analogy. These four documents show the same uprising hailed, condemned, explained and propagated within a single political world.

## How historians have read it

- **Amartya Sen, *Poverty and Famines: An Essay on Entitlement and Deprivation* (1981).** Argues that famines can occur without a fall in food availability, because particular groups lose the ability to command food; this supports the Famine Inquiry Commission's stress on distribution and high prices rather than harvest failure alone. [Internet Archive: povertyfamineses0000sena](https://archive.org/details/povertyfamineses0000sena)
- **D. N. Dhanagare, "Social origins of the peasant insurrection in Telangana (1946–51)" (1974).** A structural class analysis of the 1946–51 revolt, attentive to land control and the internal differentiation of the peasantry; it broadly agrees with Sundarayya on agrarian roots but treats communist leadership claims with more analytical distance. [Marxists Internet Archive: dhanagare 74](https://www.marxists.org/subject/india/dhanagare-74.pdf)
- **D. N. Dhanagare, *Peasant Movements in India 1920–1950* (1983).** Places Tebhaga and Telangana in a comparative frame and argues that populist mobilisation and class structure need to be studied together; a useful corrective to accounts written by participants. [Internet Archive: peasantmovements0000dndh](https://archive.org/details/peasantmovements0000dndh)
- **Sumanta Banerjee, *In the Wake of Naxalbari: A History of the Naxalite Movement in India* (1980, reprinted 2008).** A full-length history of the movement and its aftermath; its narrative should be set against the contemporary CPI(M) resolution and Sengupta's internal critique rather than treated as definitive.
- **Kavita Panjabi, *Unclaimed Harvest: An Oral History of the Tebhaga Women's Movement* (Zubaan, 2017).** Uses oral history and memory studies to recover women participants; the obvious place to look for voices missing from Hore's diary, though the interviews themselves have not been used here.
- **Partha Chatterjee, introduction to Promode Sengupta's *Naxalbari and Indian Revolution* (1982/1983).** Reads Sengupta's polemic as a historical document within an Indian Left debate, not as a neutral forecast of failure; agrees with later scholarship that Naxalbari forced a rethinking of state and class power. [Internet Archive: naxalbari and indian revolution](https://archive.org/details/naxalbari-and-indian-revolution)

There is broad agreement on the distributional cause of the famine and on the importance of land relations in the peasant movements. Disagreement continues over the class composition and leadership of Telangana, over the significance to assign to Naxalbari, and over whether it began as an economic or an insurrectionary revolt. These should not be flattened into a single verdict.

## Limits of the record

The surviving first-hand record is uneven and politically shaped. Most of the named witnesses are men who were organisers, journalists or officials. The voices of sharecropping women, Adivasis and the families of the dead reach us through them. Hore's two women in Badgachha are figures observed by a young party activist, not interviewed at length; Sundarayya gives a chapter to women in Telangana; Das tabulates female-headed units. The Tebhaga testimony is one artist's twelve days in a small area. The Telangana narrative was written by a party leader twenty years afterwards and does not name most participants. The Naxalbari documents are all factional positions. Several key texts reach us in translation from Bengali whose translators are not always identified, and the scanned editions contain errors; exact wording needs a check against the printed originals before publication.

Material that could have answered some of these questions has not been examined. Kavita Panjabi's interviews with Tebhaga women, the Srikakulam district committee report praised by Mazumdar, and the full statement by Jangal Santhal in the 1967 journal issue in which it appeared have not been opened. Nor have the Bengal Legislative Assembly debates on the tenancy bill, or police and court records for Tebhaga, Telangana and Naxalbari. Bengali and Telugu newspapers and unpublished party papers would need a physical archive visit. No famine photograph has been examined as a primary object; the plates listed in Das's report are noted only. The Bengal Famine Archive is a contemporary art-and-memory project, not a source of 1943 testimony. The famine's contested death-toll estimates and the question of deliberate policy cannot be settled from these texts alone.
