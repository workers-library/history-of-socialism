In 1919 a Vietnamese nationalist appeared at the Paris Peace Conference, where the Allies were settling the end of the First World War, and asked for civil rights inside the French empire rather than independence. Nguyễn Ái Quoc, later known as Ho Chi Minh, presented claims on behalf of Vietnamese patriots. One was "Complete amnesty of Vietnamese political prisoners." ([List of Claims of the Annamese People, 1919](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1919/0001.htm)). He also asked for equal legal treatment with Europeans, freedom of press and opinion, technical schools, the rule of law instead of decrees, and a Vietnamese representative in the French Parliament. Addressed to an audience that could grant reforms, the petition's moderation reflects that audience as much as his convictions. It is the starting point of a revolution that changed its programme more than once.

## From petition to party, 1919 to 1930

At the Fifth Congress of the Communist International in 1924, the Moscow-directed body that guided communist parties abroad, Nguyễn Ái Quoc argued that colonial liberation belonged to the working-class revolution. He criticised the French and British parties for neglecting colonial questions and described plantations of 20,000 to 25,000 hectares, forced labour and a land tax raised by 30 percent after floods. "Ninety-five per cent of the population are peasants." ([report to the Fifth Comintern Congress, 8 July 1924](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1924/07/08.htm)) was his summary of the French colonies, though his figures elsewhere were second-hand. In 1930, convening the founding meeting in Hong Kong, he announced: "The Indochinese Communist Party has been founded. It is the Party of the working class. It will help the proletariat lead the revolution waged for the sake of all oppressed and exploited people." ([founding appeal, 18 February 1930](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1930/02/18.htm)). Proletariat means the industrial working class. The ten slogans included an eight-hour day, universal education, equality between women and men, and land for poor peasants. Looking back in 1960, Ho described reading Lenin's thesis on colonies: "This is what we need, this is the path to our liberation!" ([*The Path Which Led Me to Leninism*, first published April 1960](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1960/04/x01.htm)). That account is retrospective, written thirty years later.

## Prison poems, 1942 to 1943

Between 1942 and 1943 Ho was held by Chiang Kai-shek's police while travelling towards Chongqing. He wrote poems in classical Chinese because his jailers would have found Vietnamese suspicious, filling a worn notebook with what the editor of the published edition calls a hundred-odd quatrains. The sequence records shackles, transfer through thirteen districts of Guangxi and eighteen prisons, meagre rice, scabies, cold, and the death of a fellow prisoner from hunger. "Four months of a subhuman life, it appears, / Have aged me even more than ten years." ([*Four Months Have Passed*, 1942–43](https://archive.org/download/prison-diary-ho-chi-minh/Prison%20Diary_djvu.txt)). Elsewhere: "Thy body is in jail. / But thy spirit, never." ([*Prison Diary*, 1942–43](https://archive.org/download/prison-diary-ho-chi-minh/Prison%20Diary_djvu.txt)). These are verses, not reportage, and they reach the reader through an unnamed translator in a 1972 Hanoi edition whose printed text has scanning errors. They are the closest surviving record of Ho's private experience between the 1930s and the insurrection of 1945.

## The August Revolution of 1945

Trường Chinh, the party's general secretary, wrote within a year of the insurrection and revised the account twelve years later. Local uprisings began on 11 and 13 August, before the general order of the night of 13 August. A congress at Tân Trạo followed on 16 August, demonstrations in Hanoi on 17 and 18 August, and the seizure of the capital on 19 August: "On August 19, the entire capital rose up." ([Trường Chinh, *The August Revolution*, written 1946, revised 1958](https://archive.org/download/the-august-revolution-truong-chinh-1958/TheAugustRevolution-TruongChinh-1958_djvu.txt)). He explains the rush: an earlier rising would have been crushed, a later one might have left a Japanese-backed or restored French administration in power. "Hundreds of thousands of people starved beside granaries full of rice kept by the Japanese and French." ([same work](https://archive.org/download/the-august-revolution-truong-chinh-1958/TheAugustRevolution-TruongChinh-1958_djvu.txt)). The party had dropped the agrarian-revolution slogan, he records, and decided not to confiscate landlords' land in order to keep a broad front against Japan and France. His fourth chapter is a list of failures: the south rose late, Japanese troops were not fully disarmed, counter-revolutionaries were not firmly repressed and the Bank of Indochina was not seized. "For a new-born revolutionary power, to be lenient with counter-revolutionaries is tantamount to committing suicide." ([same work](https://archive.org/download/the-august-revolution-truong-chinh-1958/TheAugustRevolution-TruongChinh-1958_djvu.txt)). The book is a participant's explanation of a victory and a self-criticism at once.

At Ba Đình Square in Hanoi on 2 September, Ho read the Declaration of Independence, opening with the American and French declarations of rights before listing French abuses, the famine and the Japanese surrender. "The truth is that we have wrested our independence from the Japanese and not from the French." ([Vietnamese Declaration of Independence, 2 September 1945](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1945/declaration-independence.htm)). The claim that more than two million died in the famine was the new government's figure, given without method. Eighteen days later Ho wrote to older people asking them to form a National Salvation Association: "At present our independence and freedom have just been won back, but we still have to go through many difficulties in order to consolidate them." ([*Letter To Old People*, 20 September 1945](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1945/09/20.htm)).

## War and Điện Biên Phủ, 1946 to 1954

Negotiation with France collapsed in December 1946 ([Ho Chi Minh, *Wage Resistance War!*, 19 December 1946](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1946/december/19.htm)). Ho's appeal of 19 December called on the whole population to fight with whatever was to hand: "Those who have rifles will use their rifles. Those who have swords will use their swords. Those who have no swords will use their spades, hoes, and sticks." ([*Wage Resistance War!*, 19 December 1946](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1946/december/19.htm)). The transcription comes from a participant's memoir rather than an official gazette. In March 1951 Ho closed a congress that merged the Việt Minh, the wartime independence front, with another front and founded the Vietnam Workers' Party, claiming Soviet and Chinese recognition as the previous year's great political victory ([congress speech, 3 March 1951](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1951/03/03.htm)). On 12 December 1953 he wrote to the Điện Biên Phủ front, combining military orders with political training: "Resolute to kill the enemy, / Resolute to abide by the policy, / Resolute to win more successes." ([letter to the front, 12 December 1953](https://archive.org/download/dien-bien-phu-vo-nguyen-giap/Dien%20Bien%20Phu_djvu.txt)).

Võ Nguyên Giáp, the commander, wrote the best-known account ten years later. He records how the French fortified a remote valley and how his own plan changed, then gives the scale of the supply effort: "our dan cong serving in transport reached hundreds of thousands, our lines of supply were as long as from 300 to 500km." ([Giáp, *Dien Bien Phu*, 1964](https://archive.org/download/dien-bien-phu-vo-nguyen-giap/Dien%20Bien%20Phu_djvu.txt)). Dân công were civilian labourers who built roads and carried food, ammunition and wounded. The final assault began on 7 May 1954; "At 5.30 p.m., we occupied the command post: Gen. De Castries and all his staff were captured." ([same work](https://archive.org/download/dien-bien-phu-vo-nguyen-giap/Dien%20Bien%20Phu_djvu.txt)). Ho's congratulatory letter of 8 May named soldiers, porters, shock youth, young volunteer workers, and local people, and warned: "This victory is big, but it is only the beginning. We must not be self-complacent and subjective into underestimating the enemy." ([letter of 8 May 1954](https://archive.org/download/dien-bien-phu-vo-nguyen-giap/Dien%20Bien%20Phu_djvu.txt)). The battle survives in the first-hand record mainly in the victor's own words.

After the fighting, the government turned to literacy and to the class questions left by land reform. At a mass education conference in July 1956 Ho reported 2.1 million people in classes and over 90 percent illiteracy in earlier years, then warned cadres: "Making efforts does not mean issuing order and compelling people to come and study, or to force them to study beyond their capacities" ([instructions to the mass education conference, 16 July 1956](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1956/07/16.htm)). Both figures are the government's own. His instruction that children of landlords and rich peasants who rejected their parents' exploitation could teach and hold citizenship rights points to the categories the land reform had created. A letter marking a new autonomous region in the north-west in May 1955 told cadres to fight "bureaucracy, commandism, corruption and waste" and offered amnesty to those who had gone astray, which suggests that dissent or collaboration outlasted the fighting ([letter to the Thai-Meo Autonomous Region, 7 May 1955](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/ho-chi-minh/works/1955/05/07.htm)).

## Limits of the record

The land reform of 1953 to 1956 is the central gap. It touched more villagers than any other campaign, yet no peasant diary, letter or testimony from it survives in the English-language record. What exists comes chiefly from a study written for the US government from interviews with prisoners, defectors and returnees, and from exiled accounts quoted inside it. That study gives four incompatible estimates of the dead: 10,000 to 15,000 killed and 50,000 to 100,000 deported or imprisoned; about 50,000 executed and at least twice as many sent to forced labour; and a claimed 100,000 deaths. Its text calls the evidence "meager" and describes the campaign as "the only occasion on which the party in power has used violence on a large scale" ([Arnsten and Leites, RAND memorandum, 1970](https://archive.org/details/arnsten-leites-land-reform-in-rural-vietnam)), and its interviews served a counter-insurgency audience. Giáp presents the 1952–53 reform as a source of army strength, with no mention of coercion or deaths. The two describe the same campaign as mobilisation and as violence, and none of the figures should be adopted.

Almost every first-hand voice here belongs to the leadership. Ho's writings are state-published translations with unnamed translators; Trường Chinh and Giáp wrote retrospectively for state publishers and revised their texts; the prison poems survive in a 1972 translation with uneven scanning; and the Declaration's famine figure has no stated method. No peasant testimony, village record, trial transcript or party investigation report from the reform was available. No first-person account by a French soldier, settler or neutral correspondent at Điện Biên Phủ was read, and no photograph with a verified provenance was used. The record establishes what leaders said and how they explained their decisions, not how villagers experienced the insurrection, the trials or the portering, nor how many died.

## How historians have read it

- **Edwin E. Moise, *Land Reform in China and North Vietnam: Consolidating the Revolution at the Village Level*, University of North Carolina Press, 1983.** A comparative village-level study of how land reform consolidated state power; the standard scholarly corrective to both celebratory and exile accounts. [Internet Archive: landreforminchin00mois](https://archive.org/details/landreforminchin00mois)
- **Peter M. Worthing, *Occupation and Revolution: China and the Vietnamese August Revolution of 1945*, Institute of East Asian Studies, University of California, Berkeley, 2001.** Places the 1945 insurrection in the context of the Chinese occupation zone and the Japanese surrender; useful for testing the party's own timing narrative. [Internet Archive: occupationrevolu0000wort](https://archive.org/details/occupationrevolu0000wort)
- **Bernard B. Fall, *The Two Viet-Nams: A Political and Military Analysis*, Praeger, 1963 (revised 1964, 1967).** The mid-century reference account; Fall also edited Ho's writings and collected witness reports, though his land reform estimates are themselves second-hand. [Internet Archive: twovietnams0000unse](https://archive.org/details/twovietnams0000unse)
- **Bernard B. Fall, *Viet-Nam Witness, 1953–66*, Praeger, 1966.** First-person reportage and commentary by a journalist who was in Vietnam during the war; a check on the official military narrative. [Internet Archive: vietnamwitness1900fall](https://archive.org/details/vietnamwitness1900fall)
- **Michael Arnsten and Nathan Leites, *Land Reform and the Quality of Propaganda in Rural Vietnam*, RAND RM-5764-ARPA, 1970.** Analysts working for the US government who used interviews with people who had lived in the North; unusually candid about the weakness of the evidence for land reform deaths and about their own side's propaganda failures. [Internet Archive: arnsten leites land reform in rural vietnam](https://archive.org/details/arnsten-leites-land-reform-in-rural-vietnam)
- **James Walker Trullinger, *Village at War: An Account of Revolution in Vietnam*, Longman, 1980.** A village-level study of revolution in Thôn Mỹ Thủy, representative of work that shifts attention from Hanoi and the battlefield to local experience. [Internet Archive: villageatwaracco0000trul](https://archive.org/details/villageatwaracco0000trul)

Scholars agree that the Việt Minh seized power in August 1945 in the gap created by Japan's surrender, that the declaration of 2 September asserted independence, that the French return produced war in 1946, that the 1953–56 land reform involved extensive redistribution and violence, and that Điện Biên Phủ was a decisive victory in May 1954. They disagree over how much of 1945 was party preparation rather than opportunistic timing, how many people the land reform killed and imprisoned, whether to read it as mobilisation or repression, and how decisive Chinese aid was to the 1954 victory. The RAND memorandum's own range of estimates shows that the death-toll question is not settled by any single credible source.
