On 25 June 1793 Jacques Roux stood before the National Convention in Paris and asked what the revolution's declaration of rights was worth to a person without bread. A constitutional priest and an officer of the Paris municipality, he spoke for two sections and the Cordeliers Club and said: "Freedom is nothing but a vain phantom when one class of men can starve another with impunity." ([Jacques Roux, address to the National Convention, 25 June 1793, translated by Mitchell Abidor](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/roux/1793/enrages01.htm)). He demanded the death penalty for hoarders and monopolists, the requisition of necessities and a constitutional ban on speculation, and he cited women's low wages as evidence. He asked: "And can the property of knaves be more sacred than the life of a man?" ([same address, 25 June 1793](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/roux/1793/enrages01.htm)). Roux spoke in his own name. "Enragés" is a label attached afterwards to a current of angry speakers, not a party he led. His question survived him: legal equality could leave the means of life in the hands of a few.

## The common happiness

In the Arsenal section a society's rules of July 1793 admitted women and men on equal terms, met four times a week, rotated officers and charged ten sous a month so the poor were not excluded. That is one section's rules, not every club in the city. It shows the world of meeting rooms and subscriptions in which the 1790s left worked. A police observer examining Roux after Marat's assassination treated his look at Marat during a quarrel as evidence of revenge, noting two witnesses' surprise. Roux denied the threat and was released once Charlotte Corday was arrested. The report shows how readily a facial expression became evidence of intent.

Babeuf had reached a position of his own earlier. In a private letter of 8 July 1787 the young researcher of feudal land titles in Picardy, not yet calling himself Gracchus, found an equal division of goods better than equal rights of inheritance. He was reading Rousseau rather than repeating Jacobin slogans. In 1794, commissioned by the Convention to attack Jean-Baptiste Carrier, he itemised depositions about the drownings at Nantes and called for Carrier's execution, insisting that such crimes must never go unpunished. His approval of the acquittal of Carrier's co-defendants sits awkwardly beside his later defence of insurrection.

Released after the rising of 13 Vendémiaire in October 1795, Babeuf announced a paper. Its prospectus gave the maxim: "The goal of society is the common happiness." ([Gracchus Babeuf, prospectus for *Le Tribun du Peuple*, 1795, translated by Mitchell Abidor](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/conspiracy-equals/1795/tribun-peuple.htm)). He promised irregular issues and argued that a people's paper must tell the people everything, not keep secrets from them. A prospectus is a promise to subscribers, not evidence of what the paper achieved.

## The Republic of Equals

By April 1796 the programme was on Paris walls. The *Analysis of the Doctrine of Babeuf*, a placard sanctioned by the movement's leaders though not written by Babeuf, set out equal natural right, work as an obligation, labour and pleasures in common, and the constitution of 1793. Its declaration was blunt: "In a real society there should be neither rich nor poor." ([*Analysis of the Doctrine of Babeuf* (placard), April 1796, translated by Mitchell Abidor](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/conspiracy-equals/1797/placard.htm)). Another article added: "The rich who don't renounce their excess in favor of the indigent are the enemies of the people." ([same placard, Article 8, 1796](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/conspiracy-equals/1797/placard.htm)). Babeuf denied authorship in court but accepted the doctrine. The prosecution made the placard central to its charge of planned pillage.

Sylvain Maréchal, a poet and militant atheist in the secret leadership, wrote the *Manifesto of the Equals* that year. It moved past the declarations of 1791 and 1795 and past the agrarian law, meaning a redistribution of land. Its demand was explicit: "We lean towards something more sublime and more just: the common good or the community of property! No more individual property in land: the land belongs to no one." ([Sylvain Maréchal, *Manifesto of the Equals*, 1796, translated by Mitchell Abidor](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/conspiracy-equals/1796/manifesto.htm)). Its opening was plain: "We aspire to live and die equal, the way we were born: we want real equality or death; this is what we need." ([same manifesto, 1796](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/conspiracy-equals/1796/manifesto.htm)). It called the French Revolution the forerunner of a greater and final revolution. Its most contested line was "Let all the arts perish, if need be, as long as real equality remains!" ([same manifesto, 1796](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/conspiracy-equals/1796/manifesto.htm)). Even among the directors that call was disputed. The manifesto says nothing about colonial slaves and offers no sustained argument about women's equality.

Not everyone would read a manifesto. The movement also wrote songs. The *Song of the Equals*, with words by the conspirator Charles Germain, repeated a refrain about nature and the sun: "People! Take hold of your rights, / The sun shines for all." (["Song of the Equals," words by Germain, 1796, translated by Mitchell Abidor](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/conspiracy-equals/1796/song.htm)). It invoked Marat, Saint-Just and Robespierre and addressed the sans-culottes, the artisans and wage earners of Paris street politics, fusing equality with martyrdom and heroic violence. A song's circulation cannot be measured from its words alone.

## A directory of four

Seized papers show what the organisation planned. A document found at Babeuf's arrest provided for an Insurrectionary Directory of four secret members, intermediaries so agents would not know the leaders' names, and a mark on orders to guard against forgeries. Agents were to report on opinion in the twelve arrondissements and sound out soldiers. That is evidence about clandestine practice, not proof the structure worked or a rising was ready. After his arrest, writing as Tissot, Babeuf offered the government a bargain: "Would you regard it as beneath you to treat with me as between power and power?" ([Gracchus Babeuf to the Executive Directory, 12 May 1796, translated in Bax, 1911](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bax/1911/babeuf/ch07.htm)). He boasted of the confidence in him and warned that a public trial would let him plead the eternal rights of the people. The prosecution later used the letter against him.

Much of what is known about the projected society comes from Philippe Buonarroti, a directory member writing in 1828, over thirty years after the failure. His reconstructed decree combined a national community of goods, abolition of inheritance, common labour, meals and rations, free medical care, state control of foreign trade and the abolition of money. It allowed machines to reduce toil and a semaphore telegraph, but also forced labour for the idle and punishment for keeping money. A projected speech to a victorious crowd offered to let the people censure and punish leaders who betrayed them; it was never delivered. Buonarroti claimed 17,000 were ready to rise, a participant's claim, not a finding. The decree, speech and last letter survive because he printed them in a book defending the dead and building a lineage.

## The trial and the inheritance

At Vendôme Babeuf faced the High Court from February to May 1797. He admitted preaching a system of common welfare but said it had been a proposal dependent on consent. "As for the second question, I have preached the system of the welfare of all only as a simple philanthropic speculation, as a simple proposition to the people, depending entirely upon the condition of their acquiescence," he told the jury ([Gracchus Babeuf, defence at Vendôme, 1797, translated in Fried and Sanders, 1964](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/conspiracy-equals/1797/defense-speech.htm)). Elsewhere he quoted his own earlier journalism: "There are periods in which the ultimate effect of the cruel social order is that the whole of the society's wealth is concentrated in the hands of a few." ([same defence, 1797](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/conspiracy-equals/1797/defense-speech.htm)). The speech was a legal defence under sentence of death, self-exculpatory where the evidence was strongest. The seized documents point the other way.

On the eve of execution he wrote of his family's survival and the fate of republican families under a royalist reaction: "I couldn't conceive of any other way to make you happy than through the happiness of all. I failed; I sacrificed myself; it is also for you that I die." ([Gracchus Babeuf, last letter to his wife and children, 1797, translated by Mitchell Abidor](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/revolution/conspiracy-equals/1797/last-letter.htm)). He was sentenced on 7 Prairial, Year V (26 May 1797) and guillotined the next day, 8 Prairial (27 May 1797). Bax printed the revolutionary date of the sentence with the Gregorian date of the execution, a one-day conversion slip. The two dates name different events rather than a conflict in the record.

Marx and Engels later claimed this current as an ancestor. In *The Holy Family* Marx traced a line from the Cercle Social through Leclerc and Roux to Babeuf and Buonarroti, a genealogy built decades afterwards. The documents let a reader weigh it. They show a politics of material equality, a newspaper, placards, songs and a secret directory, and an argument that has not closed: whether a minority may act for a people it believes has been misled. The police report on Roux and the prosecution at Vendôme are hostile sources written to secure convictions; neither is a neutral account.

## How historians have read it

- R. B. Rose, *Gracchus Babeuf: The First Revolutionary Communist* (Stanford University Press, 1978). The standard English scholarly biography, based on archival work and broadly accepted as the reliable factual account. [Internet Archive: gracchusbabeuffi0000rose](https://archive.org/details/gracchusbabeuffi0000rose)
- R. B. Rose, *The Enragés: Socialists of the French Revolution?* (Melbourne University Press / Cambridge University Press, 1965). Argues from the printed tracts that the Enragés were not a coherent party and that calling them socialists overstates the evidence. [Internet Archive: enragessocialist0000rose](https://archive.org/details/enragessocialist0000rose)
- Ian Birchall, *The Spectre of Babeuf* (Macmillan, 1997), and "The Babeuf Bicentenary: Conspiracy or Revolutionary Party?", *International Socialism* 2:72 (September 1996). Reads Babeuf as an original thinker and organiser closer to workers' self-activity than to later conspiratorial models, and rejects both the Furet continuity thesis and the dismissive "utopian" label. [Marxists Internet Archive: babeuf](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/writers/birchall/1996/xx/babeuf.htm)
- Herbert Marcuse, "The Defense of Gracchus Babeuf Before the High Court of Vendôme" (1969). Treats the defence as a text about popular sovereignty, "true interests" and the legitimacy of revolution by a minority, stressing the authoritarian implication of claiming to know the people's real interests. [Marxists Internet Archive: babeuf](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/marcuse/works/1969/babeuf.htm)
- Daniel Guérin, *Class Struggle in the First French Republic: Bourgeois and Bras Nus, 1793–1795* (Pluto, London, 1977; translation of *La Lutte de classes sous la Première République*, 1968). A class analysis that gives the sans-culottes and the Parisian left a central place, critical of Babeuf for not relying more on working-class struggle. [Internet Archive: classstruggleinf0000guri](https://archive.org/details/classstruggleinf0000guri)
- Albert Mathiez, *Le Directoire* (Paris, 1934). The classic study of the Directory that supplies the subscription-list and vote figures used by later writers; its documentary findings remain a reference point even where its social interpretation is contested. [Internet Archive: bwb KV 731 152](https://archive.org/details/bwb_KV-731-152)

There is broad agreement that Babeuf held a communitarian theory of property, that the 1796 organisation existed, and that Buonarroti's book shaped the movement's later reputation. Whether the Enragés belong in the same lineage, whether the organisation was conspiratorial or mass-based, and whether Babeuf's egalitarianism was democratic or authoritarian in implication remain open questions. François Furet's claim of a direct line from Babeuf through Blanqui to Lenin is a well-known argument that Birchall rejects. It belongs in the dispute, not among settled conclusions.

## Limits of the record

The original 1797 prosecution volumes, among them the *Exposé fait par les accusateurs nationaux*, the *Copie des Pièces Saisies* and the *Débats du procès*, and a full run of *Le Tribun du Peuple* are not part of this record. What survives here rests on transcriptions and translations, several made in the 1960s or in 1911, and the French originals remain to be checked before any quotation is treated as final. The Buonarroti problem is structural: the victory speech, the economic decree and the last letter come from a book written by a participant who needed to defend his comrades' memory in 1828. The hostile side is weaker still, since the prosecution's words are known here only as quotations inside Birchall and Bax. The surviving testimony is overwhelmingly the writing of literate men in a clandestine leadership. Ordinary supporters appear as entries in agents' contact lists, as crowds around a placard reported by an agent, or not at all. Women are almost absent apart from general praise and names such as Sophie Lapierre, and Maréchal's later views on women's education are a warning against assuming the movement was egalitarian on every axis. No figure for the number of potential participants can be treated as established.
