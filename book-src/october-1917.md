At ten in the evening of 7 November 1917, by the calendar used in Western Europe, a shot was fired inside the Winter Palace. S. L. Maslov, the Minister of Supplies in the Provisional Government, was in the building and wrote down what he heard within days: "At 10:00 PM a shot was fired in the palace, followed by cries and shots from the cadets." (S. L. Maslov, "The Taking of the Winter Palace," *Delo Naroda*, 11 November 1917. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/bolsheviks-seize-power/bolsheviks-seize-power-texts/taking-of-the-winter-palace/)) In Russia the same date was 25 October old style, the calendar running thirteen days behind. The government whose minister sat in the palace had been formed in February, after the tsar's abdication, and had never commanded a reliable armed force. The Bolsheviks, the party led by Lenin, held a majority in the Petrograd Soviet by the autumn. What happened that night was an armed seizure carried out through the Soviet's Military Revolutionary Committee, and the men and women who described it disagreed about almost everything except the government's isolation.

## The decision

Lenin pressed for insurrection from hiding in the Vyborg district. In a letter of 12 to 14 September 1917, later published in 1921, he set out the strategic case before any date was fixed: "The present task must be an armed uprising in Petrograd and Moscow (with its region), the seizing of power and the overthrow of the government." (V. I. Lenin, "The Bolsheviks Must Assume Power," 12–14 September 1917. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1917/sep/14.htm)) This was an argument addressed to party colleagues, not a record of what they decided. Its claim that a majority of the people stood with the Bolsheviks was an assertion, and the elections to the Constituent Assembly later that year returned a plurality for the Socialist-Revolutionaries. On 10 October old style, 23 October new style, the Central Committee carried a resolution moved by Lenin, by ten votes to two, with Zinoviev and Kamenev dissenting. "all this places the armed uprising on the order of the day." (Bolshevik Central Committee resolution, 10 (23) October 1917. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/bolsheviks-seize-power/bolsheviks-seize-power-texts/central-committee-decides-on-armed-uprising/)) The resolution fixed no date, which is why historians dispute how far Lenin imposed his will on the party.

On the evening of 24 October he demanded action that night. "The situation is critical in the extreme. In fact it is now absolutely clear that to delay the uprising would be fatal." (V. I. Lenin, letter to Central Committee members, evening of 24 October (6 November) 1917. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1917/oct/24.htm)) The same letter subordinated the coming Congress of Soviets to the act itself: "The seizure of power is the business of the uprising; its political purpose will become clear after the seizure." (Lenin, same letter, 24 October 1917. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1917/oct/24.htm)) In public the body that would carry out the seizure said the opposite. The Military Revolutionary Committee, or MRC, was the Petrograd Soviet's operational arm. On 6 November it declared: "the Military Revolutionary Committee hereby declares that it exists not to make preparations to seize power but solely to defend the interests of the Petrograd garrison and the democracy against counter-revolution and pogroms." (Military Revolutionary Committee declaration, 6 November 1917. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/bolsheviks-seize-power/bolsheviks-seize-power-texts/declarations-of-the-military-revolutionary-committee/)) Placed beside Lenin's private letter, this is plainly a statement for an audience that included the garrison and the moderate socialists. It tells us what the Committee said, not what it intended.

## The night in the palace

Smolny, a former school used as the Soviet's headquarters, held the MRC's crowded upper room. Anatoly Lunacharsky, a Bolshevik who became the first Commissar of Enlightenment, remembered orders dictated and signed on knees, typists working through the night and couriers leaving with mandates. "The dashing young sailors who came to tell the truth about the fighting then going on around the Winter Palace were listened to in admiration." (Anatoly Lunacharsky, "Smolny on the Night of the Storm," date of writing not stated on the source page. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lunachar/19xx/xx/night-storm.htm)) This is an impression written by a participant in power, not a minute of the MRC. Leon Trotsky, who chaired the Petrograd Soviet and helped lead the insurrection, was more candid about its execution when he wrote in 1930: "The plan as a whole was too heavy and complicated for the problem it aimed to solve. The time allotted for preparation proved inadequate." (Leon Trotsky, *The History of the Russian Revolution*, ch. 45, 1930. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1930/hrr/ch45.htm)) He described units that failed to appear, confusion over the plan, Kronstadt sailors arriving late, and several deadlines passing while the Congress waited.

Inside the palace the cabinet refused to transfer power to any body but the Constituent Assembly. Maslov records a twenty-minute ultimatum delivered by Antonov, a cadet guard, a grenade that wounded two cadets, the arrest of the ministers and a march to the Peter and Paul Fortress through a hostile crowd. "The mob seemed determined to take the law into its own hands and one of the ministers was jostled a bit." (Maslov, "The Taking of the Winter Palace," 11 November 1917. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/bolsheviks-seize-power/bolsheviks-seize-power-texts/taking-of-the-winter-palace/)) He was a defeated minister writing for a Socialist-Revolutionary newspaper and could not see the city beyond the palace walls. The commissar appointed by the MRC to the cruiser *Aurora* gave the other side's version in a report dated 7 November and printed in 1923. "Toward evening we received orders from the Military Revolutionary Committee to fire a few blank shots upon receiving a signal from Peter and Paul Fortress and, if necessary, to shell [the Winter Palace] with shrapnel." (Commissar of the *Aurora*, report of 7 November 1917, published 1923. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/bolsheviks-seize-power/bolsheviks-seize-power-texts/action-of-the-cruiser-aurora/)) He says there was never occasion for live fire because the palace surrendered. That is an officer's account of his own orders, published when the October story was already being shaped for commemoration, but it corrects the later legend of a naval bombardment.

The foreign correspondents moved between Smolny, the palace and the streets. John Reed, an American journalist sympathetic to the Bolsheviks, recorded the leaflet his side circulated: "The Provisional Government is deposed. The State Power has passed into the hands of the organ of the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies, the Military Revolutionary Committee" (leaflet quoted by John Reed, 7 November 1917, in *Ten Days That Shook the World*, 1919. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/reed/1919/10days/10days/ch4.htm)). Reed was often not inside the palace and relied on what participants told him; his vivid dialogue is reported speech, not a transcript. Louise Bryant, who travelled with him, went in before the palace fell, talked to cadets who expected to die, and watched the ministers leave under guard. "Every one leaving the palace was searched, no matter on what side he was." (Louise Bryant, *Six Red Months in Russia*, 1918, ch. VIII. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bryant/works/russia/ch08.htm)) For all their differences of vantage point, the witnesses agree that the fighting was small. N. N. Sukhanov, a Menshevik who wrote in 1922 and had no love for the Bolsheviks, called the night almost bloodless. "The decisive operations that had begun were quite bloodless; not one casualty was recorded. The city was absolutely calm." (N. N. Sukhanov, memoir written 1922, English abridgement by Joel Carmichael. [Source](https://alphahistory.com/russianrevolution/sukhanov-october-revolution-1922/)) Maslov's grenade and Reed's dead cadet sit awkwardly beside that sentence; the differences may reflect different hours and streets, but no witness here supplies a reliable total.

Kerensky, the prime minister, saw the collapse from outside. He appealed to the Council of the Republic, found the Cossack regiments neutral, and drove toward Gatchina in a car, while an American flag flew on the escort car to seek troops that did not exist. "It was 11 P.M. How could we in Pskoff know that at that very moment the Winter Palace, where the Provisional Government was sitting, was withstanding the final bombardment and the last attacks of the Bolsheviki?" (A. F. Kerensky, *The Catastrophe*, 1927, ch. XVIII. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/kerensky/1927/catastrophe/ch18.htm)) This is an exile defending his record nine years later, and Trotsky disputes his version of the car. Yet opponent and participants describe the same thing: a government that could not find anyone to defend it.

## Peace and land

While the palace was still held, the Second All-Russia Congress of Soviets met at Smolny. The old Central Executive Committee's mandate was declared ended, the Congress took power, and most ministers were said to be already under arrest. "The Provisional Government has been overthrown. The majority of the members of the Provisional Government have already been arrested." (Second All-Russia Congress of Soviets, "To Workers, Soldiers, and Peasants!", 25 October 1917. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1917/oct/25-26/25b.htm)) It was a claim of authority issued before the building had fallen, not a situation report. Menshevik and Socialist-Revolutionary delegates walked out, an act Reed watched from the floor. The new government's programme was delivered the next day as two decrees. The Decree on Peace "calls upon all the belligerent peoples and their government to start immediate negotiations for a just, democratic peace." (Decree on Peace, 26 October 1917. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1917/oct/25-26/26b.htm)) It proposed an immediate armistice, peace without annexations or indemnities, and publication of the secret treaties. The Decree on Land went further. "Landed proprietorship is abolished forthwith without any compensation." (Decree on Land, 26 October 1917. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1917/oct/25-26/26d.htm)) Estates passed to local land committees pending the Constituent Assembly, and the decree incorporated a peasant mandate compiled from 242 local mandates. Lenin said in debate that he would accept the peasants' decision even if the Socialist-Revolutionaries won the Assembly. That was a political position, not a prediction. Both decrees were statements of policy, not evidence that the war ended or the land was transferred.

## The Constituent Assembly

The Assembly that the land decree deferred to met in January 1918. Yakov Sverdlov read the Declaration of the Rights of the Toiling and Exploited People; the Bolsheviks and Left Socialist-Revolutionaries withdrew, and Kronstadt sailors ended the sitting. The argument then moved to the Central Executive Committee on 19 January. Press reports from three party newspapers record an opposition that came from inside the revolutionary camp, not from the defeated ministers. Riazanov and Lozovskii voted against the Bolshevik resolution, and Sukhanov challenged the claim that the Assembly had refused to recognise Soviet power. "The decree to dissolve the Constituent Assembly is put to a vote and is accepted ... Lozovskii and Riazanov voted against" (press reports of the Central Executive Committee debate, 19 January 1918. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/constituent-assembly/constituent-assembly-texts/newspapers-on-the-constituent-assembly/)). The decree that followed justified the dissolution on the grounds that the Assembly had been elected on pre-October lists and had refused to consider the Soviet declaration. "In view of the above the Central Executive Committee hereby decrees: The Constituent Assembly is dissolved." (Central Executive Committee decree, 19 January 1918. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/constituent-assembly/constituent-assembly-texts/dissolution-of-the-constituent-assembly/)) Sukhanov's point in the press was that the question had never been put. A justification issued by the body that carried out the act is not a neutral account of it. Power had been seized in November and was being consolidated in January, and the record of both operations is mostly the record of what the participants said afterwards.

## Limits of the record

No Russian-language original has been opened for this account. Maslov's article, the *Aurora* report, the newspaper reports and the dissolution decree all reach English through university teaching projects that name earlier printed sources but usually not their translators. The Lenin texts name theirs; the Sukhanov excerpt names none. These are not direct readings of the 1917 newspapers. The MRC's own minutes and the verbatim proceedings of the Second Congress were not located in accessible form, so what is called the Smolny record here is eyewitness description rather than institutional minutes. The testimony is uneven and politically shaped. Ministers, party leaders, diplomats and foreign correspondents are well represented; rank-and-file soldiers, women workers and peasants appear almost only as people speaking to journalists. Sukhanov wrote in 1922 and was later abridged, Trotsky wrote in 1930 after his defeat in the leadership struggle, and Kerensky wrote in 1927 to defend his record.

Conflicts in the material resist settlement. Sukhanov records no casualty, Maslov a grenade and wounded cadets, Reed one dead cadet. Reed says looting began in the palace and was stopped by Red Guards shouting that the property belonged to the people; Bryant, inside the palace, says she saw none. Kerensky says Allied envoys lent the car with an American flag as a gesture of solidarity, while Trotsky cites Ambassador David Francis to the effect that a Russian officer commandeered it and the embassy then pretended it had been seized. Francis's own book has not been located here. The dissolution decree says the Assembly refused to consider the Soviet declaration; Sukhanov, reported in the press, says the question was never put. A caution from the period shows why even printed testimony needs care. The Marxists Internet Archive transcription of a 1918 Stalin article notes that a paragraph crediting Trotsky was omitted from later collected editions, a reminder that participant accounts can be edited for political reasons ([note](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/stalin/works/1918/11/06.htm)). Casualties in the palace and the streets cannot be established from this material. Nothing here establishes how many people in Petrograd supported the seizure, how the land decree was received in the villages, or what most soldiers thought about the dissolution. The sources give us decisions, public claims and the experience of a small, articulate minority. They do not give us a plebiscite.

## How historians have read it

- **Alexander Rabinowitch, *The Bolsheviks Come to Power: The Revolution of 1917 in Petrograd* (1976; new edition 2017).** A detailed study of party and soviet records arguing that the Bolsheviks won because they responded to rank-and-file radicalisation, not simply by conspiracy; it pushes back against the "coup" reading. [Catalog record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL4078047W/The_Bolsheviks_come_to_power)
- **Orlando Figes, *A People's Tragedy: A History of the Russian Revolution* (1996).** A narrative synthesis that accepts mass participation but treats October as a seizure enabling Bolshevik dictatorship; a detailed [H-Russia review](https://www.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=1735) disputes his "omniscient Lenin" and his handling of the 10 October resolution, so his interpretation is contested even among admirers. [Catalog record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL72141W/A_people's_tragedy)
- **Richard Pipes, *The Russian Revolution* (1990).** A top-down account whose chapter "The October coup" stresses the conspiratorial and intelligentsia-led character of the seizure; it stands at the opposite pole from Rabinowitch on the weight of popular agency. [Catalog record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL1746635W/The_Russian_Revolution)
- **Rex A. Wade, *The Russian Revolution, 1917* (2000; later editions).** A synthesis organised around the interaction of war, domestic crisis and Bolshevik organisation; Wade also edited *Revolutionary Russia: New Approaches to the Russian Revolution of 1917* (2004), a collection useful for mapping interpretive disagreement rather than settling it. [Catalog record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL505956W/The_Russian_Revolution_1917)
- **Sheila Fitzpatrick, *The Russian Revolution: 1917–1932* (1982; later editions).** A social-history approach, less absorbed by the legality of the seizure and more by who gained power and status; it complements rather than directly refutes the political narratives above. [Catalog record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL1863447W/The_Russian_Revolution_\(Opus_Books\))

Broad agreement: the Provisional Government was disabled by the war and by its own lack of reliable force; the Bolsheviks held majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow Soviets by autumn; the insurrection was timed around the Second Congress; the peace and land decrees were issued; the Constituent Assembly was dissolved in January 1918. Live disagreement: whether October was a popular revolution or a minority coup, how much Lenin personally determined events, whether a broader socialist coalition was viable, and whether the dissolution of the Assembly was defensible. Those positions are not equally evidenced: the "coup" and "mass uprising" readings each draw on genuine contemporary testimony, while claims about Lenin's private intentions rest on inference.
