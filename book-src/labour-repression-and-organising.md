On the first of May 1926, in the pit village of Pendleton, a miner's wife told a reporter from the *Manchester Evening News* what a stoppage meant in her kitchen: "it is the women who have to find and put the dumplings in the stew." The sentence came from a newspaper that supported the government and survived because a local history society copied it out decades later ([reproduced by the North West Labour History Society](https://www.nwlh.org.uk/General%20Strike%201926)). What followed was the only general strike Britain has ever known. A general strike is a coordinated stoppage across many industries, so that one trade cannot be isolated. It ran from 3 to 12 May 1926 and drew roughly three million workers out in support of miners locked out, meaning employers closed pits and withheld pay until the men accepted terms. A blacklist denies work to named people, a strikebreaker replaces strikers, and a shop steward is elected by colleagues. This chapter follows both the machinery used against organisers and the reforms organisers won.

## The instruments of suppression

Chartism met the law at the Westgate Hotel in Newport in the early hours of 4 November 1839. John Frost led a march from the mining valleys; the windows were smashed, the Riot Act was read, and soldiers fired. Henry Mullock, recalling the scene in 1897, remembered the crowd's movement rather than its politics: "We watched until they turned the corner of the Westgate and began smashing the windows" ([*South Wales Argus*, 1897](https://www.southwalesargus.co.uk/news/18010469.newport-uprising-words-people-saw-happen)). More than three thousand court documents survive from the treason trials. Who fired first is unclear, and witnesses testified after the event under a prosecution seeking convictions. Frost and two others were transported for life. Parliament was unmoved by the petitioning: in May 1842 MPs refused to hear petitioners at the bar and defeated the motion on the second Chartist petition by 49 votes to 287 ([Hansard, 3 May 1842](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1842/may/03/the-national-petition-the-charter)). In 1848 O'Connor claimed more than five million signatures; Commons clerks counted 1,975,496 ([Chartist Ancestors](https://www.chartistancestors.co.uk/full-text-of-the-petition-for-the-charter-1848/)).

At Everett, Washington, on 5 November 1916, men landed from the steamer *Verona* and a dock confrontation left five Wobblies confirmed dead and two deputies killed by friendly fire. The first shot is unknown, and the IWW, local, AFL and socialist papers each framed the event differently ([Walker C. Smith, *The Everett Massacre*](https://archive.org/details/everettmassacreh00smituoft); [University of Washington guide](https://depts.washington.edu/iww/everett_story.shtml)). Palmer set out his case in *The Forum* in 1920: "Upon these two basic certainties, first that the 'Reds' were criminal aliens and secondly that the American Government must prevent crime, it was decided that there could be no nice distinctions drawn." He cited "upward of 60,000" alleged agitators on confidential authority ([Palmer, February 1920](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/the-case-against-the-reds/)). Berkman and Goldman, deported in December 1919, wrote from Ellis Island of the "Red Ark" leaving families destitute ([Berkman and Goldman, 1919](https://archive.org/details/deportationitsme00berkuoft)). In a petition of 3 May 1927 Bartolomeo Vanzetti connected his case to the raids: "we were really tried not for murder, but for being Radicals, draft evaders, and pacifists" ([Vanzetti petition](http://dwardmac.pitzer.edu/Anarchist_Archives/bright/SaccoVan/defense.html)). The evidence remains disputed; the petition is a defence argument, not a finding.

The state intervened administratively as well as through police. The government took over the *Morning Post* and published the *British Gazette* under Winston Churchill. Its first issue said that "nearly all the newspapers have been silenced by violent concerted action." The TUC closed the whole press, including its own *Daily Herald*, then issued the *British Worker* after police raided the Herald's offices ([Modern Records Centre](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/library/mrc/blog/bulletins/)). Ellen Wilkinson complained that a publicly subscribed service gave only one side ([*Radio Times*, 28 May 1926](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/library/mrc/blog/bulletins/)). Local Communist premises were raided and people imprisoned for producing bulletins. After the General Council ended the strike without guarantees, miners stayed out for seven months and victimisation followed. William Gallacher recalled police chasing him in taxis while he spoke in County Durham ([Gallacher, 1936](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.220268/2015.220268.Revolt-On_djvu.txt)); Wal Hannington heard the call-off from inside a prison sentence ([Hannington, 1936](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.89976/2015.89976.Unemployed-Struggles-1919-1936_djvu.txt)). Both are retrospective accounts by men with political cases.

On the Clyde the Munitions Act and dilution brought stewards into conflict with officials and the state; in March 1916 the government deported Kirkwood and three other Beardmores stewards, and the union refused them benefit ([Clyde Workers' Committee records](https://www.marxists.org/archive/gallacher/1915/clyde-committee.htm)). In Brazil in 1980 a court declared the ABC strike illegal, the government removed forty-two union directors, and police arrested Lula and twelve others without warrant ([Fundação Perseu Abramo](https://fpabramo.org.br/a-greve-de-1980/)). In South Africa a state fired on a crowd at Sharpeville in 1960; the official figure was 69 dead, while historians using sworn testimony argue for at least 91 ([Sharpeville accounts](http://sahistory.org.za/article/eyewitness-accounts-sharpeville-massacre-1960); [Clark and Worger, 2023](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9781003257806-6/massacre-nancy-clark-william-worger)). These were not one tactic applied everywhere.

Haymarket Books, founded in 2001, states on its own about page that the Haymarket Martyrs' "1886 struggle for the eight-hour day, which gave us May Day, the international workers' holiday," reminds workers that ordinary people can organise ([about page](https://www.haymarketbooks.org/pg/about)). That is the publisher's account of its own name; no contemporary report is held here, so no details of a bomb, trial or executions can be asserted. Ludlow has no source in the current book.

## What organisers built and won

The record contains durable gains. Five of the Charter's six points became law; equal electoral districts were achieved in 1885, and the exception was annual parliaments ([Chartist chapter](/chartists)). The Charter excluded women because its authors feared women's suffrage would "retard the measure"; a modern register locates around ninety-three female Chartist groups between 1838 and 1851 ([*Northern Star*, 2 February 1839](https://spartacus-educational.com/ExamIR14.htm); [female Chartist organisations](https://www.chartistancestors.co.uk/female-chartist-organisations-1838-1851)). Gains were real, partial and slow.

In Lawrence the strike committee secured increases of 5 to 20 per cent for roughly 30,000 workers after the hours cut ([Neill report, 1912](https://archive.org/details/cu31924054583020)). On the Clyde the Forty Hours strike of January 1919 ended with a 47-hour week ([Gallacher, 1916](https://www.marxists.org/archive/gallacher/1916/call-workers.htm)). The IWW preamble declared that "the working class and the employing class have nothing in common," and Haywood called industrial unionism "socialism with its working clothes on" in 1912 ([IWW songbook](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Songs_of_the_Workers_%289th_edition%29); [Cooper Union debate](https://archive.org/details/120111-hillquithaywood-nycdebate)). Hillquit conceded its abstract superiority but feared a rival union would split socialists.

International labour organisation produced institutions. The Charter of Amiens of 1906 committed the French CGT to the general strike and to the union as "today a resistance group" and "in the future, a group for production and redistribution" ([Charter of Amiens](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/cgt/charter-amiens.htm)). In February 1945, 204 delegates representing 60 million workers met in London and declared that "organized labour, with so great a part in winning the war, cannot leave to others" the making of the peace ([WFTU official history](https://www.wftucentral.org/history/)). Its constitution aimed at combating "war and the causes of war"; it passed a colonial self-determination resolution and split in 1949 over the Marshall Plan. Its own history blames "imperialist and reactionary forces," a defence by one party. Civil liberties campaigns were part of the same long struggle: Du Bois was prosecuted in 1951 over the Stockholm Appeal and had his passport withheld, while Robeson's passport was revoked in 1950 and restored only in 1958 ([Du Bois and Robeson](/dubois-and-robeson)).

## Competing readings

The 1926 call-off is disputed on shared facts. TUC leaders called it a legal and practical necessity; Communist writers said they feared the strike's consequences. Schneer's archival synthesis is reported as holding that TUC leaders "feared winning even more than they feared losing"; Perkins read British moderation, Davidson replied that it was more militant ([Schneer interview](https://www.unseenhistories.com/the-general-strike-of-1926-with-jonathan-schneer/); [Perkins reviewed](https://socialistworker.co.uk/in-depth/the-1926-general-strike-nine-days-of-hope/)). Harman, Davidson and CPGB pamphlets share a framework and are not independent corroboration. The sources establish the call-off and victimisation, not the motive.

## Limits of the record

No contemporary Haymarket or Ludlow record has been opened. No blacklist is reproduced, no strikebreaker or soldier gives a first-hand account of 1926, and the rival bulletins survive mainly in quotations and recollections. The CPGB material is polemical. Most testimony comes from leaders and organisers, from Cook and Bevin to Haywood and Palmer. The number of strikers in 1926 is best held as a range.

Research priorities are the original bulletins and bound newspaper sets, the Haymarket and Ludlow records, blacklist and court files, and oral histories from people who held no office in a union or party. What this chapter can establish is that repression and reform were simultaneous: the decades that produced transportation, deportation, raids and victimisation also produced the 47-hour week, pay increases, five of six Chartist points, and labour bodies reaching millions of workers. Neither side of that record cancels the other.
