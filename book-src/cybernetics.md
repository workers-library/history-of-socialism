Norbert Wiener coined the word cybernetics for a study of control and communication in machines and living things (Norbert Wiener, *Cybernetics: or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*, MIT Press, 1948). Feedback, the idea at its centre, means feeding information about the result of an action back into the system so that it can adjust. Wiener had worked on anti-aircraft fire control during the war, and in 1943 he and two colleagues proposed that the human nervous system and a machine could be discussed in one vocabulary. He wrote:

> "It is these boundary regions of science which offer the richest opportunities to the qualified investigator."
>
> Source: [Norbert Wiener, *Cybernetics*, 1948 (read in the 1961 second edition)](https://archive.org/download/wiener-1938/Cybernetics%20or%20Communication%20and%20Control%20in%20the%20Animal%20and%20the%20Machine%20-%20Norbert%20Wiene_OCR_djvu.txt)

That proposal was a research tool, not a finished doctrine. In the Soviet Union it became a philosophical argument conducted in Marxist terms. A 1960 exchange, translated the following year by the U.S. Joint Publications Research Service, set two philosophers against each other. Borshchev, Rokhlin and Il'in held that a machine might in principle develop a quality analogous to consciousness without contradicting dialectical materialism. M. N. Andryushchenko replied that thinking belonged to highly organised living matter and that a model of a reflex was not a reflex. Her objection turned on a toy that could be taught to avoid an obstacle: the machine followed a purpose given from outside.

> "Man predetermined the character of the reaction."
>
> Source: [M. N. Andryushchenko, "Reply to Comrades Borshchev, Il'in and Rokhlin", 1960, translated by the U.S. Joint Publications Research Service](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA357950/DTIC_ADA357950_djvu.txt)

Both writers treated cybernetics as a live question. Neither simply repeated a ban.

## The science that could not be wrong

Biology in the same state took a different path. Mendelian genetics is the theory of heredity based on Gregor Mendel's laws, in which traits pass between generations through units we now call genes. Trofim Lysenko, president of the Lenin All-Union Academy of Agricultural Sciences, rejected it. At a session of the academy in July and August 1948 he told his audience that heredity was a property of the whole organism and its conditions of life, that acquired characters were inherited, and that chromosome theory was idealist. The transcript records applause and ovations. At the close of the session on 5 August he said:

> "the Central Committee of the Party has examined my report and approved it."
>
> Source: [T. D. Lysenko, concluding remarks, 5 August 1948, translated](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1947/lysenkoism-in-biology/lysenkoism-in-biology-texts/lysenko-on-the-situation-in-biological-science)

> "We do not recognize the chromosome theory of heredity; we do not recognize Mendelism-Morganism."
>
> Source: [T. D. Lysenko, concluding remarks, 5 August 1948, translated](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1947/lysenkoism-in-biology/lysenkoism-in-biology-texts/lysenko-on-the-situation-in-biological-science)

In his full report of 31 July he named the practical objection he had against the geneticists, that they taught a theory students could learn while offering nothing to the fields:

> "Morganism-Mendelism (the chromosome theory of heredity) is to this day taught, in a number of versions, in all colleges of biology and agronomy, whereas the study of Michurin genetics has in fact not been introduced at all."
>
> Source: [T. D. Lysenko, report, 31 July 1948, translated](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/lysenko/works/1940s/report.htm)

The session endorsed the report and geneticists were dismissed. Vavilov had been arrested in 1940 and died in prison in 1943. Zhores Medvedev, a biologist who was an onlooker in the earlier phase and a participant in the struggle against Lysenkoism in the 1960s, described his own changing role:

> "in the first as historian; in the second as an onlooker; and in the third as participant"
>
> Source: [Zhores Medvedev, preface dated January 1967, translated by I. Michael Lerner](https://archive.org/download/rise-and-fall-of-td-lysenko/The%20rise%20and%20fall%20of%20T.D.%20Lysenko_djvu.txt)

He reports that a petition signed by more than three hundred scientists asked for Lysenko's removal from the academy presidency by the end of 1955, and that it succeeded:

> "By the end of 1955 more than three hundred scientists had signed a petition requesting Lysenko's removal from the post of LAAAS president"
>
> Source: [Zhores Medvedev, *The Rise and Fall of T. D. Lysenko*, 1969, translated by I. Michael Lerner](https://archive.org/download/rise-and-fall-of-td-lysenko/The%20rise%20and%20fall%20of%20T.D.%20Lysenko_djvu.txt)

The two kinds of testimony, the performance before an approved audience and the participant's retrospective, show both the machinery of orthodoxy and organised resistance inside Soviet science. The petition itself was not opened.

## The satellite and the story

The state that backed Lysenko also ran a space programme. In 1954 Mikhail Tikhonravov, head of a department at a scientific research institute, proposed a simple satellite and sent the report to government with a covering letter from Sergei Korolev. He calculated orbits, tracking and recovery, and stated why a first launch mattered:

> "the launch of the first satellite in our country would have tremendous political significance."
>
> Source: [Mikhail Tikhonravov, report of early 1954, translated by Asif Siddiqi](https://history.nasa.gov/sputnik/russ1.html)

The satellite that flew was far smaller than the two to three tonnes he proposed, but the report places Sputnik inside a deliberate programme rather than a sudden response. TASS announced the launch on 5 October 1957 in *Pravda*, giving the orbital data and inviting radio amateurs to listen:

> "The power of the transmitters ensures reliable reception of the signals by a broad range of radio amateurs."
>
> Source: [TASS announcement, *Pravda*, 5 October 1957, translated](https://history.nasa.gov/sputnik/14.html)

A collective farm is a farm farmed jointly by its members. Yuri Gagarin, the first person in space, presented his origins in those terms in a memoir published in 1961:

> "The family in which I was born is the most ordinary one, it is no different from the millions of working families of our socialist Motherland."
>
> Source: [Yuri Gagarin, *The Road to Space*, 1961, translated by "Stanislav" in 2026](https://stanislavs.org/the-road-to-space-a-fragment-of-book-by-yuri-gagarin/)

The chapter available in English covers his childhood, the German occupation of his village and his schooling, stopping before the flight. It turns the cosmonaut into a product of Soviet institutions, written to the heroic conventions of state publishing. The Russian original was not read for this account.

## A nervous system for Chile

In Allende's Chile the question of control was tried in practice. Stafford Beer, a British management cybernetician who was scientific director of the project from 1971 to 1973, described it three years after the coup. His team built Cybernet, a Telex network linking enterprises to Santiago, and Cyberstride, a forecasting program. A Telex is a teleprinter network that sends typed messages over wires. An operations room had projectors and seven chairs, and an algedonic channel carried urgent warnings upward, the name coming from the Greek words for pain and pleasure:

> "The project aimed to acquire the benefits of cybernetic synergy for the whole of industry, while devolving power to the workers at the same time."
>
> Source: [Stafford Beer, Zaheer Lecture, 5 December 1974](https://archive.org/download/agt-beer-73a580/beer_djvu.txt)

He described the aim in bodily terms:

> "We used it in Chile to provide the economy with a nervous system, nerves to activate the sinews of the government."
>
> Source: [Stafford Beer, Zaheer Lecture, 5 December 1974](https://archive.org/download/agt-beer-73a580/beer_djvu.txt)

Beer was also explicit about the limits. The system covered about seventy-five per cent of nationalised industry, the large animated economic model never worked, and the economy was never commanded from the operations room. Reports were meant to stay confidential to each enterprise so that monitoring would not turn into central direction. Whether that devolution was real is disputed by critics whom Beer names but does not quote. His remembered account of Allende smiling and saying that the people had arrived is a recalled conversation, not a recording. Workers and Chilean engineers appear in this record only through his descriptions.

## How historians have read it

- **Slava Gerovitch, *From Newspeak to Cyberspeak: A History of Soviet Cybernetics* (MIT Press, 2002).** Argues that Soviet cybernetics was a language and a social movement that first challenged Stalinist ideological discourse and was then absorbed by it; the standard account of the Soviet side. [DOI: 3137.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/3137.001.0001)
- **Benjamin Peters, *How Not to Network a Nation: The Uneasy History of the Soviet Internet* (MIT Press, 2016).** Studies OGAS and the failed Soviet computer network, stressing institutional and bureaucratic obstacles rather than technical insufficiency; broadly agrees with Gerovitch on co-optation but gives more weight to organisational politics. [DOI: 001.0001](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9780262034180.001.0001)
- **Eden Medina, *Cybernetic Revolutionaries: Technology and Politics in Allende's Chile* (MIT Press, 2011); and "Designing Freedom, Regulating a Nation", *Journal of Latin American Studies* 38(3), 2006.** Treats Cybersyn as a jointly built Chilean–British project shaped by local politics, correcting accounts that make Beer the sole author. [DOI: 8417.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/8417.001.0001) and [DOI: S0022216X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022216X06001179)
- **David Joravsky, *The Lysenko Affair* (Harvard University Press, 1970).** Recasts the genetics controversy as rivalry between scientific groups and their patrons in different state apparatuses, rather than a simple story of ideology crushing science; this is a revision of the older totalitarian account. [Internet Archive: the lysenko affair](https://archive.org/details/the-lysenko-affair)
- **Zhores Medvedev, *The Rise and Fall of T. D. Lysenko* (Columbia University Press, 1969).** A participant's documentary history, stronger on names, dates and the 1960s endgame than on detached judgement; agrees with Joravsky on institutional damage but writes as an advocate. [Internet Archive: The rise and fall of T.D. Lysenko djvu](https://archive.org/download/rise-and-fall-of-td-lysenko/The%20rise%20and%20fall%20of%20T.D.%20Lysenko_djvu.txt)
- **Nikolai Krementsov, *Stalinist Science* (Princeton University Press, 1996).** Argues that the Soviet party apparatus and the scientific community became culturally fused through shared rituals and rhetoric; supports Gerovitch's language-centred approach and complicates any simple state-versus-science model. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400822140)

Broad agreement: these historians treat science under socialism as politically embedded and reject both the pure-hero and pure-victim narratives. Live disagreement: how much of Lysenkoism is explained by ideology and how much by institutional competition, and whether cybernetics was mainly a discourse of reform or a tool of administrative power. These are disagreements about evidence and interpretation, not equal claims.

## Limits of the record

The richest gap is OGAS and the Kibernetika institute. No primary text by Viktor Glushkov, no founding document of the Ukrainian Institute of Cybernetics and no issue of the journal *Kibernetika* was opened. The early press attacks on cybernetics, the 1954 dictionary entry and the 1955 philosophical papers are known from secondary literature but were not opened in the original or in translation. The party programme that listed cybernetics among the sciences needed for communism was not opened either. The decisive records are in Russian, and for the Ukrainian institute possibly in Ukrainian; Kamanin's diaries exist in English only as a historian's summary. Vavilov's own writings and the 1955 petition were not opened. The surviving testimony is uneven. Beer speaks for Cybersyn, Lysenko's speeches are performances before an approved audience, Gagarin's memoir was published by the state, and the 1948 speech survives in at least two competing English versions. The scanned texts contain machine-generated errors, and none of these sources measures the economic effect of Cybersyn or the agricultural effect of Lysenkoism. They record claims and intentions. The question of whether a system can inform a plan without becoming the plan is left open by the evidence assembled here.
