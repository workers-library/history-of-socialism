On New Year's Day in 1816 Robert Owen stood before the people of New Lanark and opened a school. The mill village in the Clyde valley was his to manage, and the address he gave that morning was printed and preserved, with a manuscript copy later entering the royal collection. "We have met to-day for the purpose of opening this Institution; and it is my intention to explain to you the objects for which it has been founded," he said ([Robert Owen, address at New Lanark, 1 January 1816, as reprinted in the 1927 Everyman edition](https://archive.org/download/newviewofsociety00owen/newviewofsociety00owen_djvu.txt)). The Institution for the Formation of Character, as he called it, would take children almost as soon as they could walk. He fixed ten as the age for entering the mill, "before which age, none of them will be permitted to enter the works" ([same address](https://archive.org/download/newviewofsociety00owen/newviewofsociety00owen_djvu.txt)). No reply from the audience survives. What remains is the employer's voice, setting out a claim about how people become who they are.

## A doctrine put to the test

Three years earlier Owen had published the first of his essays under a title that promised a new way of seeing society. "Any general character, from the best to the worst, from the most ignorant to the most enlightened, may be given to any community, even to the world at large, by the application of proper means," he wrote ([Robert Owen, *A New View of Society*, First Essay, written 1812, published 1813](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/owen/society/ch01.htm)). The sentence is a claim about cause: character is made by surroundings, and surroundings can be remade. Owen dedicated the essay to William Wilberforce and addressed later essays to the superintendents of manufactories; workers were not his first readers. He said the doctrine had been tried for twenty years, including among two or three thousand people at New Lanark, and gave figures for his returns. Those figures were his own, and the essays state a programme rather than what workers experienced.

The surroundings he wanted to change were those of the factory. In a tract of 1817 dedicated to the British Legislature he described what he said he had seen: "In the manufacturing districts it is common for parents to send their children of both sexes at seven or eight years of age, in winter as well as summer, at six o'clock in the morning, sometimes of course in the dark" ([Robert Owen, *Observations on the Effect of the Manufacturing System*, 1817](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/owen/observations.htm)). He asked for a twelve-hour day, no work under ten, six-hour days for children under twelve, and basic literacy. A twelve-hour day was a reform then, not a rejection of factory work.

Why were children still starting at nine? Reading the amended bill in committee in 1818, Owen wrote to the Prime Minister, the Earl of Liverpool: "It permits children to be employed at nine years of age for twelve hours and a half per day" ([Robert Owen, letter to the Earl of Liverpool, 1818, as reprinted in 1927](https://archive.org/download/newviewofsociety00owen/newviewofsociety00owen_djvu.txt)). He named the cotton spinners, several of them members of Parliament, who resisted the bill. Nearly forty years later, writing his autobiography in his eighty-sixth year, he recalled a Poor Law Committee that debated for two days behind closed doors before deciding not to hear him: "this committee debated for two whole days, with closed doors, whether or not I should be examined by them" ([Robert Owen, *The Life of Robert Owen*, 1857, recalling 1817](https://archive.org/download/lifeofrobertowen00owenuoft/lifeofrobertowen00owenuoft_djvu.txt)). The 1819 Factory Act fell short of what he wanted, and his memory of the committee cannot be checked against its minutes.

## The school and the county

Owen's eldest son ran the New Lanark schools and described them in 1824. Robert Dale Owen counted about 600 pupils, classes of twenty to forty and infant classes of two to five. He described dancing and singing masters, no artificial rewards or punishments, and the silent monitor, a coloured block that recorded conduct without a rebuke. "The children are governed, not by severity, but by kindness; and excited, not by distinctions, but by creating in them a wish to learn what they are to be taught," he wrote ([Robert Dale Owen, *An Outline of the System of Education at New Lanark*, 1824](https://archive.org/download/anoutlinesystem00owengoog/anoutlinesystem00owengoog_djvu.txt)). Dedicated to his father, the pamphlet is most valuable for its admissions. He called the experiment partial and imperfect, said parents went on using rewards and punishments at home, and recorded that most children entered the factory at ten. The school could not undo what the mill and the household did outside its walls.

In 1821 Owen carried the argument beyond the village. His *Report to the County of Lanark* told a county meeting that unemployment after the wars came from productive power without arrangements to distribute what it produced. Labour, he proposed, should become the natural standard of value. He set out villages of about a thousand people, common kitchens and schools, spade cultivation, and exchange at prime cost. The county's minutes survive with the report, and A. J. Hamilton of Dalzell offered between 500 and 700 acres. A named audience had answered with an offer of land. This was a plan, not an account of a community already built, and the arithmetic was Owen's own estimate. The scan is too corrupt in places to quote safely.

## Harmony, and a name for the movement

In the winter of 1824 Owen took his twenty-two-year-old son William to Indiana to inspect a settlement built by George Rapp's followers and to negotiate its purchase. William kept a diary of the journey and of a visit to the Shakers at Watervliet, where people exchanged labour without using money. "Even this sample made us all in love with a community," the young man wrote ([William Owen, diary, visit to the Shakers at Watervliet, November 1824](https://archive.org/download/diary-of-william-owen/Diary%20of%20William%20Owen_djvu.txt)). George Rapp asked whether the proposed community would forbid marriage, and Owen explained his doctrine of character to him; the Rappites' fenced fields were noted in passing. Published eighty years later from a family manuscript, the diary has an editor's preface claiming some 20,000 visitors came to New Lanark between 1815 and 1825. That figure is the editor's, not the diarist's.

The purchase of Harmony became New Harmony, and by November 1827 the co-operative press in London treated its failure as known. A correspondent said readers already knew of the want of success at Orbiston and New Harmony, and blamed selfishness. In the same issue an unsigned editorial note set out the division inside the movement: "between the modern, (or Mill and Malthus) Political Economists, and the Communionists or Socialists, is, whether it is more beneficial that this capital should be individual or in common?" ([unsigned editorial note, *Co-operative Magazine*, November 1827, p. 509](https://archive.org/download/sim_london-co-operative-magazine_1827-11_2_11/sim_london-co-operative-magazine_1827-11_2_11_djvu.txt)). A name for a new politics appeared in an argument over capital. A private catalogue record calls this the first documented use of "socialist" in the *Oxford English Dictionary*, but the claim does not come from the dictionary entry itself.

## Labour notes and authority

In 1833 the Exchange Bazaar in Gray's Inn Road opened as an Equitable Labour Exchange, where goods were valued in hours and paid for with labour notes, paper credits denominated in time rather than coin. William Lovett, a cabinet-maker and co-operator who later became a Chartist, attended its congress of sixty or seventy societies. He remembered free premises, an infant school and six days of talk with very little business. "The Exchange Bazaar was ultimately opened by Mr. Owen and his council, and for a time promised success, until in fact 'the labour notes' began to be depreciated," he wrote ([William Lovett, autobiography written 1840–74, published 1876](https://archive.org/download/cu31924028036758/cu31924028036758_djvu.txt)). Depreciation meant that the hours promised on a note were no longer trusted at the counter.

The quarrel was over who decided. Lovett's deputation objected to a circular Owen had amended against the committee's vote. Owen answered that "we must consent to be ruled by despots till we had acquired sufficient knowledge to govern ourselves" ([Robert Owen, as quoted by William Lovett, 1876, from an encounter at the Exchange Bazaar in 1833](https://archive.org/download/cu31924028036758/cu31924028036758_djvu.txt)). The words come from a man who had broken with Owen and wrote forty years later: Owen's words as Lovett recalled them, not a transcript. The exchange did not last.

Owen's later system made the claim larger. In 1836 he addressed a book to William IV and to all nations: "Man is a compound being, whose character is formed of his constitution or organization at birth, and of the effects of external circumstances acting upon that organization" ([Robert Owen, *The Book of the New Moral World*, Part I, 1836](https://archive.org/download/bookofnewmoralwo00owen/bookofnewmoralwo00owen_djvu.txt)). The five Fundamental Facts and twenty laws that followed left little room for individual choice. G. D. H. Cole judged that Owen's real work had ended in 1834 and that the later books repeated the earlier ones. The record supports a narrower conclusion. Owen showed that a mill could be run without the worst abuses and a school built inside a factory village. He did not show that the people of such a village would accept a system in which someone else decided what was good for them.

## How historians have read it

- J. F. C. Harrison, *Quest for the New Moral World: Robert Owen and the Owenites in Britain and America* (1969). The standard broad study, treating Owenism as a movement in two countries rather than as one biography. [Internet Archive: questfornewmoral00harr](https://archive.org/metadata/questfornewmoral00harr)
- G. D. H. Cole, introduction to *A New View of Society and Other Writings* (1927). Argues that Owen said what mattered in the essays of 1813–21, that his real work ended in 1834, and that he was a bad leader of men despite showing that low wages and long hours were not necessary to industrial success. [Internet Archive: newviewofsociety00owen djvu](https://archive.org/download/newviewofsociety00owen/newviewofsociety00owen_djvu.txt)
- Margaret Cole, *Robert Owen of New Lanark* (1953). A compact biography centred on the New Lanark years and the move into public campaigning. [Internet Archive: robertowenofnewl0000marg](https://archive.org/metadata/robertowenofnewl0000marg)
- Frank Podmore, *Robert Owen: A Biography*, 2 vols. (1906). The early full-length scholarly life, with illustrations and facsimiles, written before the social-history scholarship of the later twentieth century. [Internet Archive: robertowenabiog02podmgoog](https://archive.org/metadata/robertowenabiog02podmgoog)
- A. L. Morton, *The Life and Ideas of Robert Owen* (1969). A Marxist reading of Owen's thought and practice, useful for the weight it gives to his relation with the working-class movement. [Marxists Internet Archive: index](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/owen/index.htm)
- George Jacob Holyoake, *The Life and Last Days of Robert Owen, of New Lanark* (1871). Written by a secularist who knew the later Owen; strongest on the final years and on how followers presented his legacy. [Marxists Internet Archive: index](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/owen/index.htm)

There is broad agreement that New Lanark was commercially successful and educationally innovative, that the 1819 Act fell short of Owen's demands, and that New Harmony failed. Cole's periodisation and the explanations of that failure remain matters of interpretation.

## Limits of the record

The sources gathered here are weighted toward Owen and his family. No letter, diary or petition from an ordinary New Lanark worker, woman or child forms part of this record; the one address spoken to the villagers is the employer's own voice, and no reply from them survives. The claim that nearly 20,000 visitors came to New Lanark between 1815 and 1825 comes from a diary editor's preface, not from the diary itself. The 1821 report and the November 1827 periodical survive only in scans whose reading is unreliable in places, so neither can be quoted safely without the page images. The phrase "Battle of the Bees" has not been traced to any Owenite source and is best left aside. The 1816 parliamentary evidence, the constitution and minutes of New Harmony, the *New Harmony Gazette* in its own run, Owen's *Crisis* and *Economist*, the newspaper reaction to the 1833 exchange, and the hostile pamphlets by M'Gavin and Baines are not part of this record. American reporting on New Harmony appears only in reprint, and the co-operative press represented here is London-centred. Nothing in this chapter is translated, but the Welsh and German dimensions of Owen's life and of the Rappite community lie outside what could be consulted.
