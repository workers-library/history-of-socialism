Friedrich Engels walked the industrial districts of Manchester in the 1840s and wrote down what he saw. The river below the town was not scenery but a consequence of how production was organised:

> "At the bottom flows, or rather stagnates, the Irk, a narrow, coal-black, foul-smelling stream, full of debris and refuse, which it deposits on the shallower right bank."
>
> Source: [Friedrich Engels, *The Condition of the Working Class in England*, written 1844 to 1845, English edition 1887](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1845/condition-working-class/ch04.htm)

Engels was a young businessman living in the city, and his book mixes observation with official reports. It is a witness's polemic, not a measurement. Two claims about industrial work must be kept apart. Surplus value is Marx's term for the extra value that wage workers create beyond what they are paid, a claim about how production and payment are organised. Alienation is the condition in which the product and the process of a person's work confront them as forces that control them, a claim about the human relation to work. Both arise from the same body of writing, but they were made twenty years apart and are not the same proposition.

## What the witness and the reformer describe

Engels's book was written between September 1844 and March 1845 and published in Leipzig in 1845 ([MIA editorial headnote](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1845/condition-working-class/index.htm)). The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* describes it as based on close observation of production techniques and living conditions and still widely regarded as his masterpiece ([SEP, "Friedrich Engels"](https://plato.stanford.edu/Entries/engels)). He described the towns, housing, Irish immigrant labourers, mines and agricultural workers. He was not a worker and wrote with a political purpose. His pages are one observer's evidence, not a transcript of workers' words or a survey.

Robert Owen, a mill owner at New Lanark, gave a more precise account of the working day because he was arguing for a law. In 1817 he said that "in the manufacturing districts it is common for parents to send their children of both sexes at seven or eight years of age, in winter as well as summer, at six o'clock in the morning" ([Owen, *Observations on the Effect of the Manufacturing System*, 1817](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/economics/owen/observations.htm)). He asked for a twelve-hour day, no work under ten, six-hour days for children under twelve and basic literacy. Reading the amended bill in 1818, he wrote to the prime minister: "It permits children to be employed at nine years of age for twelve hours and a half per day," naming cotton spinners, several of them MPs, who resisted ([Owen to the Earl of Liverpool, 1818](https://archive.org/download/newviewofsociety00owen/newviewofsociety00owen_djvu.txt)). The 1819 Factory Act fell short of what he wanted. In his autobiography he recalled a committee debating behind closed doors before deciding not to hear him ([Owen, autobiography](https://archive.org/download/lifeofrobertowen00owenuoft/lifeofrobertowen00owenuoft_djvu.txt)), a memory that cannot be checked against the minutes.

Owen's school shows another side of discipline. His son Robert Dale described classes of twenty to forty and a "silent monitor," a coloured block that recorded conduct without a rebuke. "The children are governed, not by severity, but by kindness," he wrote in 1824 ([Robert Dale Owen, *An Outline of the System of Education at New Lanark*](https://archive.org/download/anoutlinesystem00owengoog/anoutlinesystem00owengoog_djvu.txt)). He called the experiment partial and recorded that most children entered the factory at ten. The school could not undo what the mill and the household did outside its walls.

## Hours as a field of struggle

In Lawrence, Massachusetts, the strike of 1912 began with a cut in hours from 56 to 54 a week without matching pay. The federal investigation reported that "during the first few days of the strike approximately 14,000 employees withdrew from their work, later accessions increased the number on strike to approximately 23,000." It recorded 296 strike-related arrests producing 355 court cases and a settlement giving roughly 30,000 workers increases of between 5 and 20 per cent ([Charles P. Neill, official report, 1912](https://archive.org/details/cu31924054583020)). Justus Ebert's IWW account describes a strike committee organised through nationality groups ([Ebert](https://archive.org/details/trialofnewsociet00eber)). Both agree it was not formally an IWW body, though its chairmen were members.

On the Clyde, shop stewards elected by their fellows built a committee of 250 to 300 delegates whose aim was "to place the workers in complete control of the industry" ([Gallacher, "A Call to All Workers", 1916](https://www.marxists.org/archive/gallacher/1916/call-workers.htm)). The Forty Hours strike of January 1919 brought a confrontation at George Square on 31 January and a 47-hour week settled on 10 February ([Clyde Workers' Committee records](https://www.marxists.org/archive/gallacher/1915/clyde-committee.htm)). In 1926 the Miners' Federation made the refusal of wage cuts and longer hours its watchword, and the Communist Party printed "Not a Penny off the Pay. Not a Second on the Day." ([Cook, 1926](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sections/britain/subject/general_strike/mining_crisis.htm); [CPGB manifesto](https://www.marxists.org/archive/murphy-jt/1926/strike/04.htm)). These are documented outcomes of collective pressure, not evidence that hours improved everywhere.

Distress entered Parliament too. A Burnley correspondent told MPs in May 1842: "The working classes are in an awful state of destitution; there are hundreds out of employment, and those that are employed, or partially so, cannot by their earnings procure a sufficiency of food" ([letter read to the House of Commons, 3 May 1842](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1842/may/03/the-national-petition-the-charter)). MPs defeated the motion by 49 votes to 287. The letters were selected by an advocate, not a survey.

Two months later the Mines and Collieries Bill reached its third reading. An opponent warned that if the bill passed in that shape, "hundreds of children would be thrown out of employment, and hundreds of families would be driven into workhouses"; a supporter asked whether it was "unreasonable to prevent children and boys from being employed from fourteen to sixteen hours a-day" ([Hansard, 5 July 1842](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1842/jul/05/mines-and-collieries)). MPs also disputed the investigators' evidence. One member said the commissioner Symons had told those who accompanied him at Whitehaven that the regulations were "unexceptionable" and had later written of hardship, an inconsistency he called "utterly unfounded". The exchange establishes both the length of the working day and the fact that the inspectors' reports were contested by people with interests in the mines. It does not settle how common the abuses were.

## Surplus value and alienation

Surplus value is an argument about the mechanism of capitalist production. Marx's starting point is the distinction between labour, the activity, and labour-power, the capacity to work that a worker sells for a wage. The value of labour-power is "determined by the value of the necessaries of life habitually required by the average labourer" ([*Capital*, volume I, chapter 17](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch17.htm)), and its consumption is "at one and the same time the production of commodities and of surplus-value" ([*Capital*, volume I, chapter 6](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch06.htm)). Engels restated the conclusion: "even if the capitalist buys the labor power of his laborer at its full value as a commodity on the market, he yet extracts more value from it than he paid for" ([*Socialism: Utopian and Scientific*, Part II](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1880/soc-utop/ch02.htm)). Absolute and relative surplus value, distinguished in chapter 16 of volume I, are two ways of lengthening the part of the day that produces that extra value ([*Capital*, volume I, chapter 16](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch16.htm)). Marx described work as a process in which people and nature act on one another, an exchange of matter common to every society ([*Capital*, volume I, chapter 7](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch07.htm)). The movement from labour values to prices remains disputed ([SEP, "Exploitation"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/exploitation)).

Alienation is a different claim, made in the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* written in 1844 and not published until 1932 ([MIA editorial headnote](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1844/manuscripts/preface.htm)). Marx examined estranged labour through four relations: the worker's relation to the product as "an alien object exercising power over him," to the act of production as "an alien activity not belonging to him," the estrangement of "man's species-being," and "the estrangement of man from man" ([Marx, "Estranged Labour"](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1844/manuscripts/labour.htm)). His formulation joined the themes of this chapter: "The worker becomes all the poorer the more wealth he produces, the more his production increases in power and size" ([same text](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1844/manuscripts/labour.htm)). Whether the early critique and the mature economics form one argument is contested. Althusser argued for a decisive break around 1845; other readers find continuity ([SEP, "Louis Althusser"](https://plato.stanford.edu/Entries/althusser); [Althusser, "On the Young Marx", 1961](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/althusser/1961/young-marx.htm)). They were unpublished until 1932, so nineteenth-century readers lacked that supposed key.

## Limits of the record

No source opened here records an industrial injury rate, a factory inspector's report, or the number of children employed in a named district. Engels's pages are an observer's account, Owen's figures an employer's claims, Neill's report an official investigation. None gives a worker's own account of a machine, a shift or an accident. Regulation is visible only in the demands and in Owen's statement that the 1819 Act fell short, not in the Act's text or in records of enforcement. The witnesses are reformers, organisers, officials and strike participants, each with a case to make.

The proposed studies of the Triangle fire and Blair Mountain have no sources in the current book and are research priorities, not chapters. Closing these gaps would need factory inspection returns, accident and wage series, the text and enforcement record of the Factory Acts, and testimony from workers who never became organisers. Until then this chapter can document the conditions described, the battles over hours and child labour, and the two critiques Marx and Engels built from them. It cannot measure how far factory work became safer or shorter across an industry or country.
