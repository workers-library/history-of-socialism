On 18 April 1989, students holding a sit-in outside the Great Hall of the People, the state's ceremonial hall in Beijing, presented seven demands. They were mourning Hu Yaobang, the former party leader who had died three days earlier, but the mourning had become a programme. Among the demands, preserved in a disputed translated collection, were calls to "publish information on the income of state leaders and their family members; end the ban on privately run newspapers and permit freedom of speech" ([seven student demands, 18 April 1989](https://www.nytimes.com/books/first/l/liang-tiananmen.html)). Published as *The Tiananmen Papers*, the collection is contested and no original was examined, so the wording is not a settled transcription. Even so it shows how far the students had moved in three days: from grief to elections, a free press and an end to restrictions on demonstrations.

## "Turmoil" and the hunger strike

On 26 April the Party's official newspaper, the *People's Daily*, called the protests "turmoil", casting the students as a threat to order. Their answer came on 13 May, when a hunger strike began in Tiananmen Square, the ceremonial space at the centre of Beijing where the state held its founding ceremony in 1949. The declaration issued that afternoon named inflation, official profiteering and corruption as reasons for the fast, and insisted the students were patriots. Its most-quoted line was "Democracy is not a task for a few; it takes generations." ([hunger-strike declaration, 13 May 1989](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/main_pop/ps//ps_china-tiananmen-hunger-strike.htm)). The text survives as an edited extract with unnamed translators, and its authors cannot be identified.

At about 5:40 p.m. Wang Dan announced the fast and read a declaration asking for equal dialogue and a fair judgement of the movement as patriotic and democratic. Students swore an oath: "For the democratization of China, and for the prosperity of the motherland, I willingly go on hunger strike." ([oath recorded at the Square, 13 May 1989](https://8964museum.com/time/en/t-e17-004)). Banners read "Hunger can be tolerated, but a lack of democracy cannot." ([banner recorded at the Square, 13 May 1989](https://8964museum.com/time/en/t-e17-004)). Compiled by a memorial museum from later memoirs rather than a contemporary recording, the words are probably close to what was said, but the crowd's size and the residents' behaviour cannot be checked.

## The argument at the top

The most vivid account of the leadership's divisions is also the least secure. A purported record of the Politburo Standing Committee meeting of 16 May, the small group of senior leaders who took day-to-day decisions, has Zhao Ziyang saying: "We have to revise the April 26 editorial, find ways to dispel the sense of confrontation between us and the students, and get things settled down as soon as possible." ([purported minutes of 16 May 1989](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/china/tiananmen-square-papers-andrew-nathan)). Li Peng replies that the key phrases came from Deng and cannot be changed. The editors, Andrew Nathan and Perry Link, never saw originals and describe the minutes as reconstructions from aides' notes. Alfred Chan argued in *The China Quarterly* in 2004 that such texts were stitched together from public sources and fiction; Nathan replied that the volume of named detail made fabrication implausible. The exchange is unresolved, so the passage is evidence that a split was claimed, not proof of the exact words.

## The occupation and its witnesses

Through late May the Square stayed occupied. Chai Ling, who commanded the Hunger Strike Headquarters, recorded a videotaped interview with Philip Cunningham on 28 May and asked that it be released abroad in case she was arrested. She said she had known from the start that the fast would not win results. One line became the centre of a later dispute: "Only when the Square is awash with blood will the people of China open their eyes." ([Chai Ling, interview of 28 May 1989](http://www.tsquare.tv/film/transcript_complete.php)). Chai later said the Chinese word had been mistranslated and the idea came from Li Lu; other leaders accused the filmmakers of manipulation. The footage is genuine but its interpretation contested: it shows desperation and a strategy of holding the Square, not a plan for bloodshed.

Han Dongfang, a railway electrician who helped build an independent workers' federation outside the official trade union, recalled how suddenly he was drawn in: "1989 was my first time participating in anything—I hadn't had any social movement experiences beforehand, and '89 happened so suddenly." ([Han Dongfang, oral-history interview of 2011](https://www.asiancha.com/wp/article/han-dongfang/)). He was later named a main instigator of the "counter-revolutionary rebellion" and spent twenty-two months in prison. His account was recorded after long exile and translated and edited by the interviewer, and it speaks with the politics of a later decade; it is not contemporary evidence of 1989.

Stuart Franklin, a Magnum photographer working for *Time*, watched from the sixth floor of the Beijing Hotel. "I was lying prone on a balcony on the sixth floor with Newsweek photographer Charlie Cole photographing the event around noon on that day, which I remember was 4 June." ([Stuart Franklin, retrospective account published 2021](https://www.magnumphotos.com/newsroom/the-making-of-icons/)). He photographed the Goddess of Democracy, a statue students had built, and the lone man before the tanks, and smuggled film out in a box of tea. His later essay observes that the famous image may have pushed pictures of the killings out of memory; even its date is disputed, 4 June against 5 June.

## The night of 3 June

United States Embassy reports and intelligence summaries, declassified years later, recorded what diplomats, reporters and residents could see. On 3 June unarmed troops were turned back by crowds; helmeted units with automatic weapons advanced later in the day. A morning summary of 4 June stated that "deaths from the military assault on Tiananmen Square range from 180 to 500; thousands more have been injured." ([U.S. State Department morning summary, 4 June 1989](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB16/index.html)). An earlier foreign-media figure of 50 to 70 dead had been judged "probably much too low". These were estimates, not counts, and the cables flag rumours that later proved false.

Nicholas Kristof, the *New York Times* Beijing bureau chief, and Sheryl WuDunn filed reports on 4 June. "In addition, this reporter saw five people killed by gunfire and many more wounded on the east side of the square." ([Nicholas D. Kristof, dispatch of 4 June 1989](https://web.archive.org/web/20260218123837/https://www.pulitzer.org/article/army-clears-tiananmen-square)). Kristof recorded hospital counts: 68 corpses at three hospitals, more than 38 at Fuxing, 17 at Union Medical College and 13 at Tongren. WuDunn described soldiers' reluctance and citizens blocking convoys; students repeatedly asked both reporters to tell the world what was happening. A reporter sees from particular vantage points, and Kristof noted that one widely circulated student account did not match other witnesses.

## The verdict and the dead

On 9 June, five days after the Square was cleared, Deng Xiaoping, chairman of the Central Military Commission, addressed martial-law officers. "It was also inevitable that the turmoil should grow into a counter-revolutionary rebellion." ([Deng Xiaoping, 9 June 1989](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/deng-xiaoping/1989/5.htm)). The phrase was the state's term for an attempt to overthrow socialist rule. Deng said the two main slogans aimed to overthrow the Party and dismantle socialism, argued that the army had shown restraint, and conceded that ideological education had been inadequate. He said nothing about the number killed.

The repression was a decision of the state. Martial law had been declared, the troops who cleared the Square were operating under the Central Military Commission chaired by Deng, and the Party's highest bodies subsequently endorsed what had been done. The evidence assembled here does not identify which individuals ordered particular units to fire; that question remains open, but it does not make the action ownerless.

On 23–24 June the Fourth Plenary Session of the 13th Central Committee endorsed the crackdown, removed Zhao Ziyang for "supporting the unrest and dividing the Party", and elected Jiang Zemin general secretary. The accessible account is a summary in *The Great Dictionary of Marxism*, not the plenum's communiqué; it says the plenum highly appreciated Deng's speech ([summary of the Fourth Plenum, 23–24 June 1989](https://www.marxdic.com/article/2046)). A plenum is a full meeting of a party's central committee. The summary says reform and opening would continue, which carried the settlement into the 1990s. It records a leadership decision, not the events it characterises, and its claim of popular support is an official assertion.

The official casualty report issued at the end of June claimed, in the words later quoted by Amnesty International, that "more than 3,000 civilians were wounded and over 200, including 36 college students, died during the riot" ([official report of June 1989, quoted by Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org/en/projects/the-1989-tiananmen-crackdown)). Amnesty states that the exact figures remain unknown and that the official toll is likely a serious under-reporting. No independent count exists.

Ding Zilin, a retired philosophy professor at Renmin University, recalled how her son Jiang Jielian, aged 17, left on the night of 3 June: "On the night of June 3rd, he got up from his bed and he told me that he was going to go. I tried to stop him but he was determined to go to the square." ([Ding Zilin, interview of 2013](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2013/6/4/qa-mother-mourns-tiananmen-massacre)). She broke her silence in 1991, lost her teaching post and faced detention and surveillance while campaigning with other families for an answer that, she says, never came. Her knowledge of how her son died comes from witnesses, not from her own sight.

## Limits of the record

No authoritative and complete death toll has been established. The official figure of just over 200, the State Department's range of 180 to 500 and higher estimates, including a task-force range of 500 to 2,600 in the [National Security Archive's documentary collection](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB16/index.html), are incompatible partial counts and estimates. The 241 deaths confirmed by the Beijing party secretary on 19 June, hospital records of at least 478 dead and the Tiananmen Mothers' list of 202 named victims each count something, but none is a demonstrated complete count, and this chapter does not choose between them. The May minutes rest on a contested collection. The witnesses are exiled students, one worker organiser, a bereaved parent and foreign journalists, most interviewed years later; they cannot stand in for ordinary experience. Chinese-language posters, diaries and local party and police reports were not available, the documents that would settle the decision to fire are held in closed archives, and no soldier who fired has given an account. No source records a reversal of the verdict. What the record establishes is what participants said and what the state decided; how the two relate remains open.

## How historians have read it

- **Timothy Brook, *Quelling the People: The Military Suppression of the Beijing Democracy Movement* (1992).** A study of the army and the chain of command, reconstructing the military deployment from unit histories and Chinese-language sources; it treats the decision as an institutional and operational problem. [Source: nypl.org](https://www.nypl.org/research/research-catalog/bib/b11648312)
- **Han Minzhu, ed., *Cries for Democracy: Writings and Speeches from the Chinese Democracy Movement* (Princeton University Press, 1990).** A translated collection of flyers, posters, handbills, poems and government statements, assembled under pseudonyms so that participants could speak in their own words. [Internet Archive snapshot: cries for democracy](https://web.archive.org/web/20250106060147/https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691008578/cries-for-democracy)
- **Andrew J. Nathan and Perry Link, eds., *The Tiananmen Papers* (PublicAffairs, 2001).** Presents purported secret Party documents to argue that the leadership was split and that a majority initially favoured dialogue; the editors defend the collection's authenticity despite never seeing originals. [Source: foreignaffairs.com](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/china/tiananmen-square-papers-andrew-nathan)
- **Alfred L. Chan, "The Tiananmen Papers Revisited," *The China Quarterly* 177 (March 2004), pp. 190–214, with a rejoinder by Andrew J. Nathan.** Chan argues the documents blend public sources with dramatised reconstruction and that the English edition was heavily retouched; Nathan replies that the volume of named detail makes fabrication implausible. [Source: theglobeandmail.com](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/news/world/poring-over-the-tiananmen-paper-trail/article1138348/)
- **Jean-Philippe Béja, "China Since Tiananmen: The Massacre's Long Shadow," *Journal of Democracy* 20, no. 3 (2009), pp. 5–16.** Argues that the regime recovered legitimacy by co-opting elites while erasing the movement's memory, and that opposition shifted from demonstrations to petitions and rights-based legal work. [Source: journalofdemocracy.org](https://www.journalofdemocracy.org/articles/china-since-tiananmen-the-massacres-long-shadow/)

Scholars agree on the sequence of events, the peaceful character of most protests, the use of live ammunition and the decades of censorship that followed. They remain divided over the internal decision-making, the death toll and how far 1989 shaped later Chinese politics.
