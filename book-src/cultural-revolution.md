In October 1966 a schoolgirl named Sung Yao-wu described a rally in Peking where she found herself near Mao Tse-tung. A Red Guard of the Girls' Middle School at Peking Normal University, she put an arm band on the leader's sleeve and reported the feeling in a state magazine: "We have risen to rebel, to fight!" ([Sung Yao-wu, Red Guard, *China Reconstructs*, October 1966; from a poor scan, short phrase only](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/events/cultural-revolution/1966/arm-band-mao.pdf)) The sentence was printed for foreign readers under official editorial control, so it tells us how a participant was invited to describe herself rather than what she privately thought. It is still a useful entry point, because the rebellion she announced had been authorised, step by step, in documents that can be read today.

## The documents that launched it

The movement did not begin with an undefined social mood. On 16 May 1966 the Central Committee issued a circular dissolving the existing Five-Person Group and its February "Outline Report" and replacing them with a Cultural Revolution Group under the Politburo Standing Committee. It accused Peng Chen of acting behind the leadership's back. The circular was a victorious faction's indictment, and its charges are its own claims. Nine days later, on 25 May, the philosophy teacher Nie Yuanzi and six colleagues put up a big-character poster at Peking University. A big-character poster was a large handwritten sheet displayed in public for all to read. "But here in Beida [Beijing University] the masses are being kept immobilised." ([Nie Yuanzi and six colleagues, big-character poster, 25 May 1966; bracketed gloss is the editor's](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/events/cultural-revolution/1966/ignite-the-cultural-revolution.pdf)) Mao singled the poster out for praise twice, which corroborates its importance at the launch.

On 1 August he wrote to a named school group, the Red Guards of Tsinghua University Middle School: "You say it is right to rebel against reactionaries; I enthusiastically support you." ([Mao Tse-tung, letter to Red Guards of Tsinghua University Middle School, 1 August 1966](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-9/mswv9_60.htm)) The same letter asked them to unite with all who could be united with and to give those who had made mistakes a way out. On 5 August, in "Bombard the Headquarters," he turned on officials who had tried to restrain the students. "But in the last fifty days or so some leading comrades from the central down to the local levels have acted in a diametrically opposite way." ([Mao Tse-tung, "Bombard the Headquarters," 5 August 1966](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-9/mswv9_63.htm)) The decision adopted on 8 August, known from its sixteen clauses as the Sixteen Points, gave the movement its rules. "The main target of the present movement is those within the Party who are in authority and are taking the capitalist road." ([Central Committee of the CPC, decision adopted 8 August 1966 (the "Sixteen Points"), translated in *Peking Review*, 12 August 1966](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/peking-review/1966/PR1966-33g.htm)) Those in authority taking the capitalist road meant senior officials accused of steering the Party back toward market relations. Another clause urged, "Trust the masses, rely on them and respect their initiative. Cast out fear. Don't be afraid of disturbances." ([same decision, section 4](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/peking-review/1966/PR1966-33g.htm))

The contradiction sits in the text without any historian's help. The same decision that tells the masses to cast out fear also says disputes must be settled by reasoning, not by coercion or force, and that the great majority of cadres and masses must be united behind the movement. The press repeated the warning in September 1966 and again in December. The existence of those warnings is contemporary evidence that coercion was already a recognised problem.

## The rebels were not of one mind

The word Red Guard covers people with quite different grievances. Hsu Chien-hua, a twenty-year-old kindergarten staff member in Peking, wrote in a rebel periodical in January 1967 about a work team sent into her workplace after staff put up posters attacking the principal. A work team was a group dispatched to take charge of a unit's campaign. "All the members of my family were so moved that we broke down in tears, because we were all victims of the bourgeois reactionary line." ([Hsu Chien-hua, "A Blood and Tear Accusation," 29 January 1967](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/events/cultural-revolution/1967/blood-and-tear.pdf)) This is strong evidence of what the writer believed and of the language rebel rhetoric used; it says little about the principal's conduct, whom every opponent in the piece is called a capitalist authoritarian.

Students were divided too. A February 1967 article attacked what its editors called "origin theory," the claim that a revolutionary family background made someone naturally red and a reactionary background marked them for life. "Advocates of this theory are trying to split the student body into grades and create a new privileged stratum." ([editorial preface to "Origin Theory," February 1967; the preface is a paraphrase in this edition](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/events/cultural-revolution/1967/origin-theory.pdf)) At the same time, fifteen students of the Talien Mercantile Marine Institute walked more than 1,000 kilometres through twenty-one counties and cities, carrying Mao's works and doing farm work as they went. "15 revolutionary students of the Talien Mercantile Marine Institute organized the Long March Detachment of Red Guards." ([*Peking Review*, 28 October 1966](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/events/cultural-revolution/1966/long-march-detachment.pdf)) The 1934–35 march was being deliberately re-enacted. A wall poster of 26 December 1966 sent educated youths to the mountains and countryside to be tempered by class struggle, production struggle and scientific experiment. These documents give the form and official meaning of the journeys; they do not give the students' private motives.

## Force and the limits of dissent

Within a year the language of the campaign had a harder edge. A rebel report of 3 June 1967 described fighting at a cotton mill in Chengchow on 30 May under the headline "Bloodbath in Central China." "Before the blood stains of the 'May 4' tragedy dried up and only four days after the bloody incident of 'May 26' occurred, May 30 marked another day of sorrow and heroism for those inhabiting the land of Central China!" ([rebel correspondents, "Bloodbath in Central China," 3 June 1967](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/events/cultural-revolution/1967/bloodbath.pdf)) The leaflet calls itself a factual account, but each side in these reports claimed the other had engineered a counter-revolutionary plot, and it supplies no independent casualty figures. It establishes that organised violence with casualties was being reported in the rebel press within days. It cannot tell us who killed whom or in what numbers.

Dissent at the top was narrower still. Fourteen years later the Party's 1981 Resolution named the senior leaders who had objected around February 1967. "Around February 1967, at various meetings, Tan Zhenlin, Chen Yi, Ye Jianying, Li Fuchun, Li Xiannian, Xu Xiangqian, Nie Rongzhen and other Political Bureau members and leading comrades of the Military Commission of the Central Committee sharply criticized the mistakes of the 'cultural revolution'." ([1981 Resolution, USC translation](https://china.usc.edu/node/20941)) Their objection was labelled the February adverse current and punished. The Resolution gives the victors' summary and the victors' label; it does not reproduce the minutes, so the substance of each leader's objection has to be taken from that summary. An adverse current here means a deviation from the correct revolutionary line, as defined by those who prevailed.

## After the rallies

Memoirs written after 1976 describe what the documents leave out. Nien Cheng, a Shanghai manager for Shell who was imprisoned from 1966 to 1973, opens her account with the night she was summoned to a criticism meeting. "The past is forever with me and I remember it all. I now move back in time and space to a hot summer's night in July 1966, to the study of my old home in Shanghai." ([Nien Cheng, *Life and Death in Shanghai*, opening of chapter 1](https://groveatlantic.com/book/life-and-death-in-shanghai/)) She describes the banners denouncing a colleague, the heat and the hierarchy of benches, and more than six years in solitary confinement after refusing to confess. Her book was written for readers abroad by an unusually privileged, English-educated professional, so it cannot stand for workers or peasants. Yang Jiang, a writer and translator then nearly sixty, spent three years at a May 7 cadre school, a rural institution where officials and intellectuals did manual labour and political study, and recorded the lives of intellectuals whose careers were permanently changed. Jung Chang's *Wild Swans* told the same decade through three generations of one family and became a bestseller after 1976. Neither of the latter two texts is quoted here, because the surviving record gives us their publication and subject matter rather than passages that can be checked word by word. A leaflet establishes a claim, a memoir establishes a remembered life, and a resolution establishes an official verdict. None alone establishes the whole event.

## What the evidence can bear

No source assembled here offers a defensible national death toll for 1966 to 1976, and none is offered in this chapter. The contemporary documents are overwhelmingly urban, student, party and official; the countryside appears as a destination for educated youths rather than in peasants' own words, and minority nationalities are almost absent. The memoirs were written after the outcome was known, so they cannot recover what people thought in the moment. Nearly all the contemporary texts are English translations whose translators are often unnamed, and the Red Guard magazine pieces survive in poor scans, so only short phrases can be quoted safely. A movement's own press can show what it authorised, feared and claimed. It cannot show the private reasoning of the leadership, the experience of victims who never wrote, or the long-term effects on those who survived. Establishing casualties, local sequences or the reception of these texts inside China would require archives, newspapers and oral-history collections that are not available in this record.

## How historians have read it

1. **Roderick MacFarquhar and Michael Schoenhals, *Mao's Last Revolution* (Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 2006).** A documentary political history arguing that Mao deliberately used the Red Guards against the party and that the army eventually restored order with great violence. It remains the standard high-political account. [Source: hup.harvard.edu](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674027480)
2. **Lowell Dittmer, *China's Continuous Revolution: The Post-Liberation Epoch, 1949–1981* (University of California Press, 1987).** Treats continuing the revolution as the organising principle of the period, built from documentary research and interviews with 48 émigrés; agrees with MacFarquhar on Mao's initiative but puts more weight on the theory and language of permanent mobilisation. [Source: publishing.cdlib.org](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/china-s-continuous-revolution-the-post-liberation-epoch-1949-1981.html)
3. **Yiching Wu, *The Cultural Revolution at the Margins: Chinese Socialism in Crisis* (Harvard University Press, 2014).** Argues from archival and cultural material that grassroots rebels pushed the revolution beyond Mao's intentions and critically reflected on Chinese socialism, before the state demobilised and devoured its own activists; a live challenge to purely top-down accounts. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL17576517W)
4. **Yang Jisheng, *World Turned Upside Down: A History of the Chinese Cultural Revolution* (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2021; translated by Stacy Mosher and Guo Jian).** A long narrative by an independent journalist based in mainland China, using documentary evidence to tell the whole decade; it agrees with the broad judgment that the period was catastrophic but differs from party-centred accounts in scale and sources. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL21918455W)
5. **Anita Chan, *Children of Mao: Personality Development and Political Activism in the Red Guard Generation* (Macmillan, 1985).** A participant-scholar's study of the Red Guard generation, useful for testing the memoirs against interview-based social analysis; it sits between the contemporary documents and the retrospective literary accounts. [DOI: 978 1 349 07317 7](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-349-07317-7)

Broad scholarly agreement: Mao initiated the movement, mass mobilisation produced widespread violence and humiliation, the army eventually ended the radical phase, and the 1981 Resolution is a political verdict rather than a neutral account. Live disagreement: how autonomous the grassroots radicals were, how far ideology rather than bureaucratic conflict explains the course of events, and how much the surviving record can support outside the elite and urban centres.
