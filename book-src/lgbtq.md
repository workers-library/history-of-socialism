In May 1934 a Scottish-born journalist working in Moscow sat down to write to Stalin. Harry Otter Whyte was a member of the Communist Party of Great Britain and an editor at the *Moscow Daily News*, and he was a gay man. The Soviet government had issued a decree on 7 March making sodomy a crime. Whyte asked how the new law could be reconciled with the ideas it claimed to defend: "The question is as follows: can a homosexual be considered someone worthy of membership in the Communist Party?" (Harry Whyte, letter to Stalin, May 1934; English translation by Jeff Meek. [Source: blog.historicenvironment.scot](https://blog.historicenvironment.scot/2024/02/harry-whyte-the-scot-who-challenged-stalin/)) He did not abandon socialism. He used Stalin's own attacks on the levelling of personal life, and comparisons with women, colonised peoples and Jews under Hitler, to argue that the decree was wrong. He said he had asked secret-police officials, psychiatrists and his editor for a clear answer and had received contradictory replies. Stalin never answered. A marginal note attributed to him dismissed the letter as the work of an idiot and a degenerate, and it was filed.

## The law and the letter

Whyte's question only makes sense against an earlier change. The Soviet criminal codes of 1922 and 1926 had removed penalties for same-sex relations between consenting adults. Historians working from newly opened Soviet archives have argued that this was a deliberate revolutionary project, not an oversight, and that the 1934 decree reversed it. The reversal turned a private matter into a matter of state order. Whyte's argument, the clearest surviving contemporary case by a gay communist that the new law contradicted Marxism, stayed inside the language of his time. He wrote of a "constitutional" minority, offered a two-percent estimate, and distinguished between people born homosexual and those who acquired the condition. Those claims belong to the assumptions of the 1930s and should not be repeated as findings. What endures is his method: a party member holding his own movement to its stated principles, and finding that it would not answer.

## Sugar and the new man

In Cuba the argument took a harsher form. The government's UMAP camps, a network of forced-labour sites, joined work in the sugar fields to an attempt to manufacture a particular kind of militant, masculine "new man". The camps were not secret. Castro spoke of them at the University of Havana in March 1966, and a historian counting the press found a dozen articles in 1966 and 1967 carrying photographs and interviews with cheerful internees. He also found that eleven pieces in *Granma* and *Verde Olivo* referred to the camps without once mentioning homosexuals, because propaganda had to protect their reputation. Scholarship disagrees about what they were for. One reading treats them as social engineering, a deliberate remaking of gender and labour; another argues they served economic and security goals, varied from camp to camp, and were not a single uniform institution, with Jehovah's Witnesses among the worst treated. Neither reading makes the experience of the people held there harmless.

José Rolando Valdés was sent to the Primero de Enero camp, where gay men and Christians were held together. He recalled being picked up after an incident at his naval camp, refusing the uniform, being beaten and kept in his underwear in a cell for four days. He described a sergeant putting "effeminate" boys naked into a cold water tank, a start at half past five or six in the morning, roughly twelve hours in the cane field, evening political study, seven pesos a month, and a machete wound he had to work through. "Aunque salgas con vida, a un lugar así no sobrevives del todo." (José Rolando Valdés, interview published December 2019. [Source: washingtonblade.com](https://www.washingtonblade.com/2020/01/03/a-la-umap-nunca-sobrevives-del-todo/); my translation: "Even if you leave alive, you never fully survive a place like that.") He said he did not speak of it for more than fifty years out of shame, later lived under another name, married and emigrated in 2002. His is one retrospective account, given in exile after decades of silence, with some details explicitly second-hand. It establishes what the experience was like for him, not how common it was.

## A club on Veteranenstraße

East Germany followed a different sequence. The state changed its law in 1968, moved toward acceptance in the mid-1980s, and commissioned films about gay life, yet its security service, the Stasi, continued to treat gay and lesbian organisers as a hostile force. The documentary *The Other Love*, made in 1988 by the state film studio with a museum and gay and lesbian activists, let people speak about coming out and about social discrimination. *Coming Out*, a feature film, premiered on 9 November 1989, the night the Wall opened. Both were permitted artefacts; their existence shows official approval, not the absence of prejudice.

The Sonntags-Club in Berlin began when Uschi Sillge and others found rooms in 1986 and spread the word through what they called "Buschfunk". "Im Februar 1986 hatten wir ihn gefunden, den Mittzwanziger-Klub in der Veteranenstraße." (Uschi Sillge, recollection first published 1994. [Internet Archive snapshot: content 11666](https://web.archive.org/web/20200927231406/http://lernen-aus-der-geschichte.de/Lernen-und-Lehren/content/11666); my translation: "In February 1986 we found it, the club for people in their mid-twenties on Veteranenstraße.") They chose a neutral name, built a group that answered letters and sent programmes to isolated people, and campaigned for the right to place contact advertisements. Stasi files recorded a directive to prevent a gay organisation, described the club as a hostile and negative force, and assigned an informer to split church from non-church groups. Sillge recalled this roughly a decade later, and the Stasi documents reach us quoted by other authors rather than read in the original files.

## The personal is political

In the West, gay liberation borrowed from the New Left and argued that sexuality could not be separated from politics. The London Gay Liberation Front manifesto insisted that legal reform was not enough: "But gay liberation does not just mean reforms. It means a revolutionary change in our whole society." (Gay Liberation Front Manifesto, London, 1971, revised 1978. [Source: fordham.edu](https://www.fordham.edu/halsall/pwh/glf-london.html)) It rejected the expectation that gay people should be patient: "We do not intend to ask for anything. We intend to stand firm and assert our basic rights." (Gay Liberation Front Manifesto, London, 1971, revised 1978. [Source: fordham.edu](https://www.fordham.edu/halsall/pwh/glf-london.html)) Carl Wittman's American "A Gay Manifesto" wrote from a white, male, middle-class position, said he could not speak for lesbians, compared the gay ghetto to a refugee camp, and refused to put gay liberation behind other struggles: "Neither capitalist or socialist countries have treated us as anything other than non grata so far." (Carl Wittman, "A Gay Manifesto", 1970. [Source: libcom.org](https://libcom.org/article/gay-manifesto-carl-wittman)) A Marxist cell within the same movement replied that personal consciousness had to be tied to institutions: "We assert that human liberation in all its forms, including Gay Liberation, requires effective self-determination, i.e., democracy, in all spheres of social life." (The Red Butterfly, "Comments on Carl Wittman's 'A Gay Manifesto'", 1970. [Source: libcom.org](https://libcom.org/article/gay-manifesto-carl-wittman)) These texts were written in confrontational language, and some of their claims about children are not findings; they are quoted here only for the argument about strategy.

The socialist gay journal *Gay Left*, published from 1975 to 1980, set out to bring both worlds together. A participant historian recalls that the collective was all male and white, was criticised for both, and ended as the political climate shifted right. The archive also carries a warning that the journal published material supporting paedophilia, a record of serious error alongside useful analysis. The Combahee River Collective, a Black feminist and lesbian socialist group in Boston, offered a different answer. Its members came out of the civil rights, Black nationalist and Panther movements and were disillusioned by sexism and homophobia. They defined their socialism in plain terms: "We are socialists because we believe that work must be organized for the collective benefit of those who do the work and create the products, and not for the profit of the bosses." (Combahee River Collective Statement, April 1977. [Source: historyisaweapon.com](https://historyisaweapon.com/defcon1/combrivercoll.html)) They held that racial, sexual, heterosexual and class oppression interlock, and they refused separatism: "Although we are feminists and Lesbians, we feel solidarity with progressive Black men and do not advocate the fractionalization that white women who are separatists demand." (Combahee River Collective Statement, April 1977. [Source: historyisaweapon.com](https://historyisaweapon.com/defcon1/combrivercoll.html)) The statement was an agreed position, not a record of every disagreement; it notes a lesbian and straight split inside the collective itself.

## Limits of the record

Almost all of this testimony is retrospective and politically shaped. Sillge recalled 1986 in 1994, Valdés recalled the late 1960s after fifty years of silence, and the historians of the gay left wrote as former participants. The films were made by a state studio or by committed advocates; the collective statement records what its authors agreed to say. The result is a history of arguments, policies and remembered experience, not a measured account of how many people were interned, how ordinary Party members or colleagues behaved, or what happened to those who never organised. Most of the people who speak are literate, politically active, male and often in exile. Lesbian, bisexual and trans voices inside the socialist states are thin, the East German material being the exception. Language is a further barrier: Harry Whyte's letter was read in Spanish translation, the Sillge and Valdés recollections in German and Spanish, while the Russian original, the Stasi files and the Cuban military press were not opened. Archive references are reproduced from other publications rather than checked against the holdings. Finally, the sources themselves mostly use the word "homosexual"; that is their term and it is kept here where they use it. "LGBTQ+" is the framing of this account, not the language of the participants.
