Kronstadt was a fortress island in the Gulf of Finland, within sight of Petrograd ([Avrich, *Kronstadt, 1921*, p. 1](https://files.libcom.org/files/kronstadt_1921_avrich.pdf)). Its sailors had been celebrated as the pride of the revolution since 1917, when they helped the Bolsheviks take power and later served as commissars in the civil war. On the evening of 28 February 1921, the crews of the battleships *Petropavlovsk* and *Sevastopol* met to answer a question that had grown through the winter of War Communism, the system of grain requisitioning and military control that kept the cities fed: could a soviet, an elected council of workers' and soldiers' deputies, exist without the Communist Party running it? By 1 March a mass meeting had adopted a resolution drafted from the ship's proposals and chaired by Stepan Petrichenko. Alexander Berkman, writing in 1922 from reports reaching Petrograd, put the assembly at sixteen thousand.

## Fifteen demands

The resolution was political before it was military. It asked for new soviet elections by secret ballot with free agitation beforehand; freedom of speech and press for workers, peasants, anarchists and left socialists; freedom of assembly for unions and peasant organisations; the release of political prisoners; a commission to review prison and camp cases; the abolition of the party's political departments and armed Communist detachments; the removal of the roadblocks that seized food; equal rations; and peasant freedom over land, cattle and handicraft work. "In view of the fact that the present soviets do not express the will of the workers and peasants, immediately to hold new elections by secret ballot, with freedom to carry on agitation beforehand for all workers and peasants" (Petropavlovsk resolution, adopted 1 March 1921. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/events/kronstadt/1921/resolution.htm)). Equal rations matter here, because Trotsky later suggested the sailors wanted privileges. The voting record matters too: the resolution carried with two abstentions, and Kalinin and Vasiliev voted against it. That record appears in the insurgent press and in two accounts written from outside the fortress, which is as close to corroboration as this evidence gets.

## Two newspapers

In Moscow the response came within hours. On 2 March Trotsky, chairman of the Revolutionary War Council, issued a communiqué in *Pravda* naming an ex-Tsarist general, Kozlovsky, as the leader and describing a French counter-intelligence plot worked through White Guard leaflets and Socialist Revolutionaries, the peasant-oriented party. "Behind the SRs, this time too, stood a Tsarist General." (Leon Trotsky, communiqué, 2 March 1921. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1921/military/ch59.htm)). Kozlovsky was declared an outlaw, Petrograd was put under a state of siege, and power passed to a defence committee. This was propaganda written by a belligerent, not an investigation, and the claim that Kozlovsky directed the rising is contradicted by the rebels' own account. Their paper, *Izvestiia*, described a committee formed to hold the town and fortress and anxious to avoid a fight. "The Provisional Committee is deeply concerned that there should not be spilled a single drop of blood." (Provisional Revolutionary Committee of Kronstadt, *Izvestiia* no. 1, 3 March 1921; translated by Scott Zenkatsu Parker. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/events/kronstadt/izvestia/01.htm)). Its reports of an uprising already under way in Petrograd were false. Each paper is evidence of what its editors wanted readers to believe, not of what was happening elsewhere.

## The last warning

On 5 March Trotsky issued an ultimatum. "Only those who surrender unconditionally may count on the mercy of the Soviet Republic." (Leon Trotsky, "A Last Warning," 5 March 1921. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1921/military/ch60.htm)). It demanded that the arrested commissars be released and the garrison disarmed, and it assigned responsibility for civilian harm to the insurgents before any assault began. The much repeated phrase about shooting sailors like partridges is not in this signed text; it comes from later retellings. Emma Goldman was in the Tauride Palace on 4 March and heard Zinoviev, Kalinin, an arsenal worker and a sailor speak. She recorded a resolution ordering surrender on pain of extermination, and on 5 March she, Berkman, Perkus and Petrovsky asked the Petrograd Soviet for a five-person mediation commission. None was sent. The commissars seized on 2 March were detained, not killed; Berkman records that a motion to detain the others was voted down. Two assaults failed before Red troops crossed the ice on 16 and 17 March.

## The victory and its uses

Lenin gave the crisis little weight in public. Reval, March 13 on 13 March, while it was still unresolved, he said: "This Kronstadt affair in itself is a very petty incident. It no more threatens to break up the Soviet Government than the Irish disorders are threatening to break up the British Empire." (V. I. Lenin, *New York Herald* interview, 13 March 1921, printed 15 March. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1921/mar/15a.htm)). The Tenth Congress adopted "On Party Unity" on 16 March, citing the uprising as proof that a faction, an organised group within the party campaigning for a distinct programme, helped counter-revolution. It ordered all such groups dissolved and allowed expulsion, including of Central Committee members. The tax in kind, decreed on 21 March, began the retreat from War Communism. Trotsky, addressing foreign journalists as the fighting ended, argued that the old revolutionary crews had been replaced by casual elements and barely engaged with the fifteen points. Karl Radek, writing in April, conceded that peasant discontent and resentment of requisitioning were real causes and put the sailors' own question: they had made the revolution and proclaimed Soviet power, but who now exercised it?

The defeated drew their own verdict. Goldman later wrote that the event broke the last thread that held her to the Bolsheviks; Berkman and Serge reached the same verdict. Victor Serge, who had worked in Petrograd alongside Zinoviev, rejected Trotsky's version sixteen years on. "I was in Petrograd at that time, working together with Zinoviev, I saw these events first-hand." (Victor Serge, "The Truth about Kronstadt," November 1937. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/serge/1937/11/truth-kronstadt.htm)). Petrichenko, in exile, denied outside direction. "Popular anger against the dictatorship of the Communist Party—or rather against its bureaucracy—took the form of an insurrection. This is how precious blood came to be spilt." (Stepan Petrichenko, January 1926, in Russian; read in Ida Mett's English translation. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/archive/mett/1938/kronstadt.htm)). These are self-defences written after defeat, not transcripts. White émigré papers, banks and governments welcomed the revolt and tried to send help, which Trotsky's own quotations from the foreign press confirm; a chain of command running from them to the fortress is not established by anything here. Outside encouragement and a genuine local grievance are not mutually exclusive.

## Limits of the record

The documentary base is real but uneven. The sailors' side survives through a hostile-edited printing of the resolution, fourteen issues of a paper produced under siege, and the retrospective testimony of its president; the government's side through official communiqués, press interviews and victorious polemics. Rank-and-file voices are almost absent. Red Army records, Cheka files and Soviet archives were closed to Mett in 1948 and much remains inaccessible; Getzler later used newly published archival documents that lie outside this record. Nearly every primary text is a translation, sometimes a translation of a translation, and Petrichenko is read only inside Mett's English. Four points remain unsettled: the alleged outside direction; the crews' class composition, which Trotsky called casual, Radek mainly peasant, and Mett long-service; the conduct of the fighting and the number dead; and whether negotiation could have averted the assault. Point four of the resolution is dated 10 March in some texts and 1 March in another, a date already past when it was adopted. The "partridges" phrase is unverified in the signed ultimatum, casualty figures are estimates without a stated method, and the photographs in the RFE/RL essay have no archival provenance ([Avrich, *Kronstadt, 1921*, 1970, ch. 1](https://files.libcom.org/files/kronstadt_1921_avrich.pdf); [Mett, *Kronstadt*, 1938](https://www.marxists.org/archive/mett/1938/kronstadt.htm)). Goldman's and Serge's accounts are recollections shaped by their break with the Bolsheviks, and one sailor's memory cannot stand for a garrison. No reliable total of the dead can be recovered from these sources.

## How historians have read it

- **Paul Avrich, *Kronstadt 1921* (Princeton University Press, 1970; Norton, 1974).** The standard monograph, drawing on émigré material and survivors; its endnotes are used by the Trotsky edition as the fullest account. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3237243W/Kronstadt_1921)
- **Israel Getzler, *Kronstadt 1917–1921: The Fate of a Soviet Democracy* (Cambridge University Press, 1983).** Reads the rising as a "Third Revolution" seeking to restore the soviet democracy of 1917–18, tracing institutional memory through figures such as Anatolii Lamanov. [Cambridge University Press: DE4B625C19583B5711DDCD6](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/kronstadt-19171921/DE4B625C19583B5711D442484405DCD6)
- **Isaac Deutscher, *The Prophet Armed: Trotsky 1879–1921* (Oxford University Press, 1954).** A sympathetic Trotsky biography whose Kronstadt passage calls the Bolshevik denunciation "groundless" and hears in the sailors' demands "a bitter and hostile echo" of Trotsky's own promises. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL5128439W/The_Prophet_Armed)
- **Orlando Figes, *A People's Tragedy: A History of the Russian Revolution* (1996).** A broad social narrative useful on the disputed question of the crews' composition. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL72141W/A_people's_tragedy)
- **Ida Mett, *The Kronstadt Commune* (Paris, 1938; English translation, 1967).** An activist history which prints the rebels' documents, the committee's membership list and Petrichenko's evidence. [Marxists Internet Archive: kronstadt](https://www.marxists.org/archive/mett/1938/kronstadt.htm)

Broad agreement: the White-generals explanation is inadequate; the demands centred on soviet elections, civil liberties and food; the rising was crushed by force in March 1921 and NEP followed within weeks. Live disagreement concerns the revolt's class character, whether it was counter-revolutionary in effect, whether force was avoidable and what it signifies for later socialist traditions. These positions are not equally supported by the documents printed here: the conspiracy claim rests mainly on the belligerents' communiqués, while the demands and the sequence of repression are documented from several directions.
