Ernesto Guevara de la Serna was born in Rosario, Argentina, in 1928 and was killed in Bolivia in 1967. Between those dates he became a doctor, a commander in the Cuban revolutionary war, a minister in the Cuban government, a banker, and the most widely read theorist of rural guerrilla war in Latin America. His life and his writings are treated together here because he repeatedly joined a military decision to a political or ethical argument. The evidence comes from his own diaries, letters, essays and speeches, from the recollections of those who fought beside him, and from historians who have assessed what his ideas achieved in practice.

## Argentina, the journey, and Guatemala

Pathfinder Press describes him as "Ernesto Che Guevara (1928 to 1967) was an Argentine-born central leader of the Cuban Revolution" ([Pathfinder Press, Ernesto Che Guevara author page](https://www.pathfinderpress.com/collections/ernesto-che-guevara)). The Marxists Internet Archive encyclopedia calls him "Argentinian doctor; joined Castro in Mexico … a leader of the 1956-59 Cuban Revolution. Che served as president of Cuba's national bank and as Cuba's minister of industry in the period immediately following the Cuban Revolution" ([MIA Encyclopedia of Marxism, "Guevara, Che (1928-1967)"](https://www.marxists.org/glossary/people/g/u.htm#guevara-che)). He wrote little about his childhood, and his own retrospective accounts are deliberately brief. Writing to Guillermo Lorentzen in May 1963 he reduced the whole of his life before the Cuban Revolution to a single sentence:

> "I was born in Argentina, I fought in Cuba, and I began to be a revolutionary in Guatemala."
>
> Source: [Che Guevara, letter to Guillermo Lorentzen, Havana, 4 May 1963](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/05/04.htm)

The young man who appears in *The Motorcycle Diaries* is literate, self-ironising and medically trained. He warned his readers against the heroic pose before he had begun the journey:

> "This is not a story of heroic feats, or merely the narrative of a cynic; at least I do not mean it to be. It is a glimpse of two lives running parallel for a time, with similar hopes and convergent dreams."
>
> Source: [*The Motorcycle Diaries*, "So we understand each other"](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1952/motorcycle-diaries/ch01.htm)

He described his method as that of a witness: "Man, the measure of all things, speaks here through my mouth and narrates in my own language that which my eyes have seen." He also marked the distance between the person who wrote the notes and the person who revised them years later:

> "The person who wrote these notes passed away the moment his feet touched Argentine soil again. The person who reorganizes and polishes them, me, is no longer, at least I am not the person I once was. All this wandering around 'Our America with a capital A' has changed me more than I thought."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1952/motorcycle-diaries/ch01.htm)

The journey of 1951 to 1952 took him through the Chilean nitrate fields, the Andes, the Amazon and a leper colony. Its political education appears less as doctrine than as a series of encounters with poor people. Staying with farm labourers in Chile, he recorded that "in general they didn't try to communicate with us, as is typical of the subjugated Araucanian race who maintain a deep suspicion of the white man who in the past has brought them so much misfortune and now continues to exploit them" ([*The Motorcycle Diaries*, chapter 6](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1952/motorcycle-diaries/ch06.htm)). At that point he still described himself as a drifter whose "destiny is to travel" ([chapter 7](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1952/motorcycle-diaries/ch07.htm)). The "Our America" of the diary was not an abstraction. It was the America of the Araucanian labourer and the Quechua-speaking peasant, and its unity would later be defined as "language, habits, religion, a common foreign master" ([*Message to the Tricontinental*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/04/16.htm)). The authorized Ocean Press edition of 2005, with its map, itinerary and appendix reproducing the 1960 speech to medical students, explicitly links the young traveller to the mature revolutionary ([MIA, *The Motorcycle Diaries* index](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1952/motorcycle-diaries/index.htm)).

Asthma appears in Che's own account of the war. In his account of the *Granma* aftermath he records that on 5 December 1956 he asked for an inferior rifle because "I was in very poor physical condition due to an attack of asthma that had bothered me throughout our ocean voyage and I did not want to be held responsible for wasting a good weapon" ([*Alegría de Pío*, in *The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf)). Fidel Castro recalled that on the voyage Che "spent the whole voyage suffering a serious asthma attack without a single chance of relief, but also without a single complaint" ([Castro, *In Tribute to Che Guevara*, 18 October 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1967/10/oct-18-1967.htm)). Castro also noted the doctor's vocation: "During those first moments he was our troop's doctor. And so the bonds were forged and the comradeship feelings arose" (Ibid.).

Che placed his political birth in Guatemala in 1954, after the elected government of Jacobo Árbenz was overthrown by a CIA-backed mercenary force. His sentence to Lorentzen is the clearest statement he left on the subject ([letter to Lorentzen, 4 May 1963](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/05/04.htm)). Castro's tribute independently describes the mechanism: Che was "imbued with a deep spirit of hatred and contempt for imperialism, not only because his political background had already acquired a considerable degree of development, but because he had only recently had the opportunity to witness in Guatemala the criminal imperialist intervention through the mercenary soldiers who ruined the revolution in that country" ([Castro tribute, 18 October 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1967/10/oct-18-1967.htm)). The *Che Reader* records that "Che's first analyses of the wars in Korea and Vietnam were written in 1954 during his stay in Guatemala, which was also invaded by imperialist forces" (note 27 to Part 3, [*The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf)). In 1963 he followed the fate of Guatemala closely. He wrote to Lorentzen that "in Guatemala the guerrillas are fighting. The people have to some extent taken up arms. There is only one possibility of slowing the development of a struggle that shows all signs of developing toward a Cuban- or Algerian-type revolution", and named "'free elections' with Arévalo" as that possibility ([letter to Lorentzen, 4 May 1963](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/05/04.htm)). Guatemala therefore marks the point where anti-imperialism became a strategic conclusion for him: he concluded that ruling classes and their foreign backers would not concede a revolutionary outcome through elections. That conclusion underlies the later theory of armed struggle.

## Mexico, the Granma, and the Sierra Maestra

Castro dates the meeting in Mexico City to 1955:

> "It was a day in the month of July or August of 1955 when we met Che. And in one night, as he tells in his accounts, he became a future expeditionary of the 'Granma'. But at that time that expedition had no ship, no weapons, no troops. And it was like this, along with Raúl, Che joined the group of the first two on the list of 'Granma'."
>
> Source: [Castro, *In Tribute to Che Guevara*, 18 October 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1967/10/oct-18-1967.htm)

Che's farewell letter of 1965 confirms the same starting point from his side: "At this moment I remember many things: when I met you in Maria Antonia's house, when you proposed I come along, all the tensions involved in the preparations" ([*Farewell letter to Fidel Castro*, 1 April 1965](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/04/01.htm)). Castro stresses the speed with which the doctor became a combatant and a commander. At the first actions Che was "our troop's doctor"; at El Uvero "he was not only a soldier, but the most distinguished of the soldiers in that action". When one flank of an attack was uncovered, "Che, who was still a doctor, asked for three or four men, among them a man with a machine-gun rifle, and in a matter of seconds he quickly set out to take the attack mission from that direction". Castro's summary is explicit: "This is how he earned the rank of Commander and chief of the second column organized in the Sierra Maestra" (Ibid.).

Che's own account of the landing is precise. In *Episodes of the Cuban Revolutionary War* he wrote:

> "Alegría de Pío is a place in Oriente Province, Niquero municipality, near Cape Cruz, where on December 5, 1956, the dictatorship's forces surprised us."
>
> "We had landed on December 2, at a place known as Las Coloradas beach. … We had reached Cuba following a seven-day voyage across the Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean Sea, without food, in a boat in poor condition, with almost everyone plagued by seasickness from lack of experience in sea travel. We had left the port of Tuxpan on November 25, a day when a stiff gale was blowing and all navigation was prohibited."
>
> Source: [*Alegría de Pío*, in *The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf); also [MIA, *Reminiscences of the Cuban Revolutionary War*, chapter 1](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/reminiscences/ch01.htm)

At the surprise attack of 5 December, with "our 82-man troop", he had to choose between his medical bag and the ammunition:

> "Perhaps this was the first time I was faced with the dilemma of choosing between my devotion to medicine and my duty as a revolutionary soldier. There, at my feet, were a knapsack full of medicine and a box of ammunition. I couldn't possibly carry both of them; they were too heavy. I picked up the box of ammunition, leaving the medicine, and started to cross the clearing, heading for the cane field."
>
> Source: [*The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf)

He ended the episode with a sentence about the collectivity that would carry the war: "This was our baptism of fire on December 5, 1956, in the outskirts of Niquero. Such was the beginning of forging what would become the Rebel Army" (Ibid.). The first victory, at La Plata on 16 January 1957, is described in language that stresses its political effect:

> "Our first victory was the result of an attack on a small army garrison at the mouth of the La Plata River in the Sierra Maestra. The effect of our victory was electrifying and went far beyond that craggy region. It was like a clarion call, proving that the Rebel Army really existed and was ready to fight."
>
> Source: [*The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf); cf. [MIA, *The Battle of La Plata*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/reminiscences/ch02.htm)

Che wrote these memoirs as a corrective to heroic legend. His prologue asks that the account not be "regarded as a full account" and insists that any narrator "be strictly truthful. They should not pretend, for their own aggrandizement, to have been where they were not, and they should be wary of inaccuracies" ([Prologue to *Reminiscences of the Cuban Revolutionary War*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/reminiscences/prologue.htm)). The same insistence appears in his letter of December 1962 to Carlos Franqui, where he corrected the record of the invasion: "It is false that the war for me took second place to meeting the needs of the peasantry. At that time winning the war was the important thing"; and "I believe that the historical truth must be respected: to fabricate it at whim does not lead to any good results" ([letter to Carlos Franqui, 24 to 28 December 1962](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1962/12/29.htm)).

The theoretical lessons he drew from the Sierra were set out in *Notes for the Study of the Ideology of the Cuban Revolution* of 8 October 1960. He identified an early "subjectivist" error, by which he meant blind faith in a rapid popular uprising, and its correction:

> "Before the landing of the Granma, a mentality predominated that, to some degree, might be called 'subjectivist': blind confidence in a rapid popular explosion, enthusiasm and faith in the power to liquidate the Batista regime by a swift, armed uprising combined with spontaneous revolutionary strikes, and the subsequent fall of the dictator."
>
> "After the landing comes the defeat, the almost total destruction of the forces, and their regrouping and integration as guerrillas. Characteristic of those few survivors, imbued with the spirit of struggle, was the understanding that to count upon spontaneous outbursts throughout the island was a falsehood, an illusion. They understood also that the fight would have to be a long one and that it would need vast campesino participation."
>
> Source: [Che Guevara, *Notes for the Study of the Ideology of the Cuban Revolution*, 8 October 1960](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1960/10/08.htm)

The outcome was a transformation of the revolutionaries themselves: "their distrust of the campesino has been converted into affection and respect for his virtues; their total ignorance of life in the country has been converted into a knowledge of the needs of our guajiros; their flirtations with statistics and with theory have been fixed by the cement which is practice" (Ibid.). Che repeated the point in *Socialism and Man in Cuba*: "Every one of the combatants of the Sierra Maestra who reached an upper rank in the revolutionary forces has a record of outstanding deeds to his or her credit. They attained their rank on this basis" ([*Socialism and Man in Cuba*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)).

## Santa Clara and the victory of 1959

Castro records the mission and the rank. Che "earned the rank of Commander and chief of the second column organized in the Sierra Maestra" and later led "the invasion leading a column, a column which was being pursued by thousands of soldiers through absolutely flat and unknown territory, carrying out, together with Camilo, a formidable military feat". Of the battle itself Castro wrote:

> "he demonstrated it in his fulminating campaign in Las Villas; and he proved it, above all, in his audacious attack on the city of Santa Clara, penetrating with a column of barely 300 men in a city defended by tanks, artillery and several thousand infantrymen. These two exploits consecrate him as an extraordinarily capable leader, as a teacher, as an artist of the revolutionary war."
>
> Source: [Castro, *In Tribute to Che Guevara*, 18 October 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1967/10/oct-18-1967.htm)

Che's own correction of the record confirms the disposition of forces. Ramiro Valdés "was the second chief of the invasion, and then he led the operations in the eastern sector while I marched toward Santa Clara" ([letter to Carlos Franqui, 1962](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1962/12/29.htm)). Granma's historical account supplies the operational detail. Che left the Sierra Maestra "a cumplir la misión encomendada por Fidel de traer la guerra hasta el centro de Cuba"; by December 1958 he had liberated "la mayoría de los pueblos de la antigua provincia de Las Villas"; and Santa Clara was defended by "el regimiento Leoncio Vidal, la estación de policía, la motorizada, una pista aérea militar y cerca de 3 000 oficiales y soldados sobre las armas". He established his command post at the Universidad Central "Marta Abreu" on 28 December 1958. The battle lasted four days. Residents barricaded the streets on 29 December, the armoured train was derailed and its garrison surrendered, and the Leoncio Vidal regiment fell at midday on 1 January 1959 ([Granma, "Santa Clara, la ciudad del Che", 8 October 2026](https://www.granma.cu/cuba/2026-10-08/santa-clara-la-ciudad-del-che-08-10-2026-09-10-36)).

The same article preserves Che's address at the university in 1959, when he was made Doctor Honoris Causa in Pedagogy. The idea of a new socialist person appears there in popular form:

> «Y, ¿qué tengo que decirle a la Universidad como artículo primero, como función esencial de su vida en esta Cuba nueva? Le tengo que decir que se pinte de negro, que se pinte de mulato, no solo entre los alumnos, sino también entre los profesores; que se pinte de obrero y de campesino, que se pinte de pueblo, porque la Universidad no es el patrimonio de nadie y pertenece al pueblo de Cuba».
>
> Source: [Granma, "Santa Clara, la ciudad del Che"](https://www.granma.cu/cuba/2026-10-08/santa-clara-la-ciudad-del-che-08-10-2026-09-10-36). English translation: "And what am I to say to the University as its first article, as the essential function of its life in this new Cuba? I must tell it to paint itself black, to paint itself mulatto, not only among the students but also among the professors; to paint itself worker and peasant, to paint itself people, because the University is nobody's patrimony and belongs to the people of Cuba."

Che divided the revolution into two stages: "the armed action up to January 1, 1959, and the political, economic and social transformations since then" ([*Notes for the Study of the Ideology of the Cuban Revolution*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1960/10/08.htm)). The early government was a coalition in which the transition to workers' power was contested. Che's account is exact:

> "In January 1959, the revolutionary government was established with the participation of various members of the treacherous bourgeoisie. The presence of the Rebel Army was the basic element constituting the guarantee of power. Serious contradictions developed right away. In the first instance, in February 1959, these were resolved when Fidel Castro assumed leadership of the government, taking the post of prime minister. This process culminated in July of the same year with the resignation under mass pressure of President Urrutia."
>
> Source: [*Socialism and Man in Cuba*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)

## Agrarian reform, nationalisation, and the Ministry of Industries

The first structural measure was the agrarian reform of 17 May 1959. The authorized footnote in *Socialism and Man in Cuba* records that this law, passed only four months after the revolution took power, "was seen as the decisive step in fulfilling the revolutionary program proposed at Moncada in 1953. Che participated in the drafting of this new law along with other comrades proposed by the revolutionary leadership" (footnote 34, [loc. cit.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)). Che gave the class content in a television speech of 20 March 1960:

> "We had to strike at the most troublesome of all the monopolies — the monopoly in land ownership — destroy it, turn the land over to the people, and then start the real struggle, because despite everything, this was just the first contact between two enemies."
>
> "One and a half percent of the landowners — Cubans or foreigners, but owners of Cuban land — possessed 46 percent of the national territory, while 70 percent owned only 12 percent of the national territory."
>
> Source: [*Political sovereignty and economic independence*, 20 March 1960](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1960/03/20.htm)

At Punta del Este on 8 August 1961 Che gave the clearest definition of the revolution's character:

> "It is an agrarian, antifeudal and anti-imperialist revolution that under the imperatives of its internal evolution and of external aggressions became transformed into a socialist revolution, and that declares itself as such before all the Americas: a socialist revolution."
>
> Source: [*On Growth and Imperialism*, 8 August 1961](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1961/08/08.htm)

He enumerated what had been done: "Our revolution nationalized the domestic economy; it nationalized basic industry, including mining. It nationalized all foreign trade, which is now in the hands of the state, and which we proceeded to diversify by trading with the whole world. It nationalized the banking system in order to have in its hands the efficient instrument with which to exercise the function of credit in accordance with the country's needs. It provides for the participation of the workers in the management of the planned national economy" (Ibid.). The same speech recorded urban reform, the abolition of racial discrimination, the struggle for women's equal rights, and a compact statement of internationalism: "It is, then, a revolution with humanist characteristics. It is in solidarity with all the oppressed peoples of the world" (Ibid.).

Che held several economic posts in the early government. Castro's retrospective of 1987 sets out the chronology in a passage whose transcription in the MIA edition contains typographical errors, marked here with [sic]:

> "He was in INRA [National Institute of Agrarian Reform] and ad [sic] managed a few industries under its jurisdiction at a time when the main industries had not yet been nationalized and only a few factories had been take [sic] over. He headed the National Bank, another of the responsibilities entrusted to him, and he also headed the Ministry of Industry when this agency was set up. Nearly all the factories had been nationalized by then and everything had to organized [sic], production had to be maintained, and Che took on the job, as he had taken on many others. He did so with total devotion, working day and night, Saturdays and Sundays, and he really set out to solve far-reaching problems. It was then that he tackled the task of applying Marxist-Leninist principles to the organization of production, the way he understood it, the way he saw it."
>
> Source: [Castro, *Che's ideas are absolutely relevant today*, 8 October 1987](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1987/10/08.htm)

Castro's summary of the economic position matters for interpreting the later debates:

> "In essence - in essence! - Che was radically opposed to using and developing capitalist economic laws and categories in building socialism, he advocated something that I had often insisted on: Building socialism and communism is not just a matter producing [sic] and distributing wealth but is also a matter of education and consciousness."
>
> Source: [Castro, 8 October 1987](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1987/10/08.htm)

Here "law of value" means, in Marx's terms, the way market exchange treats different kinds of labour as equivalent through prices, so that production is governed by exchange value rather than by a plan. Che's own early statement of principle predates the ministry: "The watchword of this moment is planning: the conscious, intelligent restructuring of all the problems that will face the people of Cuba in future years" ([*Political sovereignty and economic independence*, 20 March 1960](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1960/03/20.htm)). The authorized *Che Reader* notes identify "The Significance of Socialist Planning" of 1964 and record that Che "warned also of the trend within socialism to reform the economic system by strengthening the market, material interests and the law of value. To counter this trend, Che advocated centralized, antibureaucratic planning that enriched consciousness" (note 25, [*The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf)).

The polemics sharpened this position. The MIA footnote identifies "On the Concept of Value", published in *Our Industry* in October 1963, as the opening of the debate, and quotes Che: "We want to make it clear that the debate we have initiated can be invaluable for our development only if we are capable of conducting it with a strictly scientific approach and with the greatest equanimity" (footnote 37, [*Socialism and Man in Cuba*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)). Pathfinder's catalogue lists "On the Concept of Value: A Reply to Albert Mora" and "The Meaning of Socialist Planning: A Reply to Charles Bettelheim", both in *New International* no. 8 of 1991 ([Pathfinder author page](https://www.pathfinderpress.com/collections/ernesto-che-guevara)).

## The missile crisis, the United Nations, and peaceful coexistence

Che's account of the October 1962 missile crisis is embedded in his farewell letter of 1965 and in his speech to the United Nations in 1964. In the letter he wrote:

> "I have lived magnificent days, and at your side I felt the pride of belonging to our people in the brilliant yet sad days of the Caribbean [Missile] crisis. Seldom has a statesman been more brilliant as you were in those days."
>
> Source: [*Farewell letter to Fidel Castro*, 1 April 1965](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/04/01.htm)

At the UN on 11 December 1964 he explained the origin of the weapons:

> "As is well known, after the tremendous commotion of the so-called Caribbean crisis, the United States undertook certain commitments with the Soviet Union. These culminated in the withdrawal of certain types of weapons that the continued acts of aggression of the United States — such as the mercenary attack at Playa Girón and threats of invasion against our homeland — had compelled us to install in Cuba as an act of legitimate and essential defense."
>
> "But we emphatically refuse, since Cuba does not recognize the right of the United States, or of anyone else in the world, to determine the type of weapons Cuba may have within its borders."
>
> Source: [*At the United Nations*, 11 December 1964](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1964/12/11.htm)

In *Socialism and Man in Cuba* the crisis becomes a theoretical datum about the masses: "During the October [1962 missile] crisis and in the days of Hurricane Flora [in October 1963] we saw exceptional deeds of valor and sacrifice performed by an entire people. Finding the method to perpetuate this heroic attitude in daily life is, from the ideological standpoint, one of our fundamental tasks" ([loc. cit.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)).

The 1964 speech opens with a declaration of the anticolonial epoch: "The final hour of colonialism has struck, and millions of inhabitants of Africa, Asia and Latin America rise to meet a new life and demand their unrestricted right to self-determination and to the independent development of their nations" ([*At the United Nations*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1964/12/11.htm)). It contains the demand for the restoration of China's seat, an unqualified denunciation of apartheid and of the murder of Lumumba, and the judgement that "the difference between men does not lie in the color of their skin, but in the forms of ownership of the means of production, in the relations of production" (Ibid.). On economic development it states that "so long as the economically dependent peoples do not free themselves from the capitalist markets and, in a firm bloc with the socialist countries, impose new relations between the exploited and the exploiters, there will be no solid economic development" (Ibid.).

For Che's theoretical break with Soviet orthodoxy, the speech's qualification of peaceful coexistence, the doctrine that states with different social systems should avoid war and compete peacefully, is the central passage:

> "We must also state that it is not only in relations among sovereign states that the concept of peaceful coexistence needs to be precisely defined. As Marxists we have maintained that peaceful coexistence among nations does not encompass coexistence between the exploiters and the exploited, between the oppressors and the oppressed."
>
> "We must also warn that the principle of peaceful coexistence does not encompass the right to mock the will of the peoples, as is happening in the case of so-called British Guiana."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1964/12/11.htm)

Che did not reject coexistence between states outright. He refused to extend it to the class struggle, to colonial liberation, or to the defence of a revolutionary state under attack. The 1967 *Message to the Tricontinental* carried the same argument further ([loc. cit.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/04/16.htm)).

## Algiers, the new man, and the departure from Cuba

Between June and September 1959 Che toured the countries of the Bandung Pact, the precursor of the Non-Aligned Movement, which meant a grouping of states that refused formal alignment with either Cold War bloc ([note 20, *The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf)). In February 1965 he addressed the Second Economic Seminar of Afro-Asian Solidarity in Algiers, where Cuba was an observer and he sat on the presiding committee ([*At the Afro-Asian Conference in Algeria*, 24 February 1965](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/02/24.htm)). The speech states internationalism as a necessity rather than a sentiment:

> "There are no borders in this struggle to the death. We cannot be indifferent to what happens anywhere in the world, because a victory by any country over imperialism is our victory, just as any country's defeat is a defeat for all of us. The practice of proletarian internationalism is not only a duty for the peoples struggling for a better future, it is also an inescapable necessity."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/02/24.htm)

It is also a founding text of the critique of unequal exchange, the process by which rich countries obtain more value from poor ones through prices they control:

> "There should be no more talk about developing mutually beneficial trade based on prices forced on the backward countries by the law of value and the international relations of unequal exchange that result from the law of value."
>
> "The socialist countries have the moral duty to put an end to their tacit complicity with the exploiting countries of the West."
>
> "For us there is no valid definition of socialism other than the abolition of the exploitation of one human being by another."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/02/24.htm)

It gives the economic programme in the language of law rather than preference: "It is worth emphasizing once again that the means of production should preferably be in the hands of the state, so that the marks of exploitation may gradually disappear. Furthermore, development cannot be left to complete improvisation. It is necessary to plan the construction of the new society. Planning is one of the laws of socialism, and without it, socialism would not exist" (Ibid.). On military solidarity the formulation is equally categorical: "Arms cannot be commodities in our world. They must be delivered to the peoples asking for them to use against the common enemy, with no charge and in the quantities needed and available" (Ibid.). The authorized note records that Che had been touring Africa since December 1964 and that at this time he was preparing for involvement in the liberation movement in the Congo, which began in April 1965 (note 19, [*The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf)).

*Socialism and Man in Cuba* is the central theoretical essay. It was a letter to Carlos Quijano, editor of the Uruguayan weekly *Marcha*, first published on 12 March 1965 under the title "From Algiers, for *Marcha*. The Cuban Revolution Today" and republished on 5 November 1965 as "Exclusive: A Special Note from Che Guevara" (footnote 29, [*Socialism and Man in Cuba*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)). It refuses the liberal premise that socialism abolishes the individual for the state. Che writes of "this multifaceted being" that it "is not, as is claimed, the sum of elements of the same type … which acts like a flock of sheep. It is true that it follows its leaders, basically Fidel Castro, without hesitation. But the degree to which he won this trust results precisely from having interpreted the full meaning of the people's desires and aspirations" (Ibid.). Of the relation between Castro and the crowd he writes:

> "At the great public mass meetings one can observe something like the dialogue of two tuning forks whose vibrations interact, producing new sounds. Fidel and the mass begin to vibrate together in a dialogue of growing intensity until they reach the climax in an abrupt conclusion crowned by our cry of struggle and victory."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)

The central theoretical move is the analysis of the transition. Che rejects the idea that mature capitalism is the only precondition for socialist revolution: "some countries that were weak limbs on the tree of imperialism were torn off first, a phenomenon foreseen by Lenin". He then insists that the transition in such countries cannot rely on capitalist categories:

> "The pipe dream that socialism can be achieved with the help of the dull instruments left to us by capitalism (the commodity as the economic cell, profitability, individual material interest as a lever, etc.) can lead into a blind alley. When you wind up there after having traveled a long distance with many crossroads, it is hard to figure out just where you took the wrong turn. Meanwhile, the economic foundation that has been laid has done its work of undermining the development of consciousness. To build communism it is necessary, simultaneous with the new material foundations, to build the new man and woman."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)

The "new man" is a programmatic claim about consciousness, incentives and labour, not a rhetorical ideal. Che begins from the premise that the individual is "an unfinished product" in whom "the vestiges of the past are brought into the present in one's consciousness, and a continual labor is necessary to eradicate them". Education is two-sided: "society acts through direct and indirect education" while "the individual submits to a conscious process of self-education". The motor of mobilisation "must be moral in character, without neglecting, however, a correct use of the material incentive, especially of a social character", and "society as a whole must be converted into a gigantic school". Work becomes "a quota for the fulfillment of one's social duty", and the means of production belong to society while "the machine is merely the trench where duty is performed" (Ibid.). The new person is already being born, but the image "is not yet completely finished; it never will be, since the process goes forward hand in hand with the development of new economic forms". Che insists that this is a new phase "unforeseen" by Marx in the *Critique of the Gotha Program*: "we are not dealing with a period of pure transition … but rather with an initial period of the transition to communism, or of the construction of socialism". His theoretical modesty is explicit: "we must agree that we are still in diapers and that it is necessary to devote ourselves to investigating all the principal characteristics of this period before elaborating an economic and political theory of greater scope". The two pillars of the future theory will be "the education of the new man and woman and the development of technology", and the aim is "the human being of the 21st century, although this is still a subjective aspiration, not yet systematized". Internationalism follows: "Proletarian internationalism is a duty, but it is also a revolutionary necessity. This is the way we educate our people" (Ibid.).

The essay contains its most-quoted sentence in an argument about the vanguard's sacrifices:

> "At the risk of seeming ridiculous, let me say that the true revolutionary is guided by great feelings of love. It is impossible to think of a genuine revolutionary lacking this quality. Perhaps it is one of the great dramas of the leader that he or she must combine a passionate spirit with a cold intelligence and make painful decisions without flinching."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)

Its closing claims collective practice as the source of freedom: "We socialists are freer because we are more fulfilled; we are more fulfilled because we are freer"; "our freedom and its daily sustenance are paid for in blood and sacrifice. Our sacrifice is a conscious one: an installment paid on the freedom that we are building"; and "the road is long and, in part, unknown. We recognize our limitations. We will make the human being of the 21st century — we, ourselves. We will forge ourselves in daily action, creating a new man and woman with a new technology" (Ibid.). The essay ends: "Patria o muerte! [Homeland or death!]"

Two further points matter for the argument about revisionism. Che warns against the Soviet-style cultural doctrine: "But why try to find the only valid prescription in the frozen forms of socialist realism?" He insists on "an ideological-cultural mechanism that permits both free inquiry and the uprooting of the weeds that multiply so easily in the fertilized soil of state subsidies". He names revisionism directly: "By reacting against the human being of the 19th century we have relapsed into the decadence of the 20th century. It is not a very grave error, but we must overcome it lest we leave open the door for revisionism" (Ibid.). The 1960 *Notes* supply the complementary formulation: "The Cuban Revolution takes up Marx at the point where he himself left science to shoulder his revolutionary rifle. And it takes him up at that point, not in a revisionist spirit, of struggling against that which follows Marx, of reviving 'pure' Marx" ([*Notes for the Study of the Ideology of the Cuban Revolution*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1960/10/08.htm)). Che's anti-revisionism therefore had two targets: the restoration of a "pure" pre-Leninist Marx and the bureaucratic degeneration of actually existing socialism, meaning the socialist states as they existed under party rule rather than the theoretical ideal.

Che resigned from the Cuban leadership in his letter of 1 April 1965:

> "I feel that I have fulfilled the part of my duty that tied me to the Cuban revolution in its territory, and I say farewell to you, to the comrades, to your people, who now are mine. I formally resign my positions in the leadership of the party, my post as minister, my rank of commander, and my Cuban citizenship. Nothing legal binds me to Cuba. The only ties are of another nature — those that cannot be broken as can appointments to posts."
>
> "I state once more that I free Cuba from all responsibility, except that which stems from its example. If my final hour finds me under other skies, my last thought will be of this people and especially of you."
>
> "I carry to new battlefronts the faith that you taught me, the revolutionary spirit of my people, the feeling of fulfilling the most sacred of duties: to fight against imperialism wherever it may be. … Wherever I am, I will feel the responsibility of being a Cuban revolutionary, and I shall behave as such."
>
> Source: [*Farewell letter from Che to Fidel Castro*, 1 April 1965](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/04/01.htm)

His self-criticism reveals his standard of leadership: "My only serious failing was not having had more confidence in you from the first moments in the Sierra Maestra, and not having understood quickly enough your qualities as a leader and a revolutionary" (Ibid.). The MIA archive also carries his farewell letter to his parents from mid-1965 ([works index](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/works.htm)).

In October 2026 the official Cuban press marked the anniversary of Che's death with a meeting between young people and the country's leaders. The speaker who recalled the farewell letter was Miguel Díaz-Canel Bermúdez, First Secretary of the Communist Party of Cuba and President of the Republic, not Fidel Castro, who died in 2016. The report quotes him as follows:

> «El Presidente evocó momentos históricos que han quedado como testimonios de la «admiración común» que unía a dos hombres excepcionales: la carta de despedida del Che, en la cual el Guerrillero Heroico deja explícita su admiración rotunda por el hermano Fidel; y esa confesión del Comandante en Jefe, cuando dijo: «Yo todavía sueño con el Che».»
>
> Source: [Granma, "El Che permanece entre los jóvenes", 8 October 2026](https://www.granma.cu/cuba/2026-10-08/el-che-permanece-entre-los-jovenes-vivo-como-no-lo-queria-el-enemigo-08-10-2026-17-10-54). English translation: "The President recalled historical moments that remain as testimony to the 'common admiration' that united two exceptional men: Che's farewell letter, in which the Heroic Guerrilla makes explicit his unstinting admiration for his brother Fidel; and that confession of the Commander in Chief, when he said: 'I still dream of Che.'"

The same report records Díaz-Canel saying that the two leaders were "estudiosos severos del marxismo" and "críticos de las malas apologías del marxismo" ("serious students of Marxism" and critics of "bad apologies for Marxism"). He also referred to the continuing official significance of Che's "Hombre nuevo" or New Man: "No podemos construir un mundo mejor si no hay un Hombre nuevo" ("We cannot build a better world if there is no New Man"). He closed with a line Che had published in 1965, "el presente es de lucha; el futuro es nuestro" ("the present is a time of struggle; the future is ours") (Ibid.; [*Socialism and Man in Cuba*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)). The report also lets ordinary participants speak. Leanet Proenza del Hoyo, a coordinator of the youth project Farma Joven, described volunteers delivering medicines to older people and to children's homes, and Alejandra de la Cruz Moreno described students supporting a paediatric hospital. A Palestinian student at Cuba's Latin American School of Medicine spoke of his family's gratitude for Cuban medical training and of medicines donated by Palestinians to Cuban hospitals (Ibid.). These are short accounts in an official newspaper, and they describe a commemoration rather than an independent assessment, but they are testimony from participants rather than from leaders.

## The Congo and Bolivia

Pathfinder's author page states that Guevara "led volunteer internationalist columns in Congo 1965, Bolivia 1966–67" ([Pathfinder](https://www.pathfinderpress.com/collections/ernesto-che-guevara)). The *Che Reader* fixes the timing of the Congo involvement as beginning in April 1965 and records that at the Tricontinental Conference of January 1966 he was in Tanzania, having left the Congo (notes 19 and 26, [*The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf)). Its bibliography lists *Episodes of the Revolutionary War in the Congo*, in which, according to note 35, "he detailed the most important lessons of that struggle" and concluded that the national bourgeoisie was "a spent force, politically speaking" (Ibid.). The *Message to the Tricontinental* revisits the African experience: "In the Congo these characteristics appeared briefly, generated by the memory of Lumumba, but they have been losing strength in the last few months" ([*Message to the Tricontinental*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/04/16.htm)).

In Bolivia Che led the Ejército de Liberación Nacional, or ELN, the National Liberation Army of Bolivia, in a guerrilla campaign from 1966 to 1967. The authorized *Bolivian Diary* was written in those years and first published in 1968; the MIA edition is translated by Deborah Shnookal and published by Ocean Press in 2005 ([*The Bolivian Diary* index](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/bolivian-diary/index.htm)). The *Instructions to Urban Cadres* of around 22 January 1967 open: "A support network of the character we want to create should be guided by a series of norms, which are summarized below. Activity will be primarily clandestine in nature." The authorized footnote records that Che wrote the document for Loyola Guzmán when she visited the camp on 26 January 1967, and that the force was established in March 1967 as the National Liberation Army of Bolivia ([*Instructions to Urban Cadres*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/01/22.htm)). Guzmán, a Bolivian activist who handled finances and contacts for the urban support network, is one of the ordinary participants whose testimony survives; she later gave accounts of the network's work and of the repression that followed the arrests in La Paz ([National Security Archive document on the guerrilla support mechanism](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/28_1.pdf); [Global Voices interview, 2009](https://globalvoices.org/2009/01/19/bolivia-loyola-guzman-supporting-the-no-vote)).

Communiqué No. 1 of March 1967 announced the first battle:

> "On the morning of March 23, troops from the Fourth Division, quartered in Camiri, about 35-strong and led by Major Hernán Plata Ríos, penetrated guerrilla territory along the Ñacahuazú River. The entire group fell into an ambush set up by our forces."
>
> Source: [*Communiqué No. 1 to the Bolivian People*, first published 27 March 1967](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/03/27.htm)

The communiqué concluded: "In publicly announcing the first battle of the war, we are establishing what will be our norm: revolutionary truth. … Hostilities have begun. In future communiqués we will set forth our revolutionary positions clearly. National Liberation Army of Bolivia" (Ibid.). The phrase repeats the demand for truthfulness Che had made in the prologue to the Cuban war memoirs.

The campaign failed. Its outcome is documented by participants and historians, and it should not be left as an unexamined testing of a theory. The foco, the small armed nucleus at the centre of Che's method, never won the peasant support its doctrine required. The local people spoke Guaraní while the Bolivian recruits from the highlands spoke Quechua or Spanish, and many campesinos had received ten-hectare homesteads under earlier land reforms, giving them a stake in the existing order. Robert W. Jones Jr., writing in the military history journal *Veritas*, records that in their encounters with the guerrillas the campesinos "were curious, but they did not respond to the revolutionary fervor of the strange band of foreigners", and that the foco "failed to gain any support from the local population" ([Jones, "'Today a New Stage Begins': Che Guevara in Bolivia", *Veritas*, Vol. 4 No. 4, 2008](https://arsof-history.org/articles/v4n4_new_stage_begins_page_1.html)). This is a military historian's account written from the Bolivian army's side, and it should be read with that limit in mind, but its detail is specific: the difficulty of the Ñancahuazú terrain, the desertion of two Bolivian recruits who revealed the camp, the discovery of papers and photographs at the farm, and the seizure of the urban contact list from a vehicle used by Tamara Bunke, known as Tania. The Bolivian Communist Party under Mario Monje refused active support after a dispute over command in December 1966. The "Long March" of February and March 1967 exhausted the band and failed to win recruits. The urban network was rolled up, and the last radio link to Havana was lost before the final actions ([Ibid.]; [Inti Peredo, "My Campaign with Che", in *The Bolivian Diary*, pp. 239, 364 and 367](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/bolivian-diary/diary.pdf)).

The foco was destroyed by the Bolivian army, which received United States training and assistance. Che was wounded and captured on 8 October 1967 and killed on 9 October. Castro's memorial oration was deliberately restrained about the last hours:

> "And he fell. … We do not possess sufficient elements of judgment to be able to make any deduction about all the circumstances that preceded that combat, about the degree to which he could have acted in an excessively aggressive manner, but, we repeat, if as a guerrilla he had an Achilles heel, that heel Achilles was his excessive aggressiveness, his absolute contempt for danger."
>
> Source: [Castro, *In Tribute to Che Guevara*, 18 October 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1967/10/oct-18-1967.htm)

Castro also recorded that Che had been "willing to fight five, ten, fifteen, twenty years, all his life if necessary", and that "with only a handful of men an entire oligarchic army" had been confronted; "you will not possibly find any case in which someone with such a small number of men has undertaken a task of greater importance" (Ibid.). The authorized Pathfinder page gives the political characterisation: Che was "wounded, captured, and murdered by Bolivian army in CIA-organized operation" ([Pathfinder](https://www.pathfinderpress.com/collections/ernesto-che-guevara)). The peer-reviewed assessment by the Utrecht historian Dirk Kruijt is plainer. He notes the paradox that Che's texts on guerrilla strategy were studied and applied by many insurgents in the 1960s while Che himself "supervised or was directly in charge of three ill-fated guerrilla campaigns in Bolivia/Argentina (1963/64), the Congo (1965), and again Bolivia (1966/67)" ([Kruijt, "Che Guevara and guerrilla warfare", *Globalizations*, Vol. 20, No. 8, 2023, pp. 1528 to 1539, DOI 10.1080/14747731.2022.2066055](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/che-guevara-and-guerrilla-warfare)). Kruijt examines the rural foco as a doctrine, the reasons for those failures, and the way other Latin American guerrilla leaders later used and modified the method (Ibid.). The Bolivian campaign therefore stands as a defeat, not as a proof of the theory it embodied.

## The theory of the foco

The book *Guerrilla Warfare* is known through references in Che's archive. The MIA works index warns that the 1963 article "Guerrilla war, a method" is "not to be confused with his famous book on the subject", and the *Che Reader* lists "Guerrilla Warfare" among his key references on revolutionary strategy (note 32, [*The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf)). A foco is a small armed nucleus that begins operations in a favourable area and seeks, through its own action, to create the political and military conditions for a wider revolt. The Spanish term *foquismo* names the doctrine built around that method.

The full theoretical argument is in the article of September 1963, first published in *Cuba Socialista*, Año II, No. 25 ([*Guerrilla warfare: A method*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/09/guerrilla-warfare.htm); [alternative translation](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/misc/guerrilla-war-method.htm)). It opens by subordinating the method to a political end:

> "Above all, we must emphasize at the outset that this form of struggle is a means to an end. That end, essential and inevitable for any revolutionary, is the conquest of political power. In the analysis of specific situations in different countries of America, we must therefore use the concept of guerrilla warfare in the limited sense of a method of struggle in order to gain that end."
>
> Source: [*Guerrilla warfare: A method*, September 1963](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/09/guerrilla-warfare.htm)

Che repeatedly asks for the analysis of specific situations rather than a universal rule, and he rejects the idea that armed struggle and mass struggle are opposites:

> "We reject this implication, for guerrilla warfare is a people's warfare; an attempt to carry out this type of war without the population's support is a prelude to inevitable disaster. The guerrilla is the combat vanguard of the people, situated in a specified place in a certain region, armed and willing to carry out a series of warlike actions for the one possible strategic end — the seizure of power. The guerrilla is supported by the peasant and worker masses of the region and of the whole territory in which it acts. Without these prerequisites, guerrilla warfare is not possible."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/09/guerrilla-warfare.htm)

The three contributions he attributed to the Cuban Revolution follow:

> "We consider that the Cuban Revolution made three fundamental contributions to the laws of the revolutionary movement in the current situation in America. First, people's forces can win a war against the army. Second, it is not always necessary to wait for all conditions favorable to revolution to be present; the insurrection itself can create them. Third, in the underdeveloped parts of America, the battleground for armed struggle should in the main be the countryside."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/09/guerrilla-warfare.htm)

The second proposition is the foco theory in compact form. By "subjective conditions" Che meant the consciousness, organisation and leadership that a revolution needs, as distinct from the "objective conditions" of economic and social crisis. The revolutionary nucleus does not merely express already mature conditions; through armed action it generates the subjective conditions it requires. Che quoted the Second Declaration of Havana to the same effect: "The subjective conditions in each country, the factors of revolutionary consciousness, organization and leadership, can accelerate or delay revolution, depending on the state of their development. Sooner or later in each historic epoch objective conditions ripen, consciousness is acquired, organization is achieved, leadership arises, and revolution takes place" (Ibid.). He refused voluntarist credentials: "No-one can solicit the role of vanguard party as if it were a diploma given by a university. To be the vanguard party means to be at the forefront of the working class through the struggle for achieving power" (Ibid.).

On violence and the peaceful road the article is explicit: "Violence is not the monopoly of the exploiters and as such the exploited can use it too and, moreover, ought to use it when the moment arrives." The famous formulation is a theoretical claim, not a piece of rhetoric: "we should not fear violence, the midwife of new societies, but violence should be unleashed at that precise moment in which the leaders have found the most favorable circumstances" (Ibid.). He cited Martí, "He who wages war in a country when he can avoid it is a criminal, just as he who fails to promote war which cannot be avoided is a criminal", and Lenin on civil war: "To repudiate civil war, or to forget about it, would mean sinking into extreme opportunism and renouncing the socialist revolution" (Ibid.).

The operational doctrine follows. The beginnings are small and defensive:

> "Let us think how a guerrilla foco can start. Nuclei with relatively few people choose places favorable for guerrilla warfare with the intention of either unleashing a counterattack or weathering the storm, and from there they start taking action."
>
> "There are three conditions for survival that a guerrilla force must embrace if it is emerging subject to the premises described here: constant mobility, constant vigilance and constant distrust. Without these three elements of military tactics the guerrilla will find it hard to survive."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/09/guerrilla-warfare.htm)

The political character of the unit is never allowed to dissolve into military technique:

> "The guerrillas cannot forget their function as vanguard of the people — their mandate — and as such they must create the necessary political conditions for the establishment of a revolutionary power based on the support of the masses. … If a single military error can liquidate the guerrilla, a political error can hold back its development for long periods. The struggle is political-military and it must be developed and understood as such."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/09/guerrilla-warfare.htm)

The strategic arc runs from a defensive stage, through equilibrium, to the annihilation of the repressive army and the capture of the cities. The whole doctrine closes with a prediction: "We can predict its advent and triumph because it is the inevitable result of historical, economic and political conditions; and its direction cannot change. … This is a prediction. We make it with the conviction that history will prove us right" (Ibid.). Experience did not prove it right in Bolivia. Che does not glorify combat for its own sake: "walking is a form of fighting and that avoiding combat at a given moment is another"; "as long as the guerrilla army is in the position of deciding the 'how' and the 'when,' no combat should be fought that will not end in victory" (Ibid.).

The memoirs of the Cuban war are the empirical substructure of this theory. The sequence they record, of landing, defeat, regroupment, peasant alliance and victory, is what the claim that insurrection creates conditions rests on. Che noted that "two events, hardly important in terms of the number of combatants, but of great psychological value" erased the mutual distrust between city-born guerrillas and campesinos, and that "At this point, the campesinos entered the guerrilla war for the first time" ([*Notes for the Study of the Ideology of the Cuban Revolution*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1960/10/08.htm)). MIA dates *Reminiscences of the Cuban Revolutionary War* to 1963 and gives the Ocean Press edition of 2005, translated by Alexandra Keeble ([index](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/reminiscences/index.htm)); the *Che Reader* uses the title *Episodes of the Revolutionary War*, and Pathfinder publishes *Episodes of the Cuban Revolutionary War 1956–58* of 1996 ([Pathfinder](https://www.pathfinderpress.com/collections/ernesto-che-guevara)). These are variants of one body of work.

## The Tricontinental message and internationalism

The *Message to the Tricontinental* was written by Che in a training camp in Pinar del Río before setting out for Bolivia in 1966 and was first published on 16 April 1967 under the title "Create Two, Three, Many Vietnams, That is the Slogan" (note 26, [*The Che Reader*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/che-reader/che-reader.pdf)). The MIA archive identifies its publisher as the Executive Secretariat of the Organization of the Solidarity of the Peoples of Africa, Asia and Latin America, known as OSPAAAL ([*Message to the Tricontinental*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/04/16.htm)). It opens with Martí, "Now is the time of the furnaces, and only light should be seen", and challenges the supposed peace of the post-1945 world: "Twenty-one years without a world war, in these times of maximum confrontations, of violent clashes and sudden changes, appears to be a very high figure" (Ibid.).

On Vietnam it writes of solidarity that "is not a matter of wishing success to the victim of aggression, but of sharing his fate; one must accompany him to his death or to victory" (Ibid.). On the divisions of the socialist camp it says:

> "And the guilt also applies to those who maintain a war of abuse and snares — started quite some time ago by the representatives of the two greatest powers of the socialist camp."
>
> "We all know great controversies rend the world now fighting for freedom; no one can hide it. … In time of war, the expression of current differences constitutes a weakness; but at this stage it is an illusion to attempt to settle them by means of words. History shall erode them or shall give them their true meaning."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/04/16.htm)

The strategic conclusion is uncompromising: "In our struggling world every discrepancy regarding tactics, the methods of action for the attainment of limited objectives should be analyzed with due respect to another man's opinions. Regarding our great strategic objective, the total destruction of imperialism by armed struggle, we should be uncompromising" (Ibid.). On the Sino-Soviet dispute he took a position distinct from both camps: "we, the dispossessed, cannot take sides for one form or the other of these discrepancies … Regarding our great strategic objective, the total destruction of imperialism by armed struggle, we should be uncompromising" (Ibid.).

On Latin America the message gives the foco doctrine an international dimension: "if the focuses of war grow with sufficient political and military insight, they shall become practically invincible and shall force the Yankees to send reinforcements". It names the continental project and the multiplication of theatres:

> "America, a forgotten continent in the last liberation struggles, is now beginning to make itself heard through the Tricontinental and, in the voice of the vanguard of its peoples, the Cuban Revolution, will today have a task of much greater relevance: creating a Second or a Third Vietnam, or the Second and Third Vietnam of the world."
>
> "We must bear in mind that imperialism is a world system, the last stage of capitalism — and it must be defeated in a world confrontation."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/04/16.htm)

The most quoted lines follow: "How close we could look into a bright future should two, three or many Vietnams flourish throughout the world with their share of deaths and their immense tragedies, their everyday heroism and their repeated blows against imperialism, impelled to disperse its forces under the sudden attack and the increasing hatred of all peoples of the world!" (Ibid.). The theory of internationalism is then stated in its strongest form:

> "And let us develop a true proletarian internationalism; with international proletarian armies; the flag under which we fight would be the sacred cause of redeeming humanity. To die under the flag of Vietnam, of Venezuela, of Guatemala, of Laos, of Guinea, of Colombia, of Bolivia, of Brazil — to name only a few scenes of today's armed struggle — would be equally glorious and desirable for an American, an Asian, an African, even a European."
>
> "Each spilt drop of blood, in any country under whose flag one has not been born, is an experience passed on to those who survive, to be added later to the liberation struggle of his own country. And each nation liberated is a phase won in the battle for the liberation of one's own country."
>
> Source: [Ibid.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/04/16.htm)

It closes with the passage Castro quoted in his memorial oration: "Wherever death may surprise us, let it be welcome, provided that this, our battle cry, may have reached some receptive ear and another hand may be extended to wield our weapons and other men be ready to intone the funeral dirge with the staccato singing of the machine-guns and new battle cries of war and victory" (Ibid.). The 1964 UN speech and the 1967 message belong to one line of argument. In 1964 Che held that coexistence among states must be extended to all states but did not apply to class struggle or colonial peoples; by 1967 he treated state-to-state diplomacy as something that must not be allowed to paralyse the anti-imperialist war. That position had already led him to resign his Cuban citizenship in 1965.

## The economic writings and what the programme achieved

Che's economic thought is not a side interest. His economic writings, his period in the Ministry of Industries and the Bolivian campaign need to be kept distinct: what he proposed, what was attempted in Cuba while he held office, what happened in Bolivia, and how historians have judged the results are four different questions.

The texts fall into four groups. The speeches of 1960 and 1961 establish planning as "the conscious, intelligent restructuring of all the problems that will face the people of Cuba in future years" and enumerate the nationalisations ([*Political sovereignty and economic independence*, 20 March 1960](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1960/03/20.htm); [*On Growth and Imperialism*, 8 August 1961](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1961/08/08.htm)). The polemics of 1963 and 1964 develop the argument against the law of value and for central planning, including the replies to Mora and Bettelheim (footnote 37, [*Socialism and Man in Cuba*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm); [Pathfinder](https://www.pathfinderpress.com/collections/ernesto-che-guevara)). The Algiers speech gives the international dimension of the critique: "There should be no more talk about developing mutually beneficial trade based on prices forced on the backward countries by the law of value and the international relations of unequal exchange that result from the law of value", and "Planning is one of the laws of socialism, and without it, socialism would not exist" ([*At the Afro-Asian Conference in Algeria*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/02/24.htm)). Finally *Socialism and Man in Cuba* gives the philosophical grounding: "The alienated human specimen is tied to society as a whole by an invisible umbilical cord: the law of value. This law acts upon all aspects of one's life, shaping its course and destiny" ([loc. cit.](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)).

What Che proposed has been reconstructed in detail by the historian Helen Yaffe. Her book *Che Guevara: The Economics of Revolution*, published by Palgrave Macmillan in 2009, is based on substantial primary research including thirty-one interviews with Che's former colleagues. The reviewer Tim Anderson calls it "good history" and "coherent and independent", and identifies its main themes as post-revolution consolidation, the creation of a new budgetary system and the "great debate" of 1963 to 1964 over the form Cuban socialism should take, with consequences for education, salaries, administration, worker participation and science ([Tim Anderson, review of Yaffe, *Development in Practice*, Vol. 20, No. 3](https://developmentinpractice.org/reviews/che-guevara-economics-revolution)). At the centre of Che's proposal was the Budgetary Finance System, in which state enterprises were financed from the state budget rather than by borrowing from banks at interest, as in the Soviet Auto-Finance System. Under the budgetary system, contracts and purchase orders between state enterprises would replace money exchanges, and the plan would determine investment and employment. The aim was to reduce the operation of the law of value inside the state sector and to make socialist consciousness, rather than material reward, the main motor of production ([Kamran Nayeri, "The Economic and Political Thoughts of Ernesto Che Guevara", Socialist Project, 14 May 2024](https://socialistproject.ca/2024/05/economic-and-political-thoughts-of-ernesto-che-guevara)). Nayeri writes from a Trotskyist standpoint, and his argument that Che underestimated the continuing operation of the law of value and overestimated the effect of nationalisation without workers' control is a political judgement, not a neutral description. It is useful precisely because it identifies a real limit in the scheme.

What was attempted in Cuba was narrower than the theory. Che introduced the budgetary system in the enterprises under his own ministry and promoted moral incentives and voluntary labour there. In October 1963 he created the Red Battalion within the Ministry of Industries, initially ten brigades of ten workers each, each committing to at least eighty hours of voluntary labour in six months; by January 1964 the number had doubled to twenty ([Nayeri, 2024, citing Tablada and Yaffe](https://socialistproject.ca/2024/05/economic-and-political-thoughts-of-ernesto-che-guevara)). Castro's 1987 account confirms the scope in institutional terms: Che headed the National Bank, then the Ministry of Industries, when most factories had been nationalised and "everything had to organized [sic]" ([Castro, 8 October 1987](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1987/10/08.htm)). The "great debate" continued after Che left Cuba. Anderson notes that interpretations of it differ: some see a dispute over the law of value under socialism, others a disagreement about moral incentives, others a conflict between Cuban identity and a utopian view of the New Man, others a debate about how far enterprises should be financially decentralised ([Anderson, review of Yaffe](https://developmentinpractice.org/reviews/che-guevara-economics-revolution)). Yaffe's own view gives weight to the argument about state finance (Ibid.). Castro later defended Che against two caricatures: "Che had great faith in man. Che was a realist and did not reject material incentives. He deemed them necessary during the transitional stage, while building socialism. But Che attached more importance — more and more importance — to the conscious factor, to the moral factor" ([Castro, 8 October 1987](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1987/10/08.htm)).

What the programme did not achieve is also documented. The budgetary system never became the economy-wide settlement Che envisaged. Cuba's leadership did not adopt his proposals as a whole; Yaffe is quoted as concluding that "Guevara's proposals have never met consensus within Cuba. While most Cuban leaders agree in theory with his approach to socialist construction, many have argued that conditions were not ripe for its implementation" (Yaffe 2009, p. 263, quoted in [Nayeri, 2024](https://socialistproject.ca/2024/05/economic-and-political-thoughts-of-ernesto-che-guevara)). Later Cuban policy moved back toward Soviet-style accounting, material incentives and closer integration with the Soviet bloc, and the moral themes associated with Che were reasserted only in the "rectification" period from the mid-1980s ([Anderson, review of Yaffe](https://developmentinpractice.org/reviews/che-guevara-economics-revolution)). The official celebration of Che's ethics in 2026, including Díaz-Canel's call to "retomar los conceptos del Hombre nuevo" ("take up again the concepts of the New Man"), is evidence of continuing symbolic force, not of the implementation of the budgetary system ([Granma, 8 October 2026](https://www.granma.cu/cuba/2026-10-08/el-che-permanece-entre-los-jovenes-vivo-como-no-lo-queria-el-enemigo-08-10-2026-17-10-54)). The honest assessment is therefore mixed. Che's programme did shape a real debate, did introduce alternative methods in an important ministry, and did leave a body of theory that later Cuban and foreign radicals returned to. It did not displace the Soviet model in Cuba during his lifetime, and its assumption that consciousness and moral incentives could substitute for market signals across a whole economy remained untested at that scale.

What happened in Bolivia was a defeat. The foco was destroyed, Che was killed, and the Bolivian Communist Party withheld the support that might have widened the revolt. Kruijt's peer-reviewed study places Bolivia alongside the Congo and the earlier attempt in Bolivia and Argentina as one of three ill-fated campaigns, while noting that the method remained attractive to other insurgents ([Kruijt, 2023](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/che-guevara-and-guerrilla-warfare)). Castro's claim in 1967 that the enemies "believe they have defeated his ideas, defeated his guerrilla conception … And what they achieved was, with a stroke of luck, to eliminate his physical life" was a political defence of a fallen comrade, not an assessment that the campaign had worked ([Castro, 18 October 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1967/10/oct-18-1967.htm)). Similarly, his later insistence that Che's ideas had been misunderstood and never seriously tried should be read as an act of memory rather than as a finding of historical inquiry (Ibid.). The distinction between what the theory promised and what the campaign achieved is the central point: the prediction that insurrection "can create" the conditions for revolution was tested in Ñancahuazú, and the conditions were not created.

## The four contributions

Read together, Che's texts make four contributions, none of which can be separated cleanly from the others. The first is the foco as a creator of subjective conditions. The guerrilla nucleus is "the combat vanguard of the people", and "it is not always necessary to wait for all conditions favorable to revolution to be present; the insurrection itself can create them" ([*Guerrilla warfare: A method*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/09/guerrilla-warfare.htm)). This is a theory of the relation between objective and subjective factors in revolution, not a manual of tactics, and Che's account of the post-*Granma* regroupment and peasant alliance is its supporting evidence ([*Notes for the Study of the Ideology of the Cuban Revolution*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1960/10/08.htm)).

The second is guerrilla war as a road to revolution. The method is subordinated to "the conquest of political power" and develops through three stages from strategic defensive to equilibrium to the annihilation of the repressive army ([*Guerrilla warfare: A method*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1963/09/guerrilla-warfare.htm)). In the essay "Cuba: Historical exception or vanguard" of 1961 Che generalised the point: "We accept that exceptions exist which give the Cuban Revolution its peculiar characteristics. It is clearly established that in every revolution there are specific factors, but it is no less established that all follow laws that society cannot violate" ([1961 essay](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1961/04/09.htm)). His theory of underdevelopment belongs here: "In truth, we are colonial, semicolonial or dependent countries, whose economies have been deformed by imperialism, which has peculiarly developed only those branches of industry or agriculture needed to complement its own complex economy" (quoted in footnote 40 of [*Socialism and Man in Cuba*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm), from the 1961 essay).

The third is the New Man and the politics of consciousness. Socialism must build "the new man and woman" alongside the material base, and the motor of mobilisation "must be moral in character, without neglecting … the material incentive". Work becomes "a quota for the fulfillment of one's social duty" and "society as a whole must be converted into a gigantic school" ([*Socialism and Man in Cuba*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)). Castro's formulation, "Building socialism and communism is not just a matter producing [sic] and distributing wealth but is also a matter of education and consciousness", is the clearest official summary ([Castro, 1987](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1987/10/08.htm)).

The fourth is proletarian internationalism as necessity. "The practice of proletarian internationalism is not only a duty … it is also an inescapable necessity" ([Algiers, 1965](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/02/24.htm)); "Proletarian internationalism is a duty, but it is also a revolutionary necessity" ([*Socialism and Man in Cuba*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/03/man-socialism.htm)); "let us develop a true proletarian internationalism; with international proletarian armies" ([*Message to the Tricontinental*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/04/16.htm)). Castro's memorial judgement that no one "has taken the proletarian internationalist spirit to its highest level" is a measure of the legacy within the Cuban tradition ([Castro, 18 October 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1967/10/oct-18-1967.htm)).

These four strands form one argument. The foco is the organisational form Che thought appropriate to the deformed economies of "Our America". The New Man is the kind of human being the foco and the revolution were supposed to produce in war and in construction. Internationalism is the scale on which the struggle had to be fought because "imperialism is a world system, the last stage of capitalism". The critique of peaceful coexistence is the political consequence of refusing to subordinate those tasks to great-power diplomacy. Che's own formulation of the unity of theory and practice is that "their flirtations with statistics and with theory have been fixed by the cement which is practice" ([1960 Notes](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1960/10/08.htm)). The record of 1966 to 1967 shows how costly the attempt to live by that formulation could be.
