On 18 November 1915, a man awaiting execution wrote a will in his cell. "My will is easy to decide, / For there is nothing to divide." (Joe Hill, written in his cell, 18 November 1915. [Source: en.wikisource.org](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Songs_of_the_Workers_%289th_edition%29/Joe_Hill%27s_Last_Will)) Hill had no property to dispose of. He asked that his body be cremated and his ashes scattered, and ended with an instruction to organise rather than mourn. He was an organiser and songwriter for the Industrial Workers of the World, a union that carried its politics in verses printed on cheap paper. The songbook that preserved his will also printed the union's constitution, its membership fees and the address of its general secretary-treasurer, William D. Haywood. A member could carry the argument in a pocket.

## The union in the songbook

The Industrial Workers of the World was founded in Chicago in 1905. Its preamble opened with a claim that left no room for gradualism: "The working class and the employing class have nothing in common." (IWW preamble, printed in *Songs of the Workers*, ninth edition, March 1916. [Source: en.wikisource.org](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Songs_of_the_Workers_%289th_edition%29)) The union organised by industry rather than by craft. A craft union brings together workers who share one skilled trade; an industrial union brings together everyone in a factory or an industry, including the unskilled. The IWW admitted foreign-born and migratory workers whom the American Federation of Labor largely left outside. Its stated aim was not only better wages but the abolition of the wage system, summed up in the phrase "one big union".

The ninth edition of the songbook was printed in Cleveland in March 1916. Alongside the preamble it carried songs by Ralph Chaplin and Joe Hill, and its index listed "Come Join the One Big Union", "Solidarity Forever", "The Preacher and the Slave" and "John Golden and the Lawrence Strike". Its advertising pages described the union's newspaper, *Solidarity*, as carrying first-hand reports from strikes. Songs were not decoration. For members who were often newly arrived, poorly paid and working long hours, they were the main way the union explained itself.

## The argument over one big union

On 11 January 1912, ten days before the Lawrence strike began, Haywood and Morris Hillquit debated at Cooper Union in New York before a Socialist Party audience. Haywood read the preamble aloud and said that "One Big Union" should be the slogan of every socialist agitator, on the platform and in the workshop. (William D. Haywood, Cooper Union debate, 11 January 1912. [Internet Archive: 120111 hillquithaywood nycdebate](https://archive.org/details/120111-hillquithaywood-nycdebate)) He attacked craft unions as "job trusts" and called industrial unionism "socialism with its working clothes on". Hillquit conceded that industrial organisation was superior in the abstract, but argued that the form of the union mattered less than class consciousness and that a rival union would split the socialist movement. The argument was not settled doctrine. One speaker treated the union as the shape socialism would take; the other feared it would divide those who already called themselves socialists. The debate survives in a modern edited transcription rather than a contemporary verbatim report, and the editor's introduction is not a participant's words.

## On the picket line

In Lawrence, Massachusetts, the immediate cause of the 1912 strike was a cut in hours from 56 to 54 a week without a matching adjustment in pay. The federal investigation reported: "During the first few days of the strike approximately 14,000 employees withdrew from their work, later accessions increased the number on strike to approximately 23,000" (Charles P. Neill, official report, 1912. [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/cu31924054583020)). It recorded 296 strike-related arrests producing 355 court cases, and a settlement giving roughly 30,000 workers increases of between 5 and 20 per cent. Justus Ebert's IWW account describes a strike committee organised through nationality groups and the difficulty of turning a spontaneous walkout into an industrial strike. Both accounts agree that the strike committee was not formally an IWW body, though its chairmen were IWW members.

A 1925 anthology reprinting James Oppenheim's poem says that young women textile workers carried a banner reading "We want Bread, and Roses, too!"; the poem itself argued that bodily subsistence and human dignity were both demands: "Hearts starve as well as bodies; Give us Bread, but give us Roses!" (James Oppenheim, "Bread and Roses", reprinted 1925. [Source: en.wikisource.org](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Poems_for_Workers_%28Gomez_1925%29/Bread_and_Roses)) The note about the banner is the anthology editor's, not the poet's, and the phrase cannot be confirmed from it alone.

A year later, in Paterson, New Jersey, John Reed wrote from jail, where he had served twenty days for covering a silk workers' strike. An Italian striker told him, in Reed's rendering: "We all one bigga da Union. I. W. W.—dat word is pierced de heart of de people!" (an Italian striker, as reported by John Reed, *The Masses*, June 1913. Eye-dialect is Reed's rendering. [Marxists Internet Archive: masses06](https://www.marxists.org/archive/reed/1913/masses06.htm)) The dialect is Reed's, not a transcript of the striker's Italian. Reed was an advocate, hostile to the mill owners and to the leaders of the AFL and Socialist Party, and his report is argument as well as observation. His conclusion was that the rank and file, not the famous visitors, had sustained the strike: "They were the strike—not Bill Haywood, not Gurley Flynn, not any other individual." (John Reed, *The Masses*, June 1913. [Marxists Internet Archive: masses06](https://www.marxists.org/archive/reed/1913/masses06.htm))

In Everett, Washington, the issue was the right to organise lumber workers and to speak in public. Walker C. Smith's IWW book opens with the landing of men from the steamer *Verona*: "In ten minutes of seething, roaring hell at the Everett dock on the afternoon of Sunday, November 5, 1916" (C. E. Payne, preface to Walker C. Smith, *The Everett Massacre*. [Internet Archive: everettmassacreh00smituoft](https://archive.org/details/everettmassacreh00smituoft)). A University of Washington collection guide records 74 defendants, the acquittal of Thomas Tracy, and two deputies killed by friendly fire. The first shot is unknown and the number of Wobblies dead is disputed, though five are confirmed. The IWW account, the local paper, the AFL paper and the socialist paper each framed the event differently. Smith's book was a defence-fund publication written after the event; it cannot settle who fired first.

## The state's answer

The Attorney General published his own account in *The Forum* in February 1920. A. Mitchell Palmer described a Radical Division in his department and the immigration law used for deportation, then set out the premise: "Upon these two basic certainties, first that the 'Reds' were criminal aliens and secondly that the American Government must prevent crime, it was decided that there could be no nice distinctions drawn" (A. Mitchell Palmer, *The Forum*, February 1920. [Source: teachingamericanhistory.org](https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/the-case-against-the-reds/)). He cited "upward of 60,000" alleged agitators on the authority of confidential information, without offering evidence that could be checked. His article is the state's case in its own words, not a record of what the raids found.

Alexander Berkman and Emma Goldman, deported in December 1919, wrote a pamphlet while held at Ellis Island: "The 'Red Ark' is gone. In the darkness of early morning it slipped away, leaving behind many wives and children destitute of support." (Alexander Berkman and Emma Goldman, December 1919. [Internet Archive: deportationitsme00berkuoft](https://archive.org/details/deportationitsme00berkuoft)) It records families denied notice of the sailing and women and children turned away from the dock. Robert Minor's introduction says Berkman wrote the last lines with a concealed pencil behind visitors; that detail belongs to Minor's introduction, not to the two authors.

The same climate entered a courtroom in Dedham. In a petition to Governor Fuller dated 3 May 1927, Bartolomeo Vanzetti connected the case to the raids: "we were really tried not for murder, but for being Radicals, draft evaders, and pacifists" (Bartolomeo Vanzetti, petition to Governor Fuller, 3 May 1927; English assisted by counsel. [Source: dwardmac.pitzer.edu](http://dwardmac.pitzer.edu/Anarchist_Archives/bright/SaccoVan/defense.html)). He challenged the ballistics testimony and the identification evidence, and said counsel had helped with the English while the thoughts were his own. His final statement, in the poetic arrangement first printed in *Labor Action*, spoke of Sacco as a worker and turned the sentence into a claim about dignity: "The taking of our lives - lives of a good shoemaker and a poor fishpeddler - all! That last moment belongs to us - that agony is our triumph." (Bartolomeo Vanzetti, in the *Labor Action* poetic arrangement, 1927. [Source: dwardmac.pitzer.edu](http://dwardmac.pitzer.edu/Anarchist_Archives/bright/SaccoVan/vanzettilast.html)) That arrangement is not a verbatim transcript.

## How historians have read it

- **Melvyn Dubofsky, *We Shall Be All: A History of the Industrial Workers of the World* (1969).** [Internet Archive: weshallbeallhist00dubo](https://archive.org/details/weshallbeallhist00dubo) The standard scholarly narrative: it treats the IWW as a response to industrial concentration and the exclusion of unskilled workers from craft unionism, and offers the frame against which the partisan pamphlets should be read.
- **Archie Green, David Roediger and Franklin Rosemont, eds, *The Big Red Songbook* (2007; revised 2016).** [Internet Archive: Big Red songbook](https://archive.org/details/Big_Red_songbook_9781629632605) Collects and annotates songs from the IWW songbooks of 1909 to 1973, with essays on Wobbly song culture; it treats songs as evidence of political culture rather than as background colour.
- **Robert W. Dunn, *The Palmer Raids* (1948).** [Internet Archive: ThePalmerRaids](https://archive.org/details/ThePalmerRaids) A Labor Research Association account written in 1948 that reads the raids as part of a longer attack on civil liberties; politically framed, but useful for its documentary citations and for contrast with Palmer's own version.
- **Matthew Anderson, "Everett Massacre Aftermath: The Battle to Control the Story" (2016).** [Source: depts.washington.edu](https://depts.washington.edu/iww/everett_story.shtml) Argues that the meaning of Everett was produced by competing press and defence-committee narratives; strongest as a guide to those sources.
- **Norman H. Clark, *Mill Town: A Social History of Everett, Washington* (1970).** [Source: epls.org](https://www.epls.org/251/The-Everett-Massacre) The local social history listed by the Everett Public Library; it supplies the town context that the IWW pamphlet largely omits.

Historians broadly agree on the chronology of the strikes, the scale of the Lawrence settlement, the fact of the raids and deportations, and the contest over public framing. They disagree on the union's efficacy and intentions, on the weight to give its revolutionary rhetoric, and on the interpretation of the Sacco-Vanzetti evidence. The partisan pamphlets are not interchangeable with the scholarly accounts: they are the evidence those accounts interpret.

## Limits of the record

The surviving material is almost entirely in English. The strikers in Lawrence and Paterson were largely Italian, Polish, Slovak, Jewish and Syrian, and where their words survive they come through English-speaking reporters who often rendered their speech in dialect. The letters of Sacco and Vanzetti were written in the men's second language and edited before publication; Vanzetti says in his petition that counsel helped with the English. Almost every IWW source here was written by an organiser with a purpose. Ebert, Smith and the defence committees wrote to defend defendants, raise money or recruit; Palmer wrote to justify his own department. A songbook shows what the union wished would be sung, not what was sung on a particular picket line. The Everett book and the 1921 convention minutes survive in digitised texts with visibly corrupted passages and without accompanying page images. Several documents, among them the Everett Defence Committee pamphlet and John L. Miller's typescript and 1982 interview, are known only through catalogue descriptions. The original pamphlets, the *Masses* issue and the 1928 letters have not been examined in any archive. The first shot at Everett, the precise last words attributed to Rossini and Rabinowitz, the full roster of National Industrial Unions between 1912 and 1920 and the ballistics dispute in Sacco and Vanzetti remain unsettled. The only industrial-union minutes available are those of Metal and Machinery Workers Industrial Union No. 440 for February 1921, one local body just outside the main span of this story.
