In 1918 Rosa Luxemburg sat in Wronki prison, writing a book she did not live to finish. A supporter of the Bolsheviks, she accepted that the Russian Revolution needed a dictatorship, meaning rule by the working class rather than by the old propertied classes. But she insisted that this rule had to belong to the whole class and to the public life of the country. "Freedom only for the supporters of the government, only for the members of one party – however numerous they may be – is no freedom at all. Freedom is always and exclusively freedom for the one who thinks differently." ([Rosa Luxemburg, *The Russian Revolution*, ch. VI "The Problem of Dictatorship", written 1918, first published 1922, English translation by Bertram Wolfe, 1940](https://www.marxists.org/archive/luxemburg/1918/russian-revolution/ch06.htm)) She was writing from a cell, without full information about the decisions of the soviet, and her manuscript was pieced together after her death. Yet the sentence is not a footnote to a settled doctrine. It is one answer, spoken at the time, to a question socialists were arguing about while the institutions were being built: what did the transition from capitalism to socialism actually require?

Marx had placed a distinct period between the two. The "transition" here means that period of revolutionary transformation, in which the working class was expected to take power and begin to reshape property and production. Lenin, in hiding from the Provisional Government in August and September 1917, quoted Marx to place it precisely: "Between capitalist and communist society lies the period of the revolutionary transformation of the one into the other... the state can be nothing but the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat." ([V. I. Lenin, *The State and Revolution*, ch. V, written 1917, first published 1918, English from the Soviet *Collected Works*; translator not named in the online headnote; ellipsis marks an omitted passage](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1917/staterev/ch05.htm)) This was a scheme written before the seizure of power, not a description of institutions that existed. The planned seventh chapter was never written. The text was also a polemic, and Chapter VI attacked Kautsky and named the Dutch theorist Anton Pannekoek, so the later council-communist critique was already a live target in 1917.

## Reforms, democracy and the party

Luxemburg's starting point had been an argument within German social democracy about whether reforms could replace revolution. In 1899 she wrote that giving up the expectation of capitalism's collapse led by stages to giving up the socialist aim itself: "Without the collapse of capitalism the expropriation of the capitalist class is impossible. Bernstein therefore renounces expropriation and chooses a progressive realisation of the 'co-operative principle' as the aim of the labour movement." ([Rosa Luxemburg, *Reform or Revolution*, ch. IX, 1899 (revised 1908), English translation by Integer, Militant Publications, 1986](https://www.marxists.org/archive/luxemburg/1900/reform-revolution/ch09.htm)) That was before any workers' state existed, so it says nothing about how a transition government should be organised.

Her 1918 manuscript supplies the organisational claim. Socialism could not be decreed, because its concrete measures were not contained in any party programme. Democracy had to begin at the seizure of power, with "unlimited democracy": "Socialist democracy begins simultaneously with the beginnings of the destruction of class rule and of the construction of socialism." ([Rosa Luxemburg, *The Russian Revolution*, ch. VIII "Democracy and Dictatorship", written 1918, Bertram Wolfe translation](https://www.marxists.org/archive/luxemburg/1918/russian-revolution/ch08.htm)) She called the Bolsheviks' work an "immortal historical service" and blamed the distortions she saw on isolation, German occupation and the failure of the German revolution, so this is not a simple denunciation. Some passages are only rough notes which the translator rendered literally.

Lenin's answer developed in the other direction. In a pamphlet written for the Second Congress of the Communist International in April 1920, he grounded the need for "iron discipline" and "absolute centralisation and rigorous discipline" in the survival of small property: "small-scale production engenders capitalism and the bourgeoisie continuously, daily, hourly, spontaneously, and on a mass scale." ([V. I. Lenin, *"Left-Wing" Communism: An Infantile Disorder*, ch. II, written April 1920, English translation by Julius Katzer, *Collected Works* vol. 31](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1920/lwc/ch02.htm)) The dispute, in other words, was over the agent as well as the form: mass participation on one side, a single disciplined will on the other.

## The unions against the appointed managers

The argument reached industrial officials in the Workers' Opposition, a faction that wanted trade unions, rather than appointed managers, to run production. Its theses were signed on 18 December 1920 by Alexander Shliapnikov, chairman of the metalworkers' union, and officials of the artillery, miners' and textile unions, and printed in *Pravda* on 25 January 1921. "Professional and production unions are to be built on the basis of workers democracy, the elective principle and accountability of all organs from the bottom to the top." ([A. Shliapnikov and signatories, *Theses of the Workers Opposition*, signed 18 December 1920, *Pravda*, 25 January 1921, translated for marxists.org in 2004 by Barbara C. Allen](https://www.marxists.org/archive/shliapnikov/1921/workers-opposition.htm)) They demanded that no one be appointed to an economic post without the union, and that union-nominated staff could be replaced by the unions. This was a programme laid before the Tenth Congress, not a record of its implementation.

Alexandra Kollontai, a leading Bolshevik, gave the fullest first-person account from inside the leadership of how bureaucratism looked in 1920–21. "The Workers' Opposition sprang from the depths of the industrial proletariat of Soviet Russia." ([Alexandra Kollontai, *The Workers' Opposition*, ch. I, early 1921, translated from the Russian; translator not named in the online edition](https://www.marxists.org/archive/kollonta/1921/workers-opposition/ch01.htm)) She traced the dispute to the Ninth Congress decision for one-man management and listed workers' initiatives over dining rooms, wood transport and nurseries that central institutions had blocked. Her sharper claim was about power inside the party: "Bureaucracy is a direct negation of mass self-activity." ([Alexandra Kollontai, *The Workers' Opposition*, ch. III, early 1921; same translation and URL basis](https://www.marxists.org/archive/kollonta/1921/workers-opposition/ch03.htm)) She was an advocate, and her account of what ordinary workers wanted rests on no survey; the Solidarity editors who reprinted her in English argued that her explanation stopped short when it came to peasant and specialist penetration of the party.

## State capitalism: a step forward, or a new ruling class?

In the spring of 1921 Lenin made the case that state capitalism, meaning state ownership and management of industry with profits taken as state income, could serve a workers' government. The New Economic Policy, or NEP, was the retreat from War Communism that allowed markets and private trade. "State capitalism would be a step forward as compared with the present state of affairs in our Soviet Republic." ([V. I. Lenin, *The Tax in Kind*, completed 21 April 1921, English translation by Yuri Sdobnikov, *Collected Works* vol. 32; the passage is quoted by Lenin from his own 1918 pamphlet](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1921/apr/21.htm)) He named concessions, co-operatives, commission agents and leases as its forms. The pamphlet quoted his own 1918 polemic at length and acknowledged that its timetable had been wrong; it was a justification of a retreat after Kronstadt and the Tenth Congress, not a neutral analysis.

Two later writers read the same institutions from the opposite end. Pannekoek, writing from the defeated German revolution in 1920, attacked the idea that a revolutionary minority could hold power on behalf of a class, a view named Blanquism after the French revolutionary Louis Blanqui: "The doctrine of the revolutionary minority, of the communist party dictatorship (Parteidiktatur), is a sign of the underestimation of the enemy's power, and of the underestimation of the necessary work of propaganda." ([Anton Pannekoek, *The New Blanquism*, *Der Kommunist* no. 27, 1920; translator not named in the online edition](https://www.marxists.org/archive/pannekoe/1920/blanquism.htm)) In exile in 1936 he argued that state ownership, as distinct from state regulation of private capital, described Soviet industry: "In Russia, this form of state capitalism predominates in industry: the work is planned, financed and managed by the state; the directors of industry are appointed by the state and profits are considered the income of the state." ([Anton Pannekoek, *State Capitalism and Dictatorship*, 1936; English in *International Council Correspondence*, January 1937; translator not named in the online edition](https://www.marxists.org/archive/pannekoe/1936/dictatorship.htm)) He denied that state capitalism was a necessary step before the seizure of power, while allowing that it was possible and quite probable as an intermediary stage afterwards and warned that it could appear as "state socialism" after a revolution if workers failed to control production through councils.

Amadeo Bordiga, in a deliberately theatrical polemic against Stalin's 1951 economic treatise, disagreed even with Pannekoek. Commodity production means goods made for sale rather than for direct use, and where commodities and wage labour circulate, he argued, the economy is capitalist: "For us, every system of commodity production in the modern world, a world of associated labour, that is the aggregation of workers in production plants, is defined as capitalist economy." ([Amadeo Bordiga, *Dialogue with Stalin*, *Il Programma Comunista*, October–December 1952, English translation by Libri Incogniti](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bordiga/works/1952/stalin.htm)) He concluded that the Soviet Union had reached neither the first phase of socialism nor even "total state capitalism". The "dialogue" is a literary device, not a transcript of any exchange, and the author says so at the outset.

## What the record cannot establish

These are the writings of party theorists, officials and candidates for office. They establish what was argued and, for Kollontai, how one leader perceived factory life; they do not measure rank-and-file opinion. Her figure of seven million industrial workers is an assertion, not a survey, and no congress minutes, factory reports, police records or workers' letters stand behind these pages, so the distance between the platform and the shop floor cannot be measured. The texts also cannot settle which transition path was feasible. Each was written under civil war, blockade or exile, and each selects facts for a polemic. Kollontai's text was circulated at the Tenth Congress and banned afterwards, but the online record conflicts over its first publication: the Marxists Internet Archive headnote gives *Pravda*, 25 January 1921, while the Solidarity introduction assigns that date and paper to the theses and dates Kollontai's own text to *Workers' Dreadnought* in 1921. A Pannekoek text called "State Capitalism and the Communist Left" is sometimes named in this connection but has not been identified; the verified text is *State Capitalism and Dictatorship*. Almost every text reached readers in English translation. The archive names translators for Luxemburg's two works, Shliapnikov's theses, Lenin's *Tax in Kind* and *"Left-Wing" Communism*, but not for Kollontai, Pannekoek's two articles or Bordiga, so the chain between the Italian original of Bordiga's text and the English quotation is not fully documented. The Russian, German, Dutch and Italian originals were not consulted. The Fourth International's 1951 thesis on state capitalism, Mandel's *The Fallacies of State Capitalism* and Bordiga's later books are pointers rather than read sources here. These texts should be read as arguments with audiences and purposes, not as transparent accounts of how the Soviet economy worked.

## How historians have read it

- **J. P. Nettl, *Rosa Luxemburg* (2 volumes, 1966).** The standard scholarly biography; it places the unfinished 1918 manuscript, its prison conditions and Paul Levi's 1922 publication in context without sharing Luxemburg's political conclusions. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL38690688W)
- **John Gerber, *Anton Pannekoek and the Socialism of Workers' Self-Emancipation, 1873–1960* (1989).** A scholarly study supplying the biography and intellectual setting that the translated articles lack. Broadly sympathetic to council communism, it can be set against the Leninist reading of Pannekoek as a "vulgariser" of Marxism. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL4777533W)
- **Moshe Lewin, *Lenin's Last Struggle* (1968).** Studies Lenin's final political period, including the shift to NEP and the argument over the transition; useful for testing whether the 1921 turn was a retreat or a strategy, and uneasy with accounts that treat *State and Revolution* as his settled position. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3958717W)
- **Barbara C. Allen (ed. and translator), *Workers' Opposition in the Russian Communist Party: Documents, 1919–30* (Brill, 2021; Haymarket Books, 2022).** A documentary collection of the primary material, including the theses translated by Allen on the Marxists Internet Archive; a source edition rather than a single argument. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL25918428W)
- **Ernest Mandel, *The Fallacies of State Capitalism* (1991).** Its title and cataloguing indicate a defence of the trotskyist "degenerated workers' state" position against the state-capitalism thesis; the text itself is a pointer here, not a summary. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL2007876W)

There is broad scholarly agreement on the documentary facts: the texts exist, were written by the named participants at the dates given, and advance incompatible definitions of the transition and the Soviet state. There is live interpretive disagreement over how to characterise that state, whether state capitalism was a necessary stage, and whether party centralism or workers' self-management was the appropriate form. The works listed here are themselves written from within or against the Marxist tradition; professional historiography of the Soviet economy is a separate literature.
