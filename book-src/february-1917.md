On the morning of 23 February 1917, by the calendar Russia still used, women walked out of the textile mills of Petrograd and into the streets over the price and scarcity of bread. Russia's old style Julian calendar ran thirteen days behind the new style Gregorian calendar used in Europe, so that date is 8 March in Western reckoning. The distinction matters, because witnesses and historians switch between the two, sometimes in a single paragraph. Leon Trotsky, writing in 1930 from documents rather than from presence in the city, put the women at the centre of the opening: "A great role is played by women workers in the relationship between workers and soldiers." He had his own argument, that the revolution began from below while the parties lagged behind, and his pages are not neutral. But the women's strike is where the contemporary record starts. Trotsky gives about ninety thousand on strike on 23 February and, by the government's own figures, two hundred and forty thousand by 25 February ([*The History of the Russian Revolution*, ch. 7](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1930/hrr/ch07.htm)). The Petrograd Soviet, an elected council of workers' and soldiers' deputies that formed in the course of the week, later compressed the grievance into one line: "In place of bread, the Tsar's Ministers gave them lead." (Soviet of Workers' Deputies proclamation, 12 March 1917; translated by Frank Golder, 1927. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/february-revolution/february-revolution-texts/formation-of-the-soviets))

## In place of bread

The crowds made the streets unusable. Meriel Buchanan, daughter of the British ambassador, reached Petrograd station on 12 March and found the ordinary life of the city stopped: "There are no cabs," I was told gently. "They have all gone out on strike." (Meriel Buchanan, recalling her arrival at Petrograd station, 12 March 1917; written 1918. [Source](https://archive.org/download/cu31924028403123/cu31924028403123_djvu.txt)) From the embassy she watched armoured lorries full of soldiers, the burning of the Law Courts, the storming of the Military Arsenal, and the surrender of the Fortress and the Admiralty. Others stood closer to the firing. Stinton Jones, a British businessman with an office on the Nevsky Prospect who says he spent twenty of every twenty-four hours in the streets between 10 and 14 March, recorded police volleys, a machine gun, and Cossacks who refused to charge. His explanations are coloured by a conspiracy theory about police agents provoking the crowd; his observations are another matter. "To call those Five Days a bloodless revolution is absurd; but to call it the revolution of restraint would not be very wide of the mark." (Stinton Jones, *Russia in Revolution*, 1917, p. x. [Source](https://archive.org/download/russiainrevolut00jone/russiainrevolut00jone_djvu.txt)) Police fired from rooftops, and the order to fire came from the city commander, Khabalov. How many died on 11 March is not settled by the surviving accounts. What is settled is that the crowd kept coming.

## They would not shoot

The decisive fact of the week was that the garrison would not fire on the people. Sir George Buchanan, the ambassador, set out the sequence in a memoir published in 1923 and based partly on his contemporary dispatches. The Pavlovsky Regiment mutinied when ordered to fire, and the Preobrazhensky and Volynsky regiments followed. "The answer to that question was given on Monday morning, when the soldiers of one of the Guard regiments — the Preobrazhenski — on being ordered to fire, turned and shot their officers." (Sir George Buchanan, *My Mission to Russia*, vol. II, 1923, p. 61. [Source](https://archive.org/download/mymissiontorussi02buch/mymissiontorussi02buch_djvu.txt)) His daughter saw the same thing from a different window: "the soldiers of the Preobrajinsky regiment turned out and shot their officers; while on the other side the Military Arsenal was stormed" (Meriel Buchanan, 12 March 1917. [Source](https://archive.org/download/cu31924028403123/cu31924028403123_djvu.txt)). By midday on 12 March, Sir George Buchanan reckoned that some twenty-five thousand troops had joined the people. N. N. Sukhanov, a Menshevik journalist sitting in the Duma, watched the armed units arrive and noted the absence of the men who should have commanded them: "There were no officers visible at all with the patrols and detachments." (N. N. Sukhanov, 27 February 1917; translated from Russian by Joel Carmichael. [Source](https://dokumen.pub/the-russian-revolution-1917-a-personal-record.html)) Witnesses of very different politics, an ambassador and a socialist, describe the same collapse of military discipline. That convergence is why the tsar lost his throne in days: once the garrison changed sides, he had no force with which to govern.

## A second power

While the shooting continued, two institutions began to claim authority in the same building. The Duma, the imperial parliament, had been prorogued and refused to disperse; its Provisional Committee, led by Rodzianko, sat in the Tauride Palace. A Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies formed in the palace on 27 February and issued a proclamation claiming to speak for the people. Its first act was to appoint commissars to the city wards and to call for local committees and a Constituent Assembly elected by universal, equal, direct and secret suffrage. Its language was deliberately simple: "The fight is still on and must go on to the end. The old power must be completely crushed to make way for popular government." (Soviet of Workers' Deputies proclamation, 12 March 1917; Golder translation. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/february-revolution/february-revolution-texts/formation-of-the-soviets))

The Soviet then did something that reached far beyond a declaration. Order No. 1 of 1 March old style, printed and posted in the city, told soldiers to elect committees in every unit, to hold their weapons in those committees rather than hand them to officers, and to obey Duma military orders only where they did not conflict with Soviet orders. Saluting and the title "Your Excellency" were abolished off duty. "All kinds of arms, such as rifles, machine guns, armored automobiles, and others, must be kept at the disposal and under the control of the company and battalion committees" (Order No. 1, 14 March 1917; translated by Frank Golder, 1927. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/revolution-in-the-army/revolution-in-the-army-texts/order-no-i/)). The order spoke only to the Petrograd garrison, not to the army at the front, and who drafted it is not fully documented; a university editorial note says soldiers pressed for it at Soviet headquarters with Bolshevik help. Sukhanov is clear about what the Duma committee wanted. Its aim was to restore order and save the dynasty, not to lead a revolution, and the Soviet, not the Duma, became the real centre. The two powers were never equal at birth. One commanded the streets and the rifles; the other issued the decrees.

## A new, free Russia

At Pskov, General Ruszki told Nicholas II that it was too late. On 2 March the tsar abdicated, and the manifesto composed around him set out the terms. The war was to be continued to victory, the abdication was made "in agreement with the State Duma," and the inheritance passed not to his son but to his brother, Grand Duke Michael Alexandrovich. "we, in agreement with the State Duma, think it best to abdicate the throne of the Russian State and to lay down the Supreme Power." (Nicholas II, abdication manifesto, 2 March 1917; translated by Frank Golder, 1927. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/february-revolution/february-revolution-texts/abdication-manifesto/)) The document is a stylised act of state, not a record of Nicholas's private reasoning, and its final paragraph still calls for obedience to "the Tsar", so little had the language of monarchy adjusted to events. Both Buchanans report that he had first meant to abdicate in favour of his son and changed this after medical advice about the boy's health, a detail the manifesto omits. Arthur Ransome, a correspondent who was in Petrograd in March 1917 but left before October, watched the mood from close to the Soviet and wrote: "The Tsar, accordingly, left the stage as politely as he could, as painlessly as a person in a play." (Arthur Ransome, "The March Revolution in Russia," 1918. [Source](https://www.marxists.org/history/archive/ransome/1918/truth-russia.htm))

The Provisional Government formed on 3 March under Prince L'vov, with Miliukov, Guchkov and Kerensky among its ministers. Its proclamation promised amnesty, freedom of speech, press, association and strike, an end to social and religious restrictions, a Constituent Assembly, a militia in place of the police, and full civic rights for soldiers off duty. It also promised to carry the war to a victorious end and to honour the Allied alliances. Two clauses conceded what the Soviet had already demanded: no disarming or removal of revolutionary troops from Petrograd, and soldiers' civic rights off duty. "the old order has been overthrown. A new, free Russia is born." (Provisional Committee of the State Duma proclamation, 3 March 1917; Golder translation. [Source](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/february-revolution/february-revolution-texts/formation-of-the-provisional-government/)) This was an assertion made while the fighting was not over, and a programme rather than a record of what was implemented. Sir George Buchanan, an ally of the tsarist state who judged the Duma leaders for failing to rally the troops around what he called the only legally constituted organ, wrote: "So far as Petrograd was concerned, the revolution was already an accomplished fact" (Sir George Buchanan, *My Mission to Russia*, vol. II, 1923, p. 66. [Source](https://archive.org/download/mymissiontorussi02buch/mymissiontorussi02buch_djvu.txt)). That is the puzzle February leaves behind. The respected politicians held the ministries; the Soviet held the rifles. The government was weak from its first morning, not because of the people it recruited but because of the force it did not command, and that is what makes the months to October intelligible.

## Limits of the record

The surviving English-language testimony is uneven and elite. An ambassador, his daughter, a journalist, a businessman and a socialist intellectual all stood near the centre of power, and their accounts of crowds are observations from above. Workers, soldiers and women strikers rarely speak for themselves; where they do, as in the words Trotsky quotes from Kayurov and Yurenev, the primary text has not been independently located. The words of the soldiers who wanted "a good Czar" at the head of a republic, overheard by Meriel Buchanan, are a single anecdote and not evidence of majority opinion. Translation is a further limit. The Golder documents are 1927 English translations; the Yale, Michigan and Marxists Internet Archive versions of Order No. 1 differ in wording; Sukhanov's English text is an abridgement of roughly half the Russian original, with bridging passages supplied by the editor; Trotsky's book reaches English through Max Eastman's 1932 translation. None of these has been checked against a Russian original.

Retrospective distortion varies. Buchanan's book appeared in 1918, Jones's in 1917 and Ransome's in 1918, close to the events but shaped by political purposes. Sir George Buchanan wrote in 1923 partly to defend his own record, Sukhanov in 1919 to 1921 after the Bolsheviks had won, and Trotsky in 1930 after his own defeat. The casualty figures on 11 March cannot be reconciled: Meriel Buchanan writes of nearly a hundred unarmed people shot on the Nevsky, Sir George Buchanan of some two hundred killed that Sunday, and Trotsky cites official figures of about forty killed and as many wounded. None of the three defines its count. Who fired is more contested still. Police fired from rooftops according to two witnesses, while Trotsky reports that a post-revolutionary commission found no machine-gun nests and treats Jones's machine-gun story as a legend. Rodzianko's first telegram to the tsar was sent, but whether it reached him is unclear: Meriel Buchanan states that General Voyeikov stopped it, while Sir George Buchanan says only that Voyeikov misrepresented the situation. No Russian-language archive has been consulted, the newspaper runs and police reports have not been read here, the photographs listed by Jones have not been examined, and no visual claim is made.

## How historians have read it

- **Tsuyoshi Hasegawa, *The February Revolution: Petrograd 1917* (University of Washington Press, 1981).** An archival reconstruction of the streets, the garrison and the institutions hour by hour; the standard detailed study and the one most useful for checking eyewitness chronology.
- **Orlando Figes, *A People's Tragedy: A History of the Russian Revolution* (Viking, 1997).** A broad synthesis arguing that ordinary Russians were protagonists rather than victims, but within "the tyranny of their own history"; a scholarly review disputes his thin treatment of workers and his portrait of Lenin. Review: [otagonists rather than victims, but within "the tyranny of their own history"](https://www.h-net.org/reviews/showrev.php?id=1735)
- **N. N. Sukhanov, *The Russian Revolution 1917: A Personal Record* (Russian 1922–23; English 1955, reissued 1984).** A participant's account prized for its "smell of life". Publisher record: [The Russian Revolution 1917: A Personal Record (Russian 1922–23; English 1955](https://web.archive.org/web/20250211053726/https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691640686/the-russian-revolution-1917)
- **Rex A. Wade, *The Russian Revolution, 1917* (Cambridge University Press, 3rd ed., 2016).** A concise scholarly survey, useful for the debate over whether the collapse was primarily a state crisis or a mass uprising.
- **Steve Smith, *Russia in Revolution: An Empire in Crisis, 1890 to 1928* (Oxford University Press, 2017).** Places February in a long structural crisis of empire, war and social change; broadly agrees with Hasegawa on the importance of the garrison but gives more weight to long-term conditions.
- **Lewis Siegelbaum, subject essay "February Revolution," *Seventeen Moments in Soviet History* (Michigan State University).** A short scholarly framing that agrees on the sequence from Women's Day strikes to abdication and stresses the conditional support the Soviet gave the Provisional Government. [Source: soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/february-revolution/)

Broad agreement: the regime collapsed in days because the garrison refused to shoot and because both the Duma and the Soviet withdrew their support from the tsar. Live disagreement: how much the revolution was a spontaneous popular rising and how much a palace-and-Duma maneuver (Figes and Hasegawa lean toward the former; Stinton Jones's contemporary conspiracy account is the extreme of the latter and is not accepted by modern scholars); how far the moderate socialists could have taken power in February rather than waiting; and how to weigh working-class agency against the war-induced breakdown of the state. These are interpretive disagreements among credible scholars, not disputes about whether the tsar abdicated.
