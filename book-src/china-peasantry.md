In the winter of 1927 Mao Zedong spent thirty-two days travelling through five counties of Hunan, trying to answer critics inside and outside the Communist Party who said the peasant movement had gone too far. He did not rely on official reports. "I called together fact-finding conferences in villages and county towns, which were attended by experienced peasants and by comrades working in the peasant movement" ([Mao Zedong, Hunan investigation, January–February 1927, written up March 1927; translated in *Selected Works*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_2.htm)). What came back was a record of village activity assembled by an investigator who had a case to make, not a survey of every household. Read for its detail rather than its famous phrases, it shows a countryside already in motion.

## The associations and the rent campaign

A peasant association was a village organisation formed to challenge landlord power. Mao recorded that by November 1926 such organisations with a total membership of 1,367,727 had been set up in thirty-seven of Hunan's seventy-five counties, and that by January 1927 the movement claimed roughly two million members and a following of about ten million. He broke the rural population down by class: "According to the survey of Changsha County, the poor peasants comprise 70 per cent, the middle peasants 20 per cent, and the landlords and the rich peasants 10 per cent of the population in the rural areas." ([Mao Zedong, same report, March 1927; *Selected Works* translation](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_2.htm)) Poor peasants held nearly all the chairmanships and committee seats at township level. His list of fourteen achievements describes what associations did: checking landlords' accounts, imposing fines, parading offenders, banning grain exports and rent increases, and pressing for lower rents and deposits. "From November onwards, as the peasants have gained ascendancy over the landlords they have taken the further step of agitating for reduced rents and deposits." ([Mao Zedong, same report, section "Hitting the Landlords Economically," March 1927; *Selected Works* translation](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_2.htm)) The class percentages came from surveys whose method Mao did not give.

Nearly twenty years later, in a village John Hersey called Crow Village in the Shansi-Chahar-Hopei Border Region, the same issue reached the level of individual households. Hersey attended a "Clearing the Accounts" meeting in 1945–46 at which nine landlords were fined and a new village head elected. A flat 25 per cent rent cut was applied with some retroactive claims, alongside a sliding "Reasonable-Burden Tax." Afterward a farmer named Wang described what the campaign had meant in grain: "They gave me back thirty piculs of red and yellow [sorghum and millet] and reduced the rent on my water land from twelve piculs a mow to eight." ([Farmer Wang, Crow Village, interviewed by John Hersey, 1946; recorded in Hersey's English with the help of a Chinese interpreter](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1946/07/27/the-communization-of-crow-village)) A *picul* was a measure of grain weight and a *mou* a measure of land area. The landlord Hsi was less impressed. "I don't feel that things are any better or any worse now than they were under the Japanese. Things are just going along." ([Landlord Hsi, Crow Village, to John Hersey, 1946; rendered in Hersey's English. The following passage in Hersey makes clear that Hsi then described his losses in detail.](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1946/07/27/the-communization-of-crow-village)) He went on to speak of mortgaging 130 of his 300 *mou* and trying side deals to win back the reduction. Hersey found that Hsi and other landlords remained materially comfortable and that the gap between them and poor peasants was narrowing, not gone. He stayed only a few days, and Wang's and Hsi's words reach us in his English through an interpreter.

## Classification and the land-to-the-tiller law

The daily experience of rent reduction came before the confiscation of land. The change is marked in the Draft Agrarian Law of the Communist Party, announced on 28 December 1947 and reproduced by William Hinton. "Article I—The agrarian system of feudal and semi-feudal exploitation is abolished. The agrarian system of 'land-to-the-tiller' is to be realized." ([Draft Agrarian Law of the Chinese Communist Party, announced 28 December 1947, as reproduced by William Hinton in *Fanshen*, prologue; Hinton's English](https://ebin.pub/fanshen-a-documentary-of-revolution-in-a-chinese-village-9780520210400.html)) Hinton watched the law applied in Long Bow in 1948 while attached to a county land-reform work team. He recorded "speak bitterness" meetings, in which villagers denounced landlords, the classification of households by class, and the division of land and property. He also recorded what the team found it had to correct. "At least a dozen people were beaten to death by angry crowds; some hardworking small holders were wrongly dispossessed..." ([William Hinton, preface to *Fanshen*, dated May 1966](https://ebin.pub/fanshen-a-documentary-of-revolution-in-a-chinese-village-9780520210400.html)) Hinton warned that Long Bow was atypical: it had been occupied by Japan, transformed within days in 1945, and reformed during a civil war. He did not speak the local dialect and worked through two interpreters. His admissions of violence and wrongful dispossession carry weight precisely because he was sympathetic to the revolution.

## One residence, one household

On 9 January 1958 the new state fixed the legal architecture of what became known as *hukou*, or household registration. The regulation required registration by household and one permanent residence per citizen, with births and deaths reported and a certificate needed to move. Article 10 set the barrier between countryside and city. "A citizen moving from a rural area to a city must apply to the household registration organ at the place of permanent residence for the move-out formalities with a certificate of employment from an urban labor department, a certificate of admission from a school, or a certificate from an urban household registration organ approving the move-in." ([Article 10, Regulations on Household Registration of the PRC, 9 January 1958; unofficial English translation by Dan Young Business Consultancy](https://www.danyoungcpa.com/regulations-household-registration-prc-1958)) The official Chinese lists the same three alternatives: 公民由农村迁往城市，必须持有城市劳动部门的录用证明，学校的录取证明，或者城市户口登记机关的准予迁入的证明 ([Article 10, official Chinese text, 9 January 1958](https://www.cecc.gov/resources/legal-provisions/regulations-on-household-registration-of-the-peoples-republic-of-china)). The regulation did not yet use the later shorthand of agricultural and non-agricultural registration, and it set no food rations or welfare entitlements. It was a rule on paper, and no source here shows how it touched a named person.

## The communes: canteens and cold rice

Seven months after the registration rules, Mao was in Shantung when a local secretary reported that a county was preparing large collective farms. "It is better to set up people's communes. Their advantage lies in the fact that they combine industry, agriculture, commerce, education and military affairs." ([Mao Zedong, Shantung, 9 August 1958, as reported in *People's Daily*; English from the Maoist Documentation Project transcription](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-8/mswv8_13.htm)) A people's commune was a vast rural unit that joined farming with industry, trade, schools and militia under one administration. The Central Committee resolution of 29 August 1958 called the commune the inevitable trend of the situation, listed canteens, nurseries and "Happy Homes" among its attractions, and said compulsion and rash orders were to be avoided. A year later the official press reported the result. "In less than two months, the mass of the peasants, then organized in more than 700,000 agricultural cooperatives, set up more than 26,000 people's communes." ([*Peking Review* editorial translating *Renmin Ribao*, 29 August 1959](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/peking-review/1959/PR1959-36b.htm)) These were the issuing bodies' claims about speed and enthusiasm, not measurements of village opinion.

Anna Louise Strong visited communes and interviewed members, and her notebooks preserve details the state documents omit. A newly married woman of twenty told her, "They have all kinds of dishes and without any trouble. Nobody likes cooking at home now except on holidays." ([A newly married commune member, twenty years old, to Anna Louise Strong, 1958–59; recorded in Strong's English](https://archive.org/download/the-rise-of-chinese-peoples-communes/Strong-1964-TheRiseOfTheChinesePeople%27sCommunes-andSixYearsAfter-OCR_djvu.txt)) Strong later warned her readers about her own early figures. "I have thought it best to preserve that text practically unchanged, including even some grossly exaggerated statistics and the over-enthusiasms on which they were based." ([Anna Louise Strong, foreword to *The Rise of the Chinese People's Communes — And Six Years After*, Peking, July 1964](https://archive.org/download/the-rise-of-chinese-peoples-communes/Strong-1964-TheRiseOfTheChinesePeople%27sCommunes-andSixYearsAfter-OCR_djvu.txt)) At the centre, coercion was already being named. In December 1958 Mao told the Central Committee, "Cadres make mistakes mostly by practising coercion, telling lies, and making false reports, reporting either more or less than the actual figures." ([Mao Zedong, speech to the Sixth Plenum of the Eighth Central Committee, 19 December 1958; Red Guard compilation, English transcription at Marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-8/mswv8_23.htm)) He discussed grain figures discounted from 860 billion to 730 billion catties, said it had not been verified whether the grain was there, and wondered whether the dining halls would collapse if they served only cold rice. The two kinds of testimony can both be true: a commune could be welcomed in one village and imposed in another.

## What the record cannot hold

The surviving witnesses were rarely disinterested and almost never spoke without a filter. Mao selected what peasants told him; Hinton and Hersey worked through interpreters; Strong was an advocate; official documents were written to defend policy. There is no direct first-person testimony here from a peasant living under *hukou*, no statement from a named landlord facing the 1947 law, no record of a village's own debate about joining a commune, and nothing from women on the land-reform period equivalent to the later oral histories. The record is also male and literate: the voices that reach us belong to leaders, journalists, activists and the few villagers a visitor chose to interview. Necessarily, the evidence says little about the south and west, and its English versions of reports, resolutions and speeches are translations whose precision depends on originals that should be checked. None of these documents explains the later famine, and it would be wrong to make them carry that argument.

## How historians have read it

1. **Chalmers A. Johnson, *Peasant Nationalism and Communist Power: The Emergence of Revolutionary China, 1937–1945* (Stanford University Press, 1962).** Argued from captured Japanese wartime records and a Yugoslav comparison that the invasion, not class redistribution, produced the peasant nationalism the Communists harnessed. [Source: sup.org](https://www.sup.org/books/asian-studies/peasant-nationalism-and-communist-power)
2. **Mark Selden, *The Yenan Way in Revolutionary China* (Harvard University Press, 1971), revised as *China in Revolution: Yenan Way Revisited* (Routledge, 2016).** Countered Johnson with base-area administrative records, arguing that rent and interest reduction, cooperatives and the "three-thirds" system won peasant support before and during the war. [Source: taylorfrancis.com](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.4324/9781315286419/china-revolution-mark-selden)
3. **Suzanne Pepper, *Civil War in China: The Political Struggle, 1945–1949*, 2nd edition (Rowman & Littlefield, 1999).** Shifted attention to the cities and to Nationalist failures in inflation, corruption and handling of students and workers; a necessary corrective to purely rural explanations. [Source: inlibra.com](https://www.inlibra.com/en/document/view/detail/uuid/93a56d7c-36a4-3e9a-9345-575dc8c001f8)
4. **Gail Hershatter, *The Gender of Memory: Rural Women and China's Collective Past* (University of California Press, 2011).** Oral histories with 72 elderly women in four Shaanxi villages; shows how land reform, collectivisation and campaigns were remembered and gendered. [Source: ucpress.edu](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-gender-of-memory/hardcover)
5. **Felix Wemheuer, "Dealing with Responsibility for the Great Leap Famine in the People's Republic of China," *The China Quarterly* 201 (2010), 176–194.** Village interviews in Henan showing that memory of the famine is shaped by local efforts to preserve village harmony, and by the urban–rural divide. [Cambridge University Press: 550C79E7B5FCA3C53398EFC](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/dealing-with-responsibility-for-the-great-leap-famine-in-the-peoples-republic-of-china/550C79E7B5FC769402092A3C53398EFC)
6. **Dali L. Yang, *Calamity and Reform in China: State, Rural Society, and Institutional Change Since the Great Leap Famine* (Stanford University Press, 1996).** Connects the political causes of the famine to the later dismantling of the communes. [Source: sup.org](https://www.sup.org/books/asian-studies/calamity-and-reform-china)

There is broad agreement that the Communists won decisive rural support and that the famine was a human catastrophe rooted in policy as well as weather. There is live disagreement about the weight of nationalism against class policy, about the relative importance of rural and urban factors in 1945–49, and about how uniform peasant experience was. The first-hand sources assembled here support variation rather than settling those arguments.
