William Blum collected evidence of what the United States government did in other countries and argued that the actions served a single imperial purpose. His books reached readers well outside universities, while reviewers in academic journals and in the CIA's own professional publication accepted the scale of his documentation but objected to the way he selected and interpreted it. This profile keeps those two things apart: the operations that can be shown in official records, and Blum's account of why they happened.

## Biography

William Henry Blum was born on 6 March 1933 in Brooklyn and died on 9 December 2018 in Virginia, aged 85. The dates come from the Library of Congress name authority record as it appeared in search results; a direct fetch of that record returned a 403 error. His death date is confirmed on his own website, which posted a farewell saying "Bill died on December 9th, 2018" and reproducing Louis Wolf's account of a fall in his apartment on 4 October 2018, a 65-day fight for life, and kidney failure as the immediate cause. No birth certificate was opened for this research, and details carried in obituaries that were not opened, such as hospital and parentage, were not independently verified ([Primary Official — LC authority record](http://id.loc.gov/authorities/n86117568); [Primary Theoretical — Blum site, farewell](https://williamblum.org/farewell)).

Blum's own account of his political development is brief and consistent. He worked with computers, including for IBM and then for the State Department, and left the State Department in 1967 because he opposed what the United States was doing in Vietnam. He abandoned an ambition to become a Foreign Service officer, then helped found and edit the *Washington Free Press*, described on his site as the first alternative newspaper in the capital. He worked as a freelance journalist in the United States, Europe and South America. A period in [Chile](/chile) in 1972–73, writing about the Allende government's "socialist experiment" and what he called its "tragic overthrow in a CIA-designed coup", made the question of intervention — the use of military, intelligence or political power to change or suppress another country's government — a permanent subject for him. In the mid-1970s he worked in London with the former CIA officer Philip Agee and Agee's associates on projects exposing CIA personnel and operations ([Primary Theoretical — About William Blum](https://williamblum.org/about)).

The memoir *West-Bloc Dissident* gives this period from the inside. Its publisher description says Blum spent four years at IBM and more than two at the State Department before the Vietnam War and the 1960s moved him into anti-war and counter-cultural politics, and that it recounts the underground press, government informers, his life in Chile under Allende, and an unsuccessful Hollywood documentary project with Oliver Stone. The book's opening line, quoted on his author page, places the reader at CIA headquarters in Langley on 4 August 1969, where he began recording employees' licence plates. That is his retrospective account, not an independent record of the events ([Primary Theoretical — West-Bloc Dissident book page](https://williamblum.org/books/west-bloc-dissident)).

From 2002 to 2003 he wrote a regular column for *The Ecologist*. From 2003 until shortly before his death he published the free monthly newsletter *The Anti-Empire Report*; his site lists 160 issues, the last dated 20 September 2018. He also gave talks and radio and television interviews ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about); [Primary Theoretical — Anti-Empire Report #160](https://williamblum.org/aer/read/160)).

## Intellectual tradition

Blum wrote from anti-imperialist journalism rather than from academic history or party theory. He called himself a "historian" on his own site, but his training was in accounting and his method was that of an investigative reporter assembling a documentary record against official denials. In an interview he said plainly, "I'm a socialist", while adding that an American form of socialism would not arrive in his lifetime or his son's. For the foreseeable future he thought the realistic demand was the "highly increased democracy" associated with Ralph Nader ([Primary Theoretical — David Ross interview, *The Bloody Road to Empire*](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html)). He disowned both patriotism and nationalism as attachments to a flag rather than to principles: "I don't love any country. I love certain principles, like human rights and civil liberties and the society which puts people before profits and society which practices genuine democracy" ([Primary Theoretical — Truthout interview, 12 March 2014](https://truthout.org/articles/william-blum-discusses-americas-deadliest-export-democracy)).

He rejected the label "conspiracy theorist" as a dismissive device while insisting that real conspiracies existed. In the Truthout interview his distinction was procedural: "I draw the line where it makes sense, and when I cannot present evidence, I stop." He complained that some of his readers were more conspiracy-minded than he was and saw 9/11 or the "Israeli lobby" behind everything. That position has to be read alongside his claim in the *Killing Hope* introduction that denial of what he called the "American holocaust" was comparable to denial of the Nazi Holocaust. The comparison is his rhetoric, not a settled historical finding, and it is one of the features of his work that academic reviewers found hardest to accept ([Primary Theoretical — *Killing Hope* introduction](https://williamblum.org/chapters/killing-hope/introduction); [Scholarly Secondary — Muravska, *LSE Review of Books*, 2015](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2015/01/21/book-review-killing-hope-us-military-and-cia-interventions-since-world-war-ii-by-william-blum/)).

His affinities lay with Noam Chomsky and Edward Herman's analysis of media and propaganda, with [Michael Parenti](/michael-parenti) on imperialism, with Gabriel Kolko's historical work, and with the dissident intelligence officers Philip Agee and John Stockwell. Adam Jones, reviewing *Rogue State*, placed Blum explicitly in that company, saying he was covering "ground well-trodden by Noam Chomsky, Edward Herman, Gabriel Kolko, Michael Parenti, and legions of independent journalists and activists" ([Scholarly Secondary — Jones, *Millennium*, 2002](http://adamjones.freeservers.com/blum.htm)). The title of his memoir was literal: he was a "West-Bloc dissident", an American who used documents released by his own government against that government's public account of itself.

## Major contributions

Blum's main contribution was compilation. *Killing Hope* assembled country-by-country accounts of United States military and CIA activity from China in the 1940s to the American empire after the Cold War. His site lists 56 chapters and three appendices: a note on how money circulates, a list of instances of United States armed forces abroad from 1798 to 1945, and a list of United States government assassination plots. *Rogue State* reorganised the same material thematically and added a "concise history of United States global interventions, 1945 to the present". On his site he described the earlier book as a "forensic overview" and the later one as a "mini-encyclopedia" ([Primary Theoretical — *Killing Hope* book page](https://williamblum.org/books/killing-hope); [Primary Theoretical — *Rogue State* book page](https://williamblum.org/books/rogue-state)).

His second contribution was a definition of intervention wide enough to cover more than invasions. In the David Ross interview he said he confined himself to "serious interventions": attempts to overthrow governments, whether successful or not; suppression of popular revolutions or movements against very repressive governments; assassinations; serious interference in foreign elections; large-scale manipulation of the media; and subversion of labour unions. He claimed "well over a hundred separate and serious interventions" in "maybe seventy or eighty countries", even though the book had 54 chapters, because some chapters covered several cases. The short list on his site, "Overthrowing other people's governments: The Master List", applies the same definition to 57 dated entries from China in 1949 to Syria in 2012, with an asterisk marking a "successful ouster" and no asterisk marking an attempt. A companion page, "Suppressing revolt and revolution", lists 20 further cases ([Primary Theoretical — Master List](https://williamblum.org/essays/read/overthrowing-other-peoples-governments-the-master-list); [Primary Theoretical — Suppressing revolt and revolution](https://williamblum.org/essays/read/suppressing-revolt-and-revolution)).

The list is not the same thing as the evidence for it. A covert operation is an action carried out in secret by an intelligence service so that the sponsoring government can deny responsibility; the Guatemala paper's discussion of "plausible denial" describes that aim. In Iran the declassified record is explicit. A CIA memorandum of 8 March 1954, "CAMPAIGN TO INSTALL PRO-WESTERN GOVERNMENT IN IRAN", states that the objective was, "Through legal, or quasi-legal, methods to effect the fall of the Mossadeq government", describes the propaganda campaign, the "war of nerves" and the covertly developed military apparatus, records that "The ouster of Mossadeq was successfully accomplished on 19 Aug 1953", and gives a grand total of $5,330,000 ([Primary Official — FRUS 1952–1954, Iran, document 363](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951-54Iran/d363)). In [Guatemala](/guatemala), a CIA position paper of 24 April 1954 on PBSUCCESS lists the psychological, military and paramilitary assets available against the Arbenz government, discusses "plausible denial", and concludes that "The best chance of removing the Arbenz regime is to proceed as energetically as possible along the lines of the present plan" ([Primary Official — FRUS 1952–1954, Guatemala, document 133](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54Guat/d133)).

Those are documented operations. Blum's chapter titles — Iran as "Making it safe for the King of Kings", Guatemala's later period as "A less publicized 'final solution'" — and his claim that the purpose was to prevent a successful alternative to capitalism are his interpretation of why the operations happened ([Primary Theoretical — *Killing Hope* table of contents](https://williamblum.org/books/killing-hope); [Primary Theoretical — David Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html)). The interpretation is arguable; the operations are not in doubt. Other entries on the Master List, particularly "attempts" such as East Germany in the 1950s, France in 1965 or Fiji in 1987, are presented on that page without any supporting citation, and they were not verified against official records in this research.

## Analysis of capitalism

Blum's account of capitalism is an account of United States foreign policy as its enforcement arm. In the *Rogue State* introduction he reduced the "engine of American foreign policy" to three imperatives: "the care and feeding of American corporations", the prevention of "the rise of any society that might serve as a successful example of an alternative to the capitalist model", and the expansion of American political, economic and military hegemony. He argued that when moral language coincides with those ends, officials "walk the high moral ground", but that it rarely does ([Primary Theoretical — *Rogue State* introduction](https://williamblum.org/chapters/rogue-state/rogue-state-introduction)). The essay "What do the Imperial Mafia really want?" breaks the same claim into motives for the 2003 invasion of Iraq: strategic expansion, ideological missions, oil, privatisation through the IMF, World Bank and WTO, profits for arms manufacturers, and the interests of supporters of Israel. These are listed as causes that "make the hearts of the imperial mafia beat faster", not as a formal model of capitalist economics ([Primary Theoretical — What do the Imperial Mafia really want?](https://williamblum.org/essays/read/what-do-the-imperial-mafia-really-want)).

Domestically he saw the same class interest operating through institutions. He told David Ross that the personnel filters of the State Department, Pentagon and newspapers ensured that people who questioned foreign-policy orthodoxy did not reach positions of authority, and that newspapers owned by multinational corporations had an interest in fixed outcomes. In the Truthout interview he described the two parties as one "on the out" and one "on the in", so that their apparent differences were effects of office rather than of principle ([Primary Theoretical — David Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html); [Primary Theoretical — Truthout interview](https://truthout.org/articles/william-blum-discusses-americas-deadliest-export-democracy)).

The table of contents of *America's Deadliest Export* includes a chapter titled "The Problem with Capitalism". That chapter was not opened, so the account above rests on his introductions, essays and interviews rather than on a reading of it ([Primary Theoretical — *America's Deadliest Export* book page](https://williamblum.org/books/americas-deadliest-export)).

## Analysis of socialism

Blum wrote no institutional blueprint for socialism. His defence was negative and historical: he argued that no socialist experiment had been allowed to develop on its own merits. "Virtually every socialist experiment of any significance in the twentieth century", he wrote in a 1997 essay, had been "overthrown, invaded, or bombed … corrupted, perverted, or subverted … sanctioned, embargoed, or destabilized … or otherwise had life made impossible for it, by the United States" ([Primary Theoretical — Will humans ever fly?](https://williamblum.org/essays/read/will-humans-ever-fly-smashing-socialism-in-the-20th-century)). The same essay used the Wright brothers analogy: if sabotage had grounded every early flight, people would have concluded that humans could never fly. He pointed to socialist and former communist parties winning elections across Eastern Europe and elsewhere in the 1990s as evidence that socialism had not died of its own failure.

In the Ross interview he went further than many Western leftists in rejecting Chomsky's view that the Soviet Union had ceased to be socialist. Blum's position was that "there hasn't been a real good example because the U.S. government and some of its allies have made sure there hasn't"; every significant attempt, including the Soviet Union and Cuba, had been subjected to invasion, destabilisation or hostile isolation. That makes him a defender of actually existing socialist experiments against external destruction rather than a theorist of their internal organisation ([Primary Theoretical — Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html)). Asked what alternative model activists should aim for, he answered in stages: he personally wanted an American socialism, but thought increased democracy was the achievable task in the near future. No detailed economic programme for such a socialism appears in the sources opened here.

## Recommended reading

These items are starting points into Blum's argument, not a measure of his whole output; the full bibliography stands above.

- *Killing Hope: U.S. Military and CIA Interventions Since World War II* (1995) — This is the best starting point for his main contribution, the country-by-country compilation of United States military and CIA activity that he described as a "forensic overview".
- *Rogue State: A Guide to the World's Only Superpower* (2000) — It is the clearest place to find his three-part account of what drives American foreign policy: the care of American corporations, the prevention of a successful alternative to capitalism, and the expansion of American political, economic and military hegemony.
- "Overthrowing other people's governments: The Master List" (2013) — This is the best short entry to his broad definition of intervention, since it applies the same categories to attempts as well as successful ousters and makes clear what he counted.
- "Will humans ever fly? Smashing socialism in the 20th century" (1997) — This is the place to begin with his defence of socialism, which argues that significant socialist experiments were overthrown, invaded, embargoed or destabilised and so were never allowed to develop on their own merits.

## Intellectual influences

Influence is claimed here only where Blum quotes, cites or praises another writer. The *Killing Hope* introduction opens with a line from Michael Parenti's *The Anti-Communist Impulse* and cites Parenti twice in its notes; it also cites the Cold War historians D. F. Fleming and Frederick Lewis Schuman, quotes Kwame Nkrumah's *Dark Days in Ghana*, draws on John Stockwell's *In Search of Enemies*, and cites Frank Kofsky, Walden Bello, Doug Henwood and *Multinational Monitor*. The same notes identify the *Declassified Documents Reference System* and *Foreign Relations of the United States* as sources he used, describing FRUS as "internal documents published about 25 to 35 years after the fact" ([Primary Theoretical — *Killing Hope* introduction](https://williamblum.org/chapters/killing-hope/introduction)).

Noam Chomsky appears both as a quoted source and as a point of personal agreement. Blum's site reproduces Chomsky's judgement that *Killing Hope* was "far and away the best book on the topic"; in the Ross interview Blum said Chomsky's idea of the "threat of a good example" matched his own reading of Vietnam, Cuba and Chile. Philip Agee and John Stockwell mattered in two ways: as collaborators and sources of inside information about CIA practice, and as authors on whom the CIA reviewer says Blum relied heavily ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about); [Primary Official — CIA review](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Intel-Officers-Bookshelf-59.3.pdf)). Parenti's influence is visible again in *Rogue State*, where Blum quotes his argument that public bewilderment at foreign policy does not mean policymakers are themselves fools ([Primary Theoretical — *Rogue State* introduction](https://williamblum.org/chapters/rogue-state/rogue-state-introduction)).

## Influence and legacy

Blum's books reached readers well outside academia. His site says they were translated into more than 15 languages, and its pages list editions in Swedish, French, Italian, Korean, Arabic, Spanish, German, Russian, Iranian, Chinese, Finnish, Greek, Dutch, Polish, Japanese, Turkish, Malayalam, Bulgarian and Portuguese translations for individual titles ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about); [Primary Theoretical — *Killing Hope* book page](https://williamblum.org/books/killing-hope); [Primary Theoretical — *Rogue State* book page](https://williamblum.org/books/rogue-state)). In 1999 he received a Project Censored award for "exemplary journalism" for an article on United States assistance to Iraq's chemical and biological weapons capability, according to his own site ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about)).

In January 2006 an audiotape attributed to Osama bin Laden said it "would be useful" for Americans to read *Rogue State*. Blum's site records this and the surge of attention that followed; interviewers returned to it repeatedly, and the *New York Times* obituary headline led with it. Blum told Truthout that the attention brought interviews with outlets that otherwise ignored him, including Tucker Carlson, and he remained careful to say that he deplored the 11 September attacks ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about); [Primary Theoretical — Truthout interview](https://truthout.org/articles/william-blum-discusses-americas-deadliest-export-democracy)). The reporting of the bin Laden quote also appeared in contemporary journalism that was seen only as search-result text and is not used here as evidence.

His "Master List" has had a second life after his death, circulating on activist and alternative sites as a compact reference for regime change, including a republication in 2019 that added Ukraine 2014 in an editorial note; that endpoint was seen in search results but not opened. *Killing Hope* and *Rogue State* were reissued by Bloomsbury Academic in 2022, a measure of continuing demand, though the publisher pages could not be opened directly ([Primary Official — publisher pages, content seen in search results, direct fetch returned 403](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/killing-hope-9781350348196)). Julia Muravska's 2015 review observed that after the 1995 revision the books largely passed under the radar of mainstream academia while remaining popular on the left and among what she called "conspiracy theorists and fringe commentators" ([Scholarly Secondary — Muravska](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2015/01/21/book-review-killing-hope-us-military-and-cia-interventions-since-world-war-ii-by-william-blum/)). That describes his reach; it is not a verdict on the evidence.

## Criticisms and controversies

The most specific factual objection was made in the CIA's own professional journal. Hayden Peake, reviewing the 2014 edition of *Killing Hope* in *Studies in Intelligence* 59(3) of September 2015, recorded that the 1986 first edition contained 49 cases and the revised edition 56, and said the operations discussed were "all well-known covert actions, viewed from the left". He acknowledged that Blum's judgements were open to debate but identified one flat error: "in the case of Operation Splinter Factor and the defection of Polish intelligence officer Jozef Swiatło, he is just wrong: there never was an Operation Splinter Factor." Peake also noted that the book is "documented with sources that support Blum's position, including several books by former CIA officers John Stockwell and Philip Agee" — a criticism of source selection rather than of citation practice. The journal carries a standard disclaimer that the views are the reviewer's own and not official United States government positions. The PDF was opened and its text extracted for this research ([Primary Official — CIA, *Studies in Intelligence* 59(3), 2015](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Intel-Officers-Bookshelf-59.3.pdf)).

Julia Muravska's review in *LSE Review of Books* of 21 January 2015 accepted that the cases were "heavily and meticulously footnoted", drawing on contemporary media, declassified archives and, at page 423, field research that produced "personal experience" and "observations". Her objection was to analysis. She described the post-Cold War chapter as reading "more like a series of hastily penned notes than academic discourse or historical narrative", containing "unsubstantiated claims and colloquialisms" with little transition or argument. She criticised what she saw as a patronising account of post-communist Eastern Europeans, an unquestioning treatment of NATO bombing in the Balkans, reliance on the Russian state-funded channel RT for the Ukraine crisis, and a failure to place the Cold War narrative in the context of superpower rivalry. She concluded that "academic readers may not be satisfied", and recommended William Keylor's *A World of Nations* and Robert Litwak's *Regime Change* as more rigorous alternatives; those books were not opened ([Scholarly Secondary — Muravska](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2015/01/21/book-review-killing-hope-us-military-and-cia-interventions-since-world-war-ii-by-william-blum/)).

Adam Jones's review of *Rogue State* in *Millennium: Journal of International Studies* (2002) made a related methodological point from a sympathetic position. He praised the "cumulative power" of the documentation, called the book "a useful reference work" and "an informative if polemical read", and quoted Blum's argument about a shared "class consciousness" between Washington and its unsavoury allies as "to-the-point and convincing". But he found the didactic structure at times "bordering on a clip-job", wrote that Blum showed a "relentless desire to interpret every action of the U.S. government in the worst possible light", and said this sometimes produced "leaden prose". His example of overreach was a chapter that included instances of "political correctness" alongside torture and drug trafficking: "Blum tosses everything but the kitchen sink into his critique" ([Scholarly Secondary — Jones](http://adamjones.freeservers.com/blum.htm)).

Two further controversies are recorded rather than adjudicated. First, Blum's comparison of United States interventions to the Holocaust, made in the 1995 introduction and in the Ross interview, is a moral analogy he used to argue that American crimes are systematically denied; it is not supported here by a demographic comparison, and no opened scholarly study accepting it was found. Second, the concise intervention lists on his website are themselves a source of interpretive risk. Chapter 17 of *Rogue State* calls itself "the most extensive compilation ever of serious post-World War II American interventions", says it covers many more cases than *Killing Hope*, and directs readers elsewhere "for sources not indicated below" ([Primary Theoretical — *Rogue State*, 2002 text, PDF opened and text extracted](https://www.cia.gov/library/abbottabad-compound/F5/F596A5C4611298BA662A56CDB4548E35_Blum_William_Rogue_State.pdf)). That is an explicit admission that the short entries are not individually sourced on the page; the same is true of the Master List. A reader using Blum as a source must therefore separate a documented act, such as the coup in Iran or the PBSUCCESS plan in Guatemala, from an entry in a list that asserts an attempt without citation, and from the political claim that all such acts served one imperial purpose.

## Related thinkers

The nearest connections are to Noam Chomsky and Edward Herman, whose propaganda critique Blum adopted and whose work he praised, and to [Michael Parenti](/michael-parenti), whom he quoted and whose account of American imperialism he shared. Philip Agee and John Stockwell supplied disclosures about CIA practice and, in Agee's case, worked alongside him in London. Gabriel Kolko is named by Adam Jones as another writer covering the same ground. Kwame Nkrumah appears in Blum's introduction as a source on non-alignment, and Ralph Nader as the figure whose "highly increased democracy" Blum proposed as an achievable near-term goal. These are connections of agreement and influence, not a shared organisation or school. No published profiles of the other thinkers named here exist in this repository's research records, so they are named in plain text rather than linked.
