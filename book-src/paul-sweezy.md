Paul Sweezy was a Marxist economist and, for more than half a century, the editor of *Monthly Review*. Two books carry his reputation. *The Theory of Capitalist Development* (1942) introduced English-speaking readers to the technical core of Marx's economics, including the argument about how values become prices. *Monopoly Capital* (1966), written with Paul A. Baran, moved the question to monopoly capital: an economy in which a few large corporations dominate their markets and administer prices instead of competing them down. Its central claim was that such a system generates a growing surplus, meaning the income left above wages and other costs of production, which ends up as profit and other returns to property, while at the same time becoming prone to stagnation. Sweezy defended that argument in print and in occasional teaching for decades afterwards. This profile sets out what he argued, what the opened records show, and where critics from several directions objected.

## Biography

Paul Marlor Sweezy was born in New York on 10 April 1910 and died at his home in Larchmont, New York, on 27 February 2004, aged 93; the *Monthly Review* memorial gives congestive heart failure as the cause ([Monthly Review memorial, 2004](https://monthlyreviewarchives.org/mr/article/view/MR-055-11-2004-04_6)). Some newspaper obituaries imply 28 February, and the discrepancy was not resolved here. His father, Everett B. Sweezy, was a vice-president of the First National Bank of New York; the family wealth later gave him the freedom to leave academia ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/); [*Monthly Review*, *The Editors*](https://monthlyreview.org/about/the-editors/)).

He attended Phillips Exeter Academy and Harvard. The *Monthly Review* record of Christopher Phelps's 1999 interview says he graduated in 1931; Foster says his father's death in 1931 interrupted his senior year and that he graduated magna cum laude in 1932. The two accounts agree that he began graduate work at Harvard and spent 1932–33 at the London School of Economics, where the Depression, the rise of fascism and radical students pushed him towards Marxism ([Phelps interview record](https://monthlyreviewarchives.org/mr/article/view/MR-051-01-1999-05_2); [Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)). He later described the Marxism he brought back to Harvard as that of a "convinced but very ignorant" person, in words quoted by *Monthly Review* ([*The Editors*](https://monthlyreview.org/about/the-editors/)). His doctorate came in 1937, and the dissertation appeared in 1938 as *Monopoly and Competition in the English Coal Trade, 1550–1850*, published by Harvard University Press as Harvard Economic Studies volume 63 ([Niigata University catalogue](http://opac.lib.niigata-u.ac.jp/en/recordID/catalog.bib/BA12903237)). The Phelps interview abstract gives the title as "1550–1800", which conflicts with every catalogue record examined and appears to be an error in that abstract.

Sweezy taught economics at Harvard from 1938, took over a course on the economics of socialism, and wrote *The Theory of Capitalist Development* out of those lectures. In 1942 he went on leave to the Office of Strategic Services, where he edited the classified *European Political Report*. After the war he resigned from Harvard, expecting to be denied tenure, and declined an academic career ([*The Editors*](https://monthlyreview.org/about/the-editors/); [Phelps interview record](https://monthlyreviewarchives.org/mr/article/view/MR-051-01-1999-05_2)). Heilbroner's contemporary review says he was "released from Harvard in 1942", a different framing that was not reconciled ([Heilbroner, 1966](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1966/05/26/a-marxist-america/)). He worked in Henry Wallace's 1948 Progressive Party campaign and headed it in New Hampshire. In 1949 he and Leo Huberman launched *Monthly Review: An Independent Socialist Magazine*, its first issue carrying Albert Einstein's "Why Socialism?". Foster records about 2,500 subscribers after the first year and a peak circulation near 12,000 in 1977; these are figures reported in a secondary essay, not measurements checked against a dataset here ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)).

During the McCarthy period Sweezy refused to surrender his lecture notes or name political associates to a New Hampshire attorney general, was jailed for contempt, and won in the United States Supreme Court, *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, on 17 June 1957; the *Monthly Review* editors' page describes the decision as 5–4 ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/); [*The Editors*](https://monthlyreview.org/about/the-editors/)). He later taught occasionally as a visiting professor at Cornell, Stanford, the New School, Yale and Manchester, received an honorary doctorate from Jawaharlal Nehru University in 1983 and the Veblen-Commons Award in 1999 ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)).

## Intellectual tradition

Sweezy belonged to the Marxian tradition of political economy and to no party. He was a Marxist in the theoretical sense: he worked with value, surplus value, accumulation and class, and treated Marx's categories as tools for investigating capitalism rather than as doctrine. *Monthly Review* under Huberman and Sweezy kept an "independent" line. A 1963 *Business Week* portrait quoted by Foster described it as "a brand of socialism that is thorough-going and tough-minded" which maintained "a sturdy independence of both Moscow and Peking" ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)). He did not call the Soviet Union socialist without qualification. In the 1986 interview with Sungur Savran and E. Ahmet Tonak he said of the Soviet Union and China that they had achieved "a certain relative if tenuous and insecure independence of capitalism", that "the Soviet Union is not operating under the laws of capitalism", and that "I don't think it's very useful to call them socialist" ([interview reprint](https://www.counterpunch.org/2024/02/23/an-interview-with-paul-sweezy/), read as indexed text only). Nigel Harris, writing from a revolutionary-socialist position, attacked precisely this readiness to accept those states as socialism ([Harris, 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/writers/harris/1967/xx/moncap.htm)).

His closest intellectual relationship was with Joseph Schumpeter, the conservative economist who mentored him at Harvard and campaigned for his tenure. *Monthly Review* describes a lifelong friendship punctuated by legendary arguments, and quotes Paul Samuelson remembering a debate between Sweezy, the "young sir Galahad", and Schumpeter, the "foxy Merlin" ([*The Editors*](https://monthlyreview.org/about/the-editors/)). That remark is a recollection of a Sweezy–Schumpeter exchange, not a debate between Sweezy and Samuelson; no direct published Sweezy–Samuelson debate was located. Sweezy also drew on Keynes. In the 1986 interview he said he "was very much influenced, as I think was my whole generation, by Keynes, by the General Theory", while insisting that Keynes lacked a theory of the state and of power relations ([1986 interview PDF](https://www.devrimcimarksizm.net/sites/dm/files/sungur_savran_and_e._ahmet_tonak_an_interview_with_paul_sweezy.pdf), read as indexed text only). What later came to be called the "Monthly Review school" of monopoly-capital theory took shape around him, Baran, Magdoff and later John Bellamy Foster ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)).

## Major contributions

The first was a work of exposition that became a theoretical intervention. *The Theory of Capitalist Development* was written to fill the absence of "any reasonably comprehensive analytical study of Marxian Political Economy" in English, as Sweezy said in its preface. It set out the labour theory of value, surplus value, crises, monopoly and fascism, and gave English-speaking readers Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz's solution of the transformation problem — how the values derived from labour time in Volume I of *Capital* are turned into the prices of production that appear in Volume III — together with an appendix by Shigeto Tsuru comparing the reproduction schemes of Quesnay, Marx and Keynes ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/); [Samuelson, 1971](https://cooperative-individualism.org/samuelson-paul_understanding-the-marxian-notion-of-exploitation-1971-jun.pdf), read as indexed text only). Samuelson called Sweezy's chapter VII "the classical reference" and noted that Sweezy edited and introduced the 1949 volume containing Böhm-Bawerk, Hilferding and Bortkiewicz, making the key texts available in English. A title sometimes attached to the 1942 book, *An Introduction to the Theory of Capitalist Development*, is not a separate work; the verified title is *The Theory of Capitalist Development: Principles of Marxian Political Economy* ([Monthly Review Press](https://monthlyreview.org/9780853450795/)).

The second contribution was the "kinked demand curve" of oligopoly. In "Demand Under Conditions of Oligopoly" (*Journal of Political Economy*, August 1939) Sweezy argued that a firm facing rivals believes it will lose customers if it raises price but will not gain many by cutting price, because rivals will retaliate; its "imagined demand curve" therefore has a corner at the current price. He drew out implications for wages and strikes: a successful wage increase might leave price and output unchanged ([Sweezy, 1939](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/255420), article text seen through the journal record's indexed preview). Foster treats this article as a direct ancestor of the later theory of monopoly capital ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)).

The third was *Monopoly Capital: An Essay on the American Economic and Social Order*, written with **Paul A. Baran** and published by Monthly Review Press in 1966, two years after Baran's death. It was not co-authored with Harry Magdoff: Magdoff was Sweezy's co-editor at *Monthly Review* and his co-author on later books, but not on this one ([Monthly Review Press](https://monthlyreview.org/9780853450733/); [Trinity College catalogue](https://lib-cat.trin.cam.ac.uk/Record/95178d81-35ff-472f-beab-2b41b5609180)). The book moved the centre of gravity from competitive capitalism to a system of giant corporations able to administer prices. Its central claim was that in monopoly capitalism Marx's tendency of the rate of profit to fall had been replaced by a tendency of the economic surplus to rise, and that stagnation, not growth, was the normal condition to be explained ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)). The publisher describes it as the first serious attempt to extend Marx's model of competitive capitalism to monopoly conditions, quoting Howard J. Sherman in the *American Economic Review* ([Monthly Review Press](https://monthlyreview.org/9780853450733/)). The long collaboration is documented in the Baran–Sweezy correspondence archive and in the selected letters published as *The Age of Monopoly Capital* in 2017 ([Baran-Sweezy Archives](https://monthlyreviewarchives.org/index.php/baran-sweezy/); [Monthly Review Press](https://monthlyreview.org/9781583676523/the-age-of-monopoly-capital)).

The fourth contribution was institutional: editing *Monthly Review* from its founding in 1949 until his death, and building Monthly Review Press as an independent socialist publisher. Foster records that the magazine never depended on a party line and became the longest-running socialist periodical in the English-speaking world ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)).

## Analysis of capitalism

Sweezy's account of capitalism began from production and value. In *The Theory of Capitalist Development* he treated value as a social relation expressed in exchange, surplus value as the source of profit, and the transformation of values into prices of production as a technical problem that had to be solved if Marx's Volume I and Volume III were to be reconciled. He gave prominence to the "realisation" problem: the possibility that commodities cannot be sold, and therefore that surplus value cannot be realised. His chapters on crises distinguished crises arising from disproportionality from those arising from underconsumption, and his chapter on "chronic depression" asked whether underconsumption must ultimately triumph. Maurice Dobb, writing the foreword to the British edition, noted that this emphasis on underconsumption, rather than disproportionality or the falling rate of profit, was likely to provoke discussion ([1970 printing, indexed text only](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/ECON647/Paul%20M.%20Sweezy%20-%20The%20Theory%20of%20Capitalist%20Development-Monthly%20Review%20%281970%29.pdf); [Dobb foreword, indexed text only](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/ECON647/Paul%20M.%20Sweezy%20-%20The%20Theory%20of%20Capitalist%20Development-Monthly%20Review%20%281970%29.pdf)).

In *Monopoly Capital* the mechanism changed. Large corporations did not compete by cutting prices; administered prices and the kinked demand curve meant that shortfalls of demand produced idle capacity rather than lower prices. The surplus grew faster than the outlets available to absorb it. Private investment, luxury consumption and "sales effort" could absorb only part; the rest was taken up by state expenditure, above all military spending, and by imperialism. Chronic stagnation followed, punctuated by wars and major innovations ([Heilbroner, 1966](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1966/05/26/a-marxist-america/); [O'Connor, 1966](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i40/articles/james-o-connor-monopoly-capital); [Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)).

Sweezy and Magdoff carried this analysis into the 1970s and 1980s, arguing that financial expansion was a response to stagnation in the real economy. Their later collections include *The Dynamics of U.S. Capitalism* (1972) and *Stagnation and the Financial Explosion* (1987), along with joint *Monthly Review* articles on the 1981 crisis and the 1987 stock-market crash ([Monthly Review Press, *Dynamics*](https://monthlyreview.org/9780853452256/dynamics-of-u-s-capitalism); [Monthly Review Press, *Stagnation*](https://monthlyreview.org/9780853457152/stagnation-and-the-financial-explosion); [Sweezy, 1981](https://monthlyreviewarchives.org/mr/article/view/MR-033-07-1981-11_1)). In the 1986 interview he used Marx's abbreviated circuit M–M′ to describe finance detached from production, and said that economics lacked a theory integrating financial and productive circuits ([1986 interview PDF](https://www.devrimcimarksizm.net/sites/dm/files/sungur_savran_and_e._ahmet_tonak_an_interview_with_paul_sweezy.pdf), indexed text only).

## Analysis of socialism

Sweezy wrote *Socialism* (McGraw-Hill, 1949) as a short handbook in the Economics Handbook Series, and collected essays on capitalism and socialism in *The Present as History* (Monthly Review Press, 1953) ([Notre Dame catalogue, *Socialism*](https://findit.library.nd.edu/Record/000093134); [HathiTrust catalogue](https://catalog.hathitrust.org/Record/001136377); [Notre Dame catalogue, *The Present as History*](https://findit.library.nd.edu/Record/000076865)). He returned to the question in *Post Revolutionary Society* (1980) and *Four Lectures on Marxism* (1981), the latter described by the publisher as a discussion of the dialectical method, the contradictions of capitalism and the future of Marxism ([Monthly Review Press, *Post Revolutionary Society*](https://monthlyreview.org/9780853455516/post-revolutionary-society); [Monthly Review Press, *Four Lectures*](https://monthlyreview.org/9780853455844/four-lectures-on-marxism)).

There is no detailed blueprint of a socialist economy in these works. His preferred institutions were implied rather than designed: social ownership, planning oriented to human need rather than private profit, and an independent socialist press and educational movement capable of sustaining working-class politics ([*The Editors*](https://monthlyreview.org/about/the-editors/)). He defended the Soviet Union and China as societies that had broken with capitalism and achieved a precarious independence from it, while rejecting the view that they were simply capitalist and refusing to call them socialist without qualification ([1986 interview reprint](https://www.counterpunch.org/2024/02/23/an-interview-with-paul-sweezy/), indexed text only). This position drew criticism from revolutionaries who thought it understated the repressive character of those states ([Harris, 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/writers/harris/1967/xx/moncap.htm)). Heilbroner objected that the book pictured socialism as "the very paradigm of sweet reason" and made claims about militarism and conquest being foreign to Marxist theory that did not survive comparison with Hungary or the Sino-Soviet conflict ([Heilbroner, 1966](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1966/05/26/a-marxist-america/)). Readers who want the historical record behind these arguments should turn to the chapters on what socialism and capitalism actually achieved, rather than treating a theoretical position as a description of institutions.

## Recommended reading

These selections are starting points, not a complete survey of Sweezy's work.

- *The Theory of Capitalist Development: Principles of Marxian Political Economy* (1942) — Sets out the labour theory of value, surplus value, crises and the transformation of values into prices; the best starting point for the technical core of his Marxian economics.
- "Demand Under Conditions of Oligopoly" (1939) — Argues that a firm facing rivals expects price rises to lose customers and price cuts to be matched, so its demand curve has a corner at the current price; the clearest entry into the idea that later became his theory of monopoly capital.
- *Monopoly Capital: An Essay on the American Economic and Social Order* (1966) — Moves the analysis to giant corporations with administered prices, where a rising surplus and chronic stagnation replace the falling rate of profit; the best starting point for his central argument.
- *Socialism* (1949) — His short handbook on socialism, which leaves a detailed blueprint aside; the clearest entry into the institutions he implied rather than designed, namely social ownership, planning for human need and an independent socialist press.
- *Stagnation and the Financial Explosion* (1987) — Carries the monopoly-capital argument into finance, treating financial expansion as a response to stagnation in the real economy; the best starting point for his later account of M–M′ and finance detached from production.

## Intellectual influences

Marx supplied the core categories: value, surplus value, accumulation, class and the critique of political economy. Schumpeter supplied an intellectual model and a personal challenge; Sweezy later wrote that his book owed much to Schumpeter "including its title", an echo of *The Theory of Economic Development* ([1970 printing, indexed text only](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/ECON647/Paul%20M.%20Sweezy%20-%20The%20Theory%20of%20Capitalist%20Development-Monthly%20Review%20%281970%29.pdf)). Within Marxism, the 1942 book works through Bernstein, Kautsky, Tugan-Baranowsky, Conrad Schmidt, Boudin, Luxemburg and Grossmann in its "breakdown controversy" chapter, and takes Bortkiewicz's correction of Marx's transformation as the starting point for its own treatment ([1970 printing, indexed text only](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/ECON647/Paul%20M.%20Sweezy%20-%20The%20Theory%20of%20Capitalist%20Development-Monthly%20Review%20%281970%29.pdf)).

Keynes mattered, especially *The General Theory* and its chapter on long-run expectations, though Sweezy thought Keynes had no theory of the state or of power ([1986 interview PDF](https://www.devrimcimarksizm.net/sites/dm/files/sungur_savran_and_e._ahmet_tonak_an_interview_with_paul_sweezy.pdf), indexed text only). Foster identifies Veblen, Hilferding, Kalecki and Josef Steindl as further sources, with Steindl's *Maturity and Stagnation in American Capitalism* (1952) directly inspiring *Monopoly Capital* ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)). Sweezy himself named Shigeto Tsuru's help with the reproduction-schema appendix and singled out Henryk Szlajfer's work on surplus and surplus value ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/); [1986 interview PDF](https://www.devrimcimarksizm.net/sites/dm/files/sungur_savran_and_e._ahmet_tonak_an_interview_with_paul_sweezy.pdf), indexed text only).

## Influence and legacy

*Monthly Review* was his most durable institutional legacy. Under his editorship it carried Marxian political economy into United States radicalism and gave a platform to writers from Asia, Africa and Latin America; Foster notes editions in Italian, Spanish and Greek and a readership far larger than its circulation ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)). Russell Jacoby's *The Last Intellectuals* (1987), quoted by Foster, grouped Baran, Sweezy, Braverman and Magdoff as a school "in coherency, originality, and boldness" unmatched by other American Marxism. David McLellan, also quoted by Foster, called the American contribution to Marxist theory most original in political economy and singled out Baran and Sweezy ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)). These are judgments by the cited writers.

The influence of *Monopoly Capital* is documented by the journal itself: a 1991 issue carried Sweezy's "Monopoly Capital After Twenty-Five Years", and a 2016 issue carried "Monopoly Capital at the Half-Century Mark", whose abstract calls the book "the single most influential work in Marxian political economy to emerge in the United States" ([1991 issue contents](https://monthlyreviewarchives.org/mr/issue/view/480); [2016 article record](https://monthlyreviewarchives.org/index.php/mr/article/view/MR-068-03-2016-07_1)). Foster and Nicholas Baran's editing of the correspondence, and the online Baran–Sweezy archive, have made the book's formation visible to historians ([Baran-Sweezy Archives](https://monthlyreviewarchives.org/index.php/baran-sweezy/)). The archive page says the online set contains "select correspondence"; a *Monthly Review* search page describes about 800 letters in 30 files, while the 2017 publisher description says roughly 1,000 survive. The counts differ and were not reconciled. Sweezy's 1999 answer to Phelps about whether the world was closer to socialism, "if socialism is ever going to happen, we're nearer to it now than we were then", is quoted by Foster ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)).

## Criticisms and controversies

The transformation problem was the first technical battleground. Samuelson's 1970 note in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* and his 1971 survey in the *Journal of Economic Literature* took Sweezy's 1942 chapter VII as the standard English reference and then argued that the whole procedure was redundant: one could start from physical inputs and a real wage, calculate prices and profit directly, and treat the "transformation" as rubbing out the value relations and writing down price relations instead ([Samuelson, 1970](http://gesd.free.fr/samarx70.pdf); [Samuelson, 1971](https://cooperative-individualism.org/samuelson-paul_understanding-the-marxian-notion-of-exploitation-1971-jun.pdf), both read as indexed text only). This was a critique of the transformation-problem literature in which Sweezy's chapter was central, not a direct Sweezy–Samuelson debate. Meghnad Desai's survey of the debate dates the revival of interest to Sweezy's book and Maurice Dobb's 1943 review, and records that Samuelson's "eraser" argument was followed by an internal critique from Steedman, using Sraffa, that positive surplus value was not a necessary condition for profit ([Desai, 1988](https://www.academia.edu/59040981/The_Transformation_Problem), indexed text only). Sweezy rejected the Sraffian conclusion in the 1986 interview, saying that a theory without value and accumulation theory was "almost total bankruptcy" ([1986 interview PDF](https://www.devrimcimarksizm.net/sites/dm/files/sungur_savran_and_e._ahmet_tonak_an_interview_with_paul_sweezy.pdf), indexed text only). Dobb's foreword had already raised doubts about the "Bortkiewicz corollary" and the description of fascism as initially a middle-class movement ([Dobb foreword, indexed text only](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/ECON647/Paul%20M.%20Sweezy%20-%20The%20Theory%20of%20Capitalist%20Development-Monthly%20Review%20%281970%29.pdf)). A 2015 survey of quantitative Marxist political economy observed that the standard interpretation of the transformation problem itself required an extra "normalisation condition" and was then challenged as redundant, so the technical dispute continued well beyond Sweezy's solution ([2015 survey](http://hdl.handle.net/10419/145414), indexed text only).

*Monopoly Capital* drew criticism from several directions at once. Robert Heilbroner's review in the *New York Review of Books* (26 May 1966) judged the moral indictment selective and poorly checked, disputed the definition of "surplus" and the claim that corporate profits tend to rise, and argued that the book treated the capitalist oligarchy as a single will, unable to explain welfare reforms in Sweden, Norway, Germany or Britain ([Heilbroner, 1966](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1966/05/26/a-marxist-america/)). Sweezy replied in the same journal on 7 July 1966, with a further reply by Heilbroner; the exchange was seen only as a record, and its text was not opened ([reply record](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1966/07/07/monopoly-capital/)). Nigel Harris, in *International Socialism* in Autumn 1967, argued that by removing competition and class struggle the book removed the driving mechanism of capitalism; he called the concept of "surplus" inconsistently defined, criticised the declared neglect of the labour process and the claim that the industrial proletariat no longer offered a revolutionary force, and attacked its reliance on the Third World as an external saviour ([Harris, 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/writers/harris/1967/xx/moncap.htm)). James O'Connor's review in *New Left Review* I/40 (November–December 1966) welcomed the book's ambition but identified a clash between Marxist method and techniques borrowed from orthodoxy; only the opening part of this review was readable without a subscription ([O'Connor, 1966](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i40/articles/james-o-connor-monopoly-capital)). Sweezy later conceded in the 1986 interview that he and Baran might have made a mistake in writing the book in Keynesian and neoclassical language, and that two planned chapters explaining its relation to Marxian value analysis were left unfinished when Baran died ([1986 interview reprint](https://www.counterpunch.org/2024/02/23/an-interview-with-paul-sweezy/), indexed text only).

His political controversies were public. The New Hampshire contempt case and Supreme Court reversal are described above. The 1986 interview records criticism that *Monthly Review* gave insufficient attention to working-class struggles in France, Italy, Britain and Portugal in the early 1970s, to which Sweezy replied that traditional Marxism had been over-optimistic about working-class integration and fragmentation ([1986 interview reprint](https://www.counterpunch.org/2024/02/23/an-interview-with-paul-sweezy/), indexed text only). Two errors in sources used here should be carried forward as warnings: the *Monthly Review* editors' page says in one biography that Sweezy died in 1994, and the Phelps interview abstract gives his dissertation title as "1550–1800" ([*The Editors*](https://monthlyreview.org/about/the-editors/); [Phelps interview record](https://monthlyreviewarchives.org/mr/article/view/MR-051-01-1999-05_2)). Both conflict with other records opened for this profile.

## Related thinkers

Karl Marx supplied the value, surplus-value and accumulation framework that Sweezy expounded and revised ([*The Theory of Capitalist Development*, publisher record](https://monthlyreview.org/9780853450795/)). Joseph Schumpeter was mentor, friend and intellectual antagonist, and the title of the 1942 book echoes his *Theory of Economic Development* ([*The Editors*](https://monthlyreview.org/about/the-editors/)). Paul A. Baran was the co-author of *Monopoly Capital* and a correspondent for fifteen years; Harry Magdoff was *Monthly Review* co-editor from 1969 and co-author of the later stagnation and financialisation volumes, not of *Monopoly Capital* ([Monthly Review Press](https://monthlyreview.org/9780853450733/); [*The Editors*](https://monthlyreview.org/about/the-editors/)). Leo Huberman co-founded and co-edited the magazine and co-wrote *The Theory of U.S. Foreign Policy* with Sweezy ([*The Editors*](https://monthlyreview.org/about/the-editors/)). John Bellamy Foster is his biographer, editor and continuator of monopoly-capital theory ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)).

Paul Samuelson's 1970 and 1971 papers criticised the transformation literature in which Sweezy's chapter was central; as noted above, no direct published Sweezy–Samuelson debate was found ([Samuelson, 1971](https://cooperative-individualism.org/samuelson-paul_understanding-the-marxian-notion-of-exploitation-1971-jun.pdf), indexed text only). Maurice Dobb, Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz and Ian Steedman mark the chain from endorsement through correction to rejection within the transformation debate ([Samuelson, 1971](https://cooperative-individualism.org/samuelson-paul_understanding-the-marxian-notion-of-exploitation-1971-jun.pdf), indexed text only). Michał Kalecki and Josef Steindl were precursors of the stagnation argument ([Foster, 2004](https://johnbellamyfoster.org/articles/other-major-scholarly-journal-articles/the-commitment-of-an-intellectual/)). Robert Heilbroner, Nigel Harris and James O'Connor were contemporary critics, writing from liberal, revolutionary-socialist and New Left positions respectively ([Heilbroner, 1966](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1966/05/26/a-marxist-america/); [Harris, 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/writers/harris/1967/xx/moncap.htm); [O'Connor, 1966](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i40/articles/james-o-connor-monopoly-capital)).
