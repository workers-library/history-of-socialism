In a Soviet archival file there is a telegram sent to Mikhail Kalinin, the nominal head of state, by an army medical officer in Khabarovsk named Anisimov. "Because [of] pregnancy Anisimova left family[.] Request decision terminate pregnancyU restoring family [of] four people [to] normal life[.]" (Anisimov to Kalinin, telegram, GARF f. 7523, op. 23, d. 209, l. 60; OCR artefacts retained, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/abolition-of-legal-abortion/abolition-of-legal-abortion-texts/request-for-an-abortion/)). The page gives no date. Anisimova does not speak in the telegram; her husband presents her pregnancy as an administrative fault to be corrected by the leadership. The message belongs to 1936, the year abortion was criminalised across the Soviet Union. It is a small document, and it sits at the end of a long argument about whether the family was a private matter or a public task.

## A question without a name

Socialists had argued about the position of women since the nineteenth century. In 1909 a young Russian Marxist named Alexandra Kollontai, then a Menshevik, wrote that "The followers of historical materialism reject the existence of a special woman question separate from the general social question of our day. Specific economic factors were behind the subordination of women; natural qualities have been a secondary factor in this process." (Alexandra Kollontai, 1909; trans. Alix Holt, [marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/archive/kollonta/1909/social-basis.htm)). By the "woman question" she meant the problem of why women were subordinate and how that might end. She refused to treat it as separate from the question of class, and she attacked bourgeois feminists for seeking legal reform while leaving property relations untouched. The essay was written eight years before the revolution, so it is a statement of a method, not a description of Soviet conditions.

Kollontai's point was that changing the law did not move the work. In 1920, by then a Bolshevik and a government minister, she wrote: "Capitalism has placed a crushing burden on woman's shoulders: it has made her a wage-worker without having reduced her cares as housekeeper or mother. Woman staggers beneath the weight of this triple load." (Alexandra Kollontai, *Communism and the Family*, 1920; trans. Alix Holt, [marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/archive/kollonta/1920/communism-family.htm)). This is the "double burden" in its original form, the combined load of paid employment, housekeeping and childrearing. Kollontai's claim was that the burden could not be shifted onto individual women, and that the state, rather than the husband, had to carry it.

## What the state promised

Within months of the seizure of power the new regime tried to make that analysis law. The 1918 Family Code of the Russian Soviet Federated Socialist Republic abolished community of property between spouses: "Marriage does not establish community of property." (Original Family Law of the RSFSR, 17 October 1918, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/new-woman/new-woman-texts/code-of-laws-concerning-the-civil-registration-of-deaths-births-and-marriages)). Either spouse could petition for divorce, children born outside marriage were placed on the same footing as others, and a mother could name a father and ask a court to make him share the costs. The purpose was to strip the household of the economic functions that, in the Marxist view, held it together.

At the first congress of the Communist International in March 1919, Kollontai introduced a resolution declaring: "The dictatorship of the proletariat can be realized and kept up only through the active and energetic participation of the women of the working class." (Comintern resolution introduced by Kollontai, 6 March 1919, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/new-woman/new-woman-texts/resolution-on-the-role-of-working-women/)). It treated the reform of the family and the socialist rearing of children as central tasks, and asserted that women produced no less than half of all existing values, a figure offered without a stated method. Kollontai put the domestic vision more concretely to an American visitor. "We must build a new society in which women are not expected to drudge all day in kitchens. We must have, in Russia, community restaurants, central kitchens, central laundries — institutions which leave the working woman free to devote her evenings to instructive reading or recreation." (Kollontai at a women's congress, c. 1922, as quoted by Louise Bryant, 1923, [marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bryant/works/1923-mom/kollontai.htm)). Bryant, an American suffragist reporting from Moscow, also recorded the contemporary charge that Kollontai's marriage had cost her a government post. Her portrait is admiring in places and condescending to peasant women in others, and her quoted speech is recollection rather than transcript. It is useful precisely because it does not simply repeat the official story.

## Work and its discontents

The party built a department for work among women, known by its Russian abbreviation as the Zhenotdel, to carry the programme into factories and villages. Maria Fedotovna Filipenko, a domestic servant turned factory worker, became a delegate to its Moscow branch. Her 1924 autobiography is the most valuable first-hand voice in the record. Before the revolution she had left her eighteen-month-old daughter locked in a room, tying the girl to a table leg to keep her from a younger child. When the nursery opened she later wrote: "I was invited to bring the children to the nursery, and I was as afraid as if they were going to take them from me forever." (Maria Fedotovna Filipenko, 1924, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/new-woman/new-woman-texts/my-life-the-story-of-maria-fedotovna-filipenko)). Her turn toward Soviet power came through the nursery, the dispensary and the canteen, not through theory. The account was published in *Delegatka*, a party journal, and shaped as a conversion story, so it is one woman's experience rather than a survey.

Kollontai herself admitted the gap. Looking back in 1926, she described women who had gained every legal right yet remained "without authority in family life, enslaved by a thousand menial household chores, bearing the whole burden of maternity" (Alexandra Kollontai, autobiography written 1926; trans. Salvator Attanasio, [marxists.org](https://www.marxists.org/archive/kollonta/1926/autobiography.htm)). The autobiography was written roughly a decade after the events and later published with passages marked as removed under political pressure, so its silences may have been imposed rather than chosen. Bolsheviks nevertheless presented the change as liberation. "Those hitherto treated like cattle have at last realized that they are human beings entitled to equal rights." (Nikolai Bukharin, 31 July 1920, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1917/new-woman/new-woman-texts/russian-bolshevism-and-the-working-women)) Bukharin, writing in 1920 for foreign readers, described women serving as delegates, administrators, nurses and fighters. The piece was avowedly propaganda and supplied no names, numbers or dissent; it shows what the leadership wanted believed.

Women who entered paid work found that the domestic load followed them. In a kolkhoz, or collective farm, in Tataria, Varvara Vanchurina chaired the village soviet. "Our men called me 'Fortnight'. You won't, they said, work on for more than a fortnight: this is not a woman's job. But here I am at it already for two years" (Varvara Vanchurina, 1933, as quoted by A. Nukhrat, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/creation-of-the-ethnic-republics/creation-of-the-ethnic-republics-texts/collective-farm-women-of-tataria)). On another farm the sixteen-year-old Pasha Angelina assembled a brigade of women tractor drivers. The writer Sergei Tretiakov, who spent two years researching among them, recorded the taunt "You should be barefoot and pregnant, not on a tractor!" (reported taunt to Pasha Angelina in Sergei Tretiakov, *Nine Girls*, 1935; not verbatim testimony, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/year-of-the-stakhanovite/year-of-the-stakhanovite-texts/nine-girls)). Both accounts reach us through literary or party editing, and the speech in them is reported, not transcribed. They show how skilled work opened to women while the judgement of their neighbours did not change.

Not every route into public life ran through the party. Evgeniia Vesnik organised the wives of engineers at the Krivoi Rog metallurgical combine to run creches, canteens and hostels, and described her fellow activists in these terms: "We are Bolsheviks without the party-ticket." (E. M. Vesnik, *Izvestiia*, 14 May 1936, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/abolition-of-legal-abortion/abolition-of-legal-abortion-texts/womens-work-at-krivoi-rog)). Her speech was dense with declarations of loyalty and her figures came from her own report. The women whose labour she organised are mostly spoken about rather than heard.

## The reversal

By the middle of the 1930s the state was retreating from the promise to absorb domestic work, and the family was being restored as a private duty. In 1936 the newspaper *Izvestiia* published readers' letters on a draft law, in a discussion whose outcome had already been settled. A medical student wrote: "Having a child now would mean leaving the Institute, lagging behind my husband, forgetting everything I have learnt and probably leaving Moscow because there is nowhere to live." (Student K. B., *Izvestiia*, 29 May 1936, [soviethistory.msu.edu](http://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/abolition-of-legal-abortion/abolition-of-legal-abortion-texts/abolition-of-legal-abortion)). An engineer put the same point more starkly: "The prohibition of abortion means the compulsory birth of a child to a woman who does not want children." (E. T., "An Engineer," *Izvestiia*, 1936, [soviethistory.msu.edu](http://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/abolition-of-legal-abortion/abolition-of-legal-abortion-texts/abolition-of-legal-abortion)). A professor added: "If a single child already ties a woman down, two, three or four children leave her no possibility at all of participating in social life and having a job. A man suffers less." (Prof. K. Bogolepov, *Izvestiia*, 1936, [soviethistory.msu.edu](http://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/abolition-of-legal-abortion/abolition-of-legal-abortion-texts/abolition-of-legal-abortion)). The letters were genuine but selected. The paper congratulated itself on 3,713 of them while conceding that some local bodies had simply passed unanimous resolutions. What they objected to was not abstract morality but housing, study time and the impossibility of combining a job with children.

On 27 June 1936 the decree was issued: "In view of the proven harm of abortions, to forbid the performance of abortions whether in hospitals and special health institutions, or in the homes of doctors and the private homes of pregnant women." (Decree of 27 June 1936, Art. 1, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1936/abolition-of-legal-abortion/abolition-of-legal-abortion-texts/protection-of-motherhood)). It raised maternity leave and allowances, expanded maternity beds, nurseries and kindergartens, increased alimony and made divorce harder and costlier. Its preamble invoked Lenin while conceding that he had thought a ban alone inadequate. The state presented the measure as protection, since it would now carry more of the cost of motherhood; the correspondents presented the same measure as an impossibility. Both readings are in the record and neither can be dismissed.

This is where the Khabarovsk telegram belongs. A man who had once been told that the household was a public responsibility now asked the head of state to dissolve a pregnancy so that a marriage of twenty years and two adult children could return to normal. Anisimova's own words are absent, and the affair is his allegation. In 1944, in the middle of the war, the settlement was completed: "To establish that the title of Mother Heroine is to be conferred upon mothers who have given birth to and reared 10 children" (Edict of 8 July 1944, Art. 14, [soviethistory.msu.edu](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1943/love-and-romance-in-war/love-and-romance-in-war-texts/aid-for-mothers-and-children/)). The edict paid allowances from the third child, raised maternity leave and supported unmarried mothers, but it also made divorce a public court process and ended a woman's right to establish paternity against a man she had not legally married. The same document that honoured large families narrowed the legal options of the women it honoured.

## How historians have read it

- **Wendy Z. Goldman, *Women, the State and Revolution: Soviet Family Policy and Social Life, 1917–1936* (Cambridge University Press, 1993).** The standard account of the reversal; argues that the jurists and activists who drafted the libertarian family law were purged after 1936, and that women came to identify socialism with a state that reneged on its promises ([Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/women-the-state-and-revolution/7D656C8DD94C784ED635CEACB95CB14B)).
- **Wendy Z. Goldman, *Women at the Gates: Gender and Industry in Stalin's Russia* (Cambridge University Press, 2002).** Moves from family law to factory and gender relations under industrialisation; agrees that official emancipation and lived experience diverged ([Carnegie Mellon University](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/history/people/faculty/goldman.html)).
- **Barbara Evans Clements, *Bolshevik Feminist: The Life of Aleksandra Kollontai* (Indiana University Press, 1979).** The first full-scale American biography, organised around Kollontai's successive roles; a benchmark against which the retrospective autobiography must be checked ([Indiana University Press](https://publish.iupress.indiana.edu/projects/bolshevik-feminist)).
- **Barbara Evans Clements, *Bolshevik Women* (Cambridge University Press, 1997).** A collective biography of the first generation of women revolutionaries, useful for testing whether Kollontai's circle was typical.
- **Lewis Siegelbaum, *Stakhanovism and the Politics of Productivity in the USSR, 1935–1941* (Cambridge University Press, 1988).** Treats the Stakhanovite campaign as both a production drive and a cultural programme; the study behind the accounts of the tractor brigade and the abortion debate.
- **Melanie Ilic, ed., *Women in the Stalin Era* (Palgrave, 2001).** Includes work on the female tractor driver as both reality and representation; broadly agrees that the 1930s opened skilled work while leaving the domestic load intact.

There is broad agreement on the outline: the early programme was radical on paper, the 1930s reversed it, and women's paid work expanded without a matching socialisation of housework. The live disagreement concerns how much agency ordinary women had within that settlement, and how far the Zhenotdel represented them rather than speaking for them. The sources lean toward the view that policy was made about women more than with them, though the Filipenko and Vanchurina accounts complicate that.

## Limits of the record

The surviving record is skewed toward activists, officials and people who agreed to be published. The factory worker, the kolkhoz chairman and the tractor driver all speak in texts selected by party editors; the women who opposed the 1936 ban appear only as names or pseudonyms in a state newspaper. Anisimova is silent, known only through her husband's telegram. There is almost no voice here from the women who lost standing when the Zhenotdel was dissolved in 1930, and none from those who later sought illegal abortions. Most of the English texts are translations, several twice removed from the Russian, and the translators are not always named on the page. The archival file behind the telegram is known through its published transcription, and the Zhenotdel's own records have not been consulted. The 1920 decree legalising abortion, the circumstances of the department's dissolution, the fate of women who underwent illegal abortions after 1936 and the non-Russian republics beyond Tatar material all remain gaps. Claims about prevalence, regional variation or the lives of ordinary women would need Russian-language sources and archive work. The dating of the 1936 decree should follow the signed text and its publication in *Izvestiia*, both of which give 27 June, rather than later legal summaries that give 27 July.
