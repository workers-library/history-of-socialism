On 17 March 1991 Soviet citizens were asked whether to preserve "the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics as a renewed federation of equal sovereign republics in which the rights and freedom of an individual of any nationality will be fully guaranteed" ([Presidential Library of Russia](https://www.prlib.ru/history/619099); [question reproduced by Michigan State University](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1991/march-referendum)). The wording mattered: this was a vote for a renewed federation of sovereign republics, not for the centralized state as it stood. Nine of fifteen republics took part and six boycotted. Of 148.5 million participants, 113.5 million, or 76.43 per cent, voted yes. Both sides later claimed the mandate.

## Novo-Ogarevo in clauses

The negotiations known as Novo-Ogarevo turned the vote into a draft treaty, published on 15 August 1991. "Each republic party to the treaty is a sovereign state." ([Draft Treaty on the Union of Sovereign States, 15 August 1991](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1991/nine-plus-one-agreement/nine-plus-one-agreement-texts/new-union-treaty/)). The Union of Soviet Sovereign Republics would be a federal state to which each republic voluntarily delegated listed powers. Its preamble invoked the March referendum, Article 1 gave republics a right to secede by an agreed procedure, and Article 23 provided that the 1922 treaty on the formation of the USSR would lose effect on signature. This is what "renewed Union" meant to the people drafting it. The text was a working translation of a draft never signed; it records an intention, not an outcome.

## August: the appeal and the tank

On 18 August an eight-man State Committee for the State of Emergency (GKChP), headed by Vice-President Gennady Yanaev, issued an appeal through the Soviet news agency TASS: "A mortal danger has come to loom large over our great Motherland." ([Appeal to the Soviet People, 18 August 1991](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1991/august-coup/august-coup-texts/putsch-addresses-the-soviet-people)). The committee claimed constitutional authority, said reform had entered a blind alley and declared that the referendum's results had been "trampled upon". It was issued by men who had detained the president, and its account of popular relief is an assertion, not corroborated fact.

Boris Yeltsin, president of the Russian republic, spoke the next day from the parliament building, known as the White House, with a tank outside: "Regardless of the reasons given for his removal, we are dealing with a rightist, reactionary, anti-constitutional coup." ([Yeltsin, 19 August 1991](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1991/august-coup/august-coup-texts/eltsins-defiance/)). He declared the committee's decisions unlawful, said the treaty signing planned for 20 August had been cancelled and called for an unlimited general strike. On 20 August he named individual plotters and held them responsible for inflation, military poverty and bloodshed in the Baltic: "The democratic changes our people struggled for over the past five years have been trampled underfoot." ([Yeltsin, 20 August 1991](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1991/august-coup/august-coup-texts/eltsin-addresses-the-nation/)). Each speech was a political act presenting its author as the defender of constitutional order; neither describes the defence of the building.

## After the putsch

Eleven days after his return to Moscow from detention at Foros, Gorbachev told the American senator Sam Nunn that the forces pulling the country together had returned: "First and foremost to exclude any encouragement of any processes of disintegration as our reforms proceed." ([conversation with Sam Nunn, 2 September 1991](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/media/27264/ocr)). He said the approaching treaty signing had precipitated the putsch. Asked whether nuclear weapons had stayed under central control during the coup, he answered: "There could be no question about them being out of central control." (Ibid.). This was a Soviet record of a private conversation, translated for the National Security Archive. It preserves Gorbachev's own optimistic reading of his position, which the following months contradicted, and the Archive notes that Nunn was not reassured.

At a state dinner in Madrid on 29 October he set out his idea of a "full-blooded" union with a common economic space and unified armed forces: "I declare clearly and definitively I am for the union state." ([dinner conversation, 29 October 1991](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/media/27269/ocr)). He called Yeltsin "a person who is not always reliable" and identified Gennady Burbulis as the influence pushing him towards a Russia that would be sole legal heir to the USSR. President George H. W. Bush replied: "It is a stunning breathtaking drama" (Ibid.). These are remarks by Gorbachev to sympathetic listeners, not an independent account of Yeltsin's intentions.

The minutes of the State Council meeting of 4 November show an institution that no longer worked. Gorbachev opened: "We were unable to exploit the fruits of our victory in August." ([State Council minutes, 4 November 1991](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/media/27271/ocr)). He warned that price deregulation had emptied the markets and said: "Russia alone cannot cope with the situation while others cannot escape the catastrophe on their own." (Ibid.). When he tried to open a discussion, Yeltsin twice told him to keep to the agenda and nobody would state a position on the treaty. The notes are partial and were probably made by Gorbachev's closest aide, and the bread figures Gorbachev cited are his statements, not checked statistics. Yeltsin's unilateral economic programme of 28 October and Ukraine's refusal to continue treaty negotiations made the question academic.

## December: three short texts

On 1 December Ukraine voted on the question "Do you support the Act of the Declaration of the Independence of Ukraine?" ([Ukrainian referendum results](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1991/ukrainian-independence/ukrainian-independence-texts/ukrainian-independence-referendum/)). The table reproduced from Ukrainian state archives gives 84.18 per cent turnout and 90.32 per cent yes, with a majority in every region, though Crimea's yes vote was 54.19 per cent on a 67.5 per cent turnout. The figures are a university project's reproduction, not the original files.

At Minsk on 8 December the leaders of Belarus, Russia and Ukraine signed the agreement establishing the Commonwealth of Independent States: "the USSR as a subject of international law and a geopolitical reality, ceases to exist." ([Belovezh Accords, 8 December 1991](https://eccis.org/en/page/176)). It provided for a common military-strategic space and unified control over nuclear weapons, and was drafted in secret without a mandate from the Soviet constitutional bodies. Less than ten minutes after the signing Yeltsin telephoned President Bush: "Mr. President, I must tell you confidentially, President Gorbachev does not know these results." ([telephone memorandum, 8 December 1991](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/27272-document-9-memorandum-telephone-conversation-bush-yeltsin-december-8-1991), as quoted in the National Security Archive's description; the transcript PDF is an image scan that could not be read directly). He also claimed the support of Kazakhstan's leader Nazarbayev, which the Archive notes was false: Nazarbayev was in Moscow and did not go to Minsk.

At Alma-Ata on 21 December eleven states extended the Commonwealth and declared that "With the formation of the Commonwealth of Independent States the USSR ceases to exist." ([Alma-Ata Declaration, 21 December 1991](https://worldhistorycommons.org/alma-ata-declaration)). They called it "neither a state, nor a super-state structure", preserved unified command of strategic forces and single control over nuclear weapons, and left open what the new body was for. The English is a Library of Congress translation, and the host page mislabels the declaration; the text itself is the 21 December one.

Less than two hours before his resignation broadcast on 25 December, Gorbachev told Bush by telephone that a decree of resignation lay on his desk, that he would transfer authority over nuclear weapons to Yeltsin and that "I do not intend to hide in the taiga, in the woods." ([last telephone call, 25 December 1991](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/27273-document-10-memorandum-telephone-conversation-gorbachev-bush-december-25-1991-last), as quoted in the Archive's description; the transcript PDF is an image scan that could not be read directly). He asked the president to support Yeltsin and said everything was under strict control; that claim is his own assessment. In his televised address he said he was resigning because the Commonwealth had been formed: "Events have taken a different path. A policy line aimed at dismembering the country and disuniting the state has prevailed, something that I cannot agree with." ([resignation speech, 25 December 1991](https://soviethistory.msu.edu/1991/end-of-the-soviet-union/end-of-the-soviet-union-texts/gorbachev-resigns-as-president)). He argued that decisions of such scale should have rested on a popular vote, named the August putsch as the point at which the crisis broke and said the old system collapsed before a new one could work. It was a final defence by the principal actor.

## How the evidence has been read

Serhii Plokhy traces political and national disintegration through 1991 with particular attention to Ukraine; Vladislav Zubok puts economic factors and Russian political choices at the centre; Svetlana Savranskaya and Thomas Blanton publish the conversation transcripts and argue that American policy backed the centre and Gorbachev personally to the end, rather than engineering the breakup ([National Security Archive briefing book, 2021](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/russia-programs/2021-12-21/end-soviet-union-1991)). A collection of contemporary British diplomatic cables from Moscow, including Rodric Braithwaite's cable of 25 December, offers a witness from the same weeks (Ibid., note 1). Anatoly Chernyaev's diary is the daily record of an aide who saw the coup at Foros and understood earlier than his chief that letting the Baltic republics and Georgia leave peacefully was the only remote chance of preserving a democratizing Union ([Chernyaev diary, 1991](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB345)). There is broad agreement on the sequence and on the importance of the failed coup and republican sovereignty; the disagreement is over weight. The anti-revisionist and Trotskyist readings of the collapse are minority positions in this scholarship and should be identified as such, not balanced equally against it.

## What the record cannot settle

Whether the Union Treaty would have held anything together is counterfactual. The documents record what leaders said they intended; the bargaining at the hunting lodge at Belovezh, the reasons Nazarbayev refused to join and the discussions in the Ukrainian and Russian parliaments in November and December are not in this record. The sources establish a sequence, not the proportions of cause. Economic collapse, nationalist mobilisation, institutional design and individual ambition all appear, but the documents cannot weigh them, and the Commonwealth's legal language does not say whether it was meant as a successor or as an instrument for burying the Union.

The record is strong on formal acts and on conversations involving Gorbachev, Yeltsin and the American president, and thin elsewhere. Most of it is English translation from Russian; the Ukrainian table is a university reproduction of archival figures, and the original files should be checked before the numbers are treated as final. Belarusian and Kazakh first-hand accounts are absent, as is testimony from the Caucasus or Central Asia about the final weeks. Diaries and memoirs that could supply ordinary experience were scanned without a text layer or not consulted, and three Archive transcripts exist only as image PDFs, so their quotations are taken from the Archive's descriptions and flagged above. Chernyaev's proximity gave him access and created loyalty; Gorbachev's diary, dinner remarks and speech all defend the proposition that he tried to preserve the Union and was betrayed. Yeltsin's calls and speeches present him as the defender of constitutionality and the GKChP called itself the saviour of the country. Economic distress appears mainly as ministers' figures, not household experience, and there is no testimony here from a Donbas worker, a Kazakh village, a conscript or a Baltic family in those weeks. Filling the gaps would need the Gorbachev Foundation archive, the Russian State Archive, Ukrainian state archives and physical access to image-only collections.
