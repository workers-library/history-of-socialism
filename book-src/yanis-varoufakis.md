Yanis Varoufakis is a Greek economist, former finance minister and political organiser whose work moved from the mathematics of industrial disputes to a claim about what capitalism has become. He became known during the euro crisis for *The Global Minotaur* and for the Modest Proposal, a set of policy measures written with Stuart Holland and James K. Galbraith. The argument that has drawn the formal scholarly response recorded below is that capitalism has been displaced by **technofeudalism**: his name for a social formation in which a handful of firms own the digital platforms that have replaced markets, and rent has taken the place of profit as the force running the economy. At the centre of that claim is **cloud rent**, which he defines as the payment the owners of those platforms extract from producers who need access to them in order to sell anything. This profile sets out what he argues, the sources he uses, and the objections made against him.

## Biography

Varoufakis was born in Athens on 24 March 1961 according to his own CV as hosted by the University of Athens and reproduced in an Institute for New Economic Thinking listing seen in search results; the direct CV address returned a 404, and his own site gives only "born in Athens, 1961". The exact date should therefore be read as reported rather than confirmed from an opened document. In his first-person account he says he was six in April 1967 when a military coup installed the dictatorship of the Colonels, and that the experience left him with a strong sense of what it means to be unfree and, at the same time, convinced that progress was possible ([Beginnings](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/about/beginnings-from-the-dictatorship-of-the-colonels-to-the-tyranny-of-economics/)). His 2025 memoir *Raise Your Soul* gives a fuller family story: an uncle, Panayis, imprisoned and tortured under the junta, hid a message to fellow prisoners inside a model aeroplane made of matchsticks and cigarette paper, and his mother Eleni broke the plane open after a prison visit in September 1969 ([author's site, 15 October 2025](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2025/10/15/raise-your-soul-my-new-book-telling-the-story-of-the-last-100-years-through-the-voices-of-5-remarkable-women/)). He calls the book "fictionalised history" and says he put words into people's mouths only where he had been told they were spoken or where he judged them plausible from conversations he witnessed. That is his own method statement, not a guarantee of documentary accuracy.

He left Greece in 1978 to study in Britain. He first wanted to study physics, then chose economics because it was, in his words, the "lingua franca of political discourse"; after a few weeks he moved from the economics school to the mathematics school at the University of Essex because he found the economics teaching simplistic and its mathematics poor. He took a BA (Hons) in Mathematical Economics at Essex in 1981, an MSc in Mathematical Statistics at Birmingham in 1982 and a PhD in Economics at Essex in 1987, with a dissertation titled *Optimisation and Strikes* on models of industrial disputes, supervised by Monojit Chatterji. He says he returned to economics only because an econometric study of strikes angered him by its methodological sloppiness, and that a career in economics departments followed as "an atheist theologian ensconced in a Middle Ages monastery" ([Beginnings](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/about/beginnings-from-the-dictatorship-of-the-colonels-to-the-tyranny-of-economics/)).

His academic posts, recorded in his own CV and in the Istanbul Policy Center profile ([institutional profile](https://ipc.sabanciuniv.edu/en/people/yanis-varoufakis)), were at Essex, the University of East Anglia, Trinity College Cambridge and the University of Glasgow, followed by a long period at the University of Sydney from 1990 to 2002. He returned to Greece in 2000, became Associate Professor of Economic Theory at the University of Athens that year and Professor of Economic Theory from September 2006. He was a visiting professor at the Lyndon B. Johnson School of Public Affairs at the University of Texas from 2013 to 2015 and worked as Chief Economist at Valve Software in Seattle in 2012. He was Minister of Finance of Greece from January to July 2015 and a member of the Hellenic Parliament for Athens B from January to September 2015. He launched DiEM25 in February 2016 and later led MeRA25 in Greece.

Penguin's author page calls him an economist, political leader and author of bestselling books, a co-founder of DiEM25, a Professor of Economics at the University of Athens and co-presenter of the podcast *The Econoclasts* ([Penguin author record](https://www.penguin.co.uk/authors/237985/yanis-varoufakis)). That podcast was launched with Wolfgang Munchau in October 2025, but the date was confirmed only by a search-result endpoint, not by an opened page. His own site is titled "Thoughts for the post-2008 world" and began in response to the 2008 crash and what he saw as the existential crisis of European institutions ([About](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/about)).

## Intellectual tradition

Varoufakis works in the Marxist political-economy tradition, but his relation to labels is argumentative rather than doctrinal. In the February 2024 *Jacobin* interview he said of *Technofeudalism*: "This book falls squarely within the Marxist political-economic tradition. I wrote it as a piece of Marxist scholarship. So, from my Marxist perspective, this is a tragic book to have to write" ([Jacobin interview](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2024/02/22/jacobin-magazines-david-moscrop-reviews-my-technofeudalism/)). Years earlier he gave a talk titled "Confessions of an Erratic Marxist in the midst of a repugnant European Crisis", listed on his About page; the page for that talk returned a 404 when opened, so only its title and listing were verified. In the book's "Influences, Readings and Acknowledgements" he writes that his father's left-wing politics, his mother's account of unpaid effort at work, Einstein's account of the dual nature of light and Marx's 1856 speech together gave him a picture of everything as "pregnant with its contrary".

He also calls himself a "libertarian Marxist" in the book, a phrase quoted by Nicholas Gane ([Gane, full text](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/188261/1/Capitalism-is-Capitalism-not-technofeudalism%20pdf.pdf)). Gane treats this as evidence that Varoufakis is antipathetic to the regulatory powers of the state; Varoufakis's own later writing complicates that reading, since his Monetary Commons proposal asks central banks to create public digital money and pay a universal dividend ([A Trust Fund for Everyone](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2025/10/10/25876/)). Both are interpretations of his position and should not be resolved into a single label.

He rejects several neighbouring descriptions of the present. In a video essay on his site he argues that "rentier capitalism" and "monopoly capitalism" will not do, because cloud rent requires massive investment in new technology and because Bezos and Zuckerberg "have replaced markets and have no interest in manufacturing anything", and that "surveillance capitalism" will not do either, because cloud capital "reproduces itself through our free-labour" and squeezes rents from vassal capitalists on platforms that are not markets. That essay was seen as a search-result endpoint with substantial quoted text, not fetched directly. In his 2026 *Jacobin* reply he adds three qualifications: there is no literal return to feudalism; rent is not new to capitalism, and Baran and Sweezy, Ricardo and financialisation all document earlier rents; and data is not "the new land", a notion he calls "data fetishism". He nonetheless insists that a qualitative change in capital itself has occurred ([2026 reply](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2026/09/26/the-marxist-case-for-the-technofeudal-hypothesis-jacobin/)).

## Major contributions

The first contribution is the argument of *The Global Minotaur*. On his own book page he says that financialisation, weak regulation, greed and globalisation were symptoms rather than causes of the 2008 crisis; the deeper cause was the emergence, from the Great Crash of 1929 through the 1970s, of a "Global Minotaur" into which the rest of the world sent capital, and which pulled the world economy from the early 1980s to 2008. America's deficit and the recycling of foreign surpluses into Wall Street are presented as the system's engine, and the weakening of that engine as the source of the European and global crises ([The Global Minotaur](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/the-global-minotaur/)). He later coined "bankruptocracy" for the post-2008 order in which, as he puts it in the notes to *Technofeudalism*, a Western banker's power is analogous to the size of his bank's losses.

The second contribution is the policy programme known as the Modest Proposal, first published with Stuart Holland in 2010 and revised in July 2013 with James K. Galbraith. It responds to four crises — banking, public debt, under-investment and social — with four policies that it claims can be implemented without treaty change: case-by-case bank recapitalisation through the European Stability Mechanism, a limited debt-conversion programme operated by the ECB, an investment-led recovery programme financed by European Investment Bank and European Investment Fund bonds, and an emergency social solidarity programme funded from central-bank interest and financial transaction charges ([Version 4.0 PDF](https://yanisvaroufakis.eu/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/a-modest-proposal-for-resolving-the-eurozone-crisis-version-4-0-final1.pdf)). The paper argues explicitly that the ECB will not be allowed to monetise sovereigns directly and that surplus countries will not consent to joint-and-several Eurobonds; its design starts from those political constraints rather than from an ideal federal Europe. His later Project Syndicate article of 17 August 2015 extends the debt-conversion idea into a scheme in which the ECB services the Maastricht-compliant portion of a member state's maturing debt while the member state remains liable to the ECB under super-seniority ([A New Approach to Eurozone Sovereign Debt](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2015/08/17/a-new-approach-to-eurozone-sovereign-debt-op-ed-in-project-syndicate/)).

The third contribution is the analysis of technocratic power in the euro crisis, developed in *Adults in the Room* and *And the Weak Suffer What They Must?*. Penguin's description of the latter says that after 2008 "the political elite's response ensured it would be the weakest citizens of the weakest nations that paid the price for the bankers' mistakes", and that the book draws on his experience of negotiations with the eurozone's financiers ([Penguin record](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/433227/and-the-weak-suffer-what-they-must-by-yanis-varoufakis/9781784704117)). The memoir's subtitle, *My Battle With Europe's Deep Establishment*, names the institutional target ([Penguin record](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/435157/adults-in-the-room-by-yanis-varoufakis/9781784705763)), though a search-result endpoint for the Institute for New Economic Thinking gives a different form, *My Struggle Against Europe's Deep Establishment*; the difference is recorded rather than resolved. These are publisher descriptions of his argument, not independent findings about the negotiations.

The fourth contribution is the technofeudalism thesis itself. In the preface he writes: "It is that capitalism is now dead, in the sense that its dynamics no longer govern our economies. In that role it has been replaced by something fundamentally different, which I call technofeudalism. At the heart of my thesis is an irony that may sound confusing at first but which I hope to show makes perfect sense: the thing that has killed capitalism is … capital itself" ([preface](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2023/09/07/technofeudalism-has-just-arrived/)). He dates its origins to the privatisation of the original internet in the early 2000s, the flow of central-bank money after 2008 into cloud capital, and the pandemic ([valori.it interview](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2024/01/13/qa-on-technofeudalism-interviewed-by-lorenzo-tecleme-for-valori-it)). In the 2026 reply he restates the mechanism in Marxist terms: cloud capital consists of "Produced Means of Behavioural Modification" (PMBM), is unproductive in Marx's sense because it produces no commodities, and replaces markets with "cloud fiefs" such as amazon.com.

The fifth contribution is a set of institutional proposals for what would follow capitalism: democratised companies, democratised money, the cloud and the land as commons, a central-bank digital wallet and a universal dividend not funded by taxation. These appear in the table of contents of *Technofeudalism*, in the Monetary Commons proposal and in the *Jacobin* interview; they are discussed below under Analysis of socialism.

## Analysis of capitalism

Varoufakis's account of capitalism begins with labour. In chapter 1 of *Technofeudalism* he distinguishes "commodity labour", the time and formal skills a worker sells for a wage, from "experiential labour", the effort, enthusiasm and flair that cannot be bought directly. Capitalists pay for the first and obtain the second without paying for it; that gap, in his telling, is the source of profit. He says he arrived at this distinction from his mother's complaint that a fertiliser factory paid her for her time but not for her passion, and only later recognised it in Marx's distinction between labour and labouring power. Capital itself, likewise, has two natures: a thing, such as a tractor or a server, and a power to command others who need access to it.

His second claim concerns the historical relation of rent and profit. In the book he writes that "Rent flows from privileged access to things in fixed supply, like fertile soil or land containing fossil fuels; you cannot produce more of these resources, however much money you might invest in them. Profit, in contrast, flows into the pockets of entrepreneurial people who have invested in things that would otherwise not have existed" (quoted with page references 121–122 by Gane, **Scholarly Secondary**). Capitalism, on this reading, won when profit overwhelmed rent; technofeudalism is the reverse, but through access to digital territory rather than soil. He tells *WIRED* that "Profit drives capitalism, rent drove feudalism" and that the Apple App Store's 30 per cent commission is "a rent. That's like a ground rent" ([WIRED interview](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2024/04/22/welcome-to-the-age-of-technofeudalism-interviewed-by-wired-magazine)).

His own definition of cloud rent appears in Appendix 1 of the book: "Cloud Rent is the payment cloudalists extract from the vassal capitalists for access to cloud fiefs" (section 9.2.3), where a vassal capitalist is "a capitalist producer who, in order to sell their commodities, must pay cloud rent for access to the cloudalists' cloud fiefs" (section 9.2.2). Earlier in the book he calls it "the digital equivalent of ground rent". The preface defines it more broadly as "a form of rent that must be paid for access to those platforms and to the cloud more broadly. I call it cloud-rent" ([preface](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2023/09/07/technofeudalism-has-just-arrived/)), and the *valori.it* interview gives the compressed version: cloud rent is "payments by capitalist producers to Big Tech's non-producing owners for access to their cloud fiefdoms".

Technofeudalism is the name he gives to the resulting social formation. Capitalism had two pillars, markets and profit; "markets and profit remain ubiquitous", he writes, but "they just aren't running the show any more" ([preface](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2023/09/07/technofeudalism-has-just-arrived/)). Markets have been replaced by digital trading platforms that look like markets but are "better understood as fiefdoms", and profit has been replaced by rent. In the *WIRED* interview he names three groups: "vassal capitalists" such as a bicycle manufacturer selling on Amazon, "cloud proles" such as warehouse workers monitored by algorithms, and "cloud serfs" — ordinary users who supply unpaid labour simply by using platforms. He argues that users add to the capital stock of these firms simply by posting, rating or moving around a map, something he says has no precedent: "we are more than serfs, we're cloud serfs producing capital" ([WIRED interview](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2024/04/22/welcome-to-the-age-of-technofeudalism-interviewed-by-wired-magazine)). In the book's Appendix 1 he calls this "universal exploitation" and says the capitalist sector still produces all exchange value, which is then siphoned off as cloud rent to the cloudalists.

The macroeconomic mechanism is a drain on demand. In the *WIRED* interview he says: "According to my calculations, between 35 and 40 percent of GDP is being siphoned off the circular flow of income by cloud rent, and that means there is less money in the economy. Investment is low, and that means less good, quality jobs in the rest of the economy" ([WIRED interview](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2024/04/22/welcome-to-the-age-of-technofeudalism-interviewed-by-wired-magazine)). This is his own estimate; no underlying dataset or calculation was opened for this profile, and it is not treated here as an established figure. He connects cloud capital to political breakdown through the same interview, arguing that algorithms reward anger because "consensus is really bad for cloud capital", and in the 2026 reply he calls the resulting politics "techlordism", an ideology that deifies cloud capital as neoliberalism deified the market. In a December 2025 essay he extends the analogy to the state, writing that "Big Tech are the new Soviets" because Amazon's software centrally matches buyers and sellers as Gosplan did, but under private ownership, and that states now lease back their own operational capacity from Amazon Web Services, Microsoft and Google. That essay was seen only as an author-site listing with the text quoted in a search result; it was not opened directly.

## Analysis of socialism

Varoufakis does not present a single constitutional blueprint for a socialist state, and that should be said plainly. What he offers is a critique of actually existing social democracy plus a set of institutional proposals. In chapter 7 of *Technofeudalism* he recalls being unable to answer a "cockney Tory" who asked what he would replace capitalism with, and says Marx's own refusal to provide a blueprint was "wishful thinking" because no bottom-up socialist blueprint has transpired anywhere. He turns instead to *Another Now*, which Penguin describes as imagining a world with no banks, no stock market, no tech giants and no billionaires, and asks whether we would really want it ([Penguin record](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/439220/another-now-by-yanis-varoufakis/9781529110630)). In the *Jacobin* interview he says the book imagines a new Bretton Woods based on Keynes's rejected proposal, merged with a participatory democratic socialist framework, redistributing income and wealth from the Global North to the Global South through green investment ([Jacobin interview](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2024/02/22/jacobin-magazines-david-moscrop-reviews-my-technofeudalism/)).

His institutional preferences recur across the opened sources. He proposes socialising both productive and cloud capital, democratising companies, treating the cloud and the land as commons, a "cloud rebellion" that unites traditional workers, cloud proles, cloud serfs and vassal small capitalists, and a central-bank digital wallet paying a universal dividend. These are proposals about [ownership and control](/ownership-and-control), not a constitution. In the Monetary Commons proposal he argues that if deposits move from commercial banks to central-bank wallets, the money supply contracts, and the central bank can offset the contraction by crediting every resident equally — "without new taxes, new debt and no magic", in his phrase ([A Trust Fund for Everyone](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2025/10/10/25876/)). The *Jacobin* interview defends the same idea as a universal basic income "not funded by taxation". He also says social democracy is impossible under technofeudalism because the liberal individual and the national political arena have been displaced by cloud fiefs and centralised platforms.

These are his proposals and judgements, not verified institutional designs. No empirical test of the Monetary Commons arithmetic was opened, and no evidence was examined about whether such a system would in fact be non-inflationary.

## Recommended reading

These five works are chosen because each represents a distinct stage of the argument. The annotations restate his own claims.

- *The Global Minotaur* (2011). Sets out the claim that America's deficit and the recycling of the world's surpluses into Wall Street sustained global growth until 2008.
- *Adults in the Room* (2017). The first-person account of the 2015 finance ministry and the confrontation with eurozone institutions, on which his analysis of technocratic power rests.
- *Talking to My Daughter: A Brief History of Capitalism* (2017/2019). The earliest statement of the labour and capital "dualities" that he later uses to explain cloud capital.
- *Another Now: Dispatches from an Alternative Present* (2020). His only extended attempt to describe a socialist alternative, written as political science fiction.
- *Technofeudalism: What Killed Capitalism* (2023). The full statement of cloud capital, cloud rent and the claim that capitalism has been superseded, including the technical appendix.

## Intellectual influences

The influences best evidenced are those he names himself. In "Influences, Readings and Acknowledgements" he names John Kenneth Galbraith's *The New Industrial State* (1967) as the source of the word "technostructure" and of the idea of a managerial class distinct from capitalists and workers. He names Shoshana Zuboff's *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*, Cédric Durand's *Technoféodalisme*, McKenzie Wark's *Capital Is Dead: Is This Something Worse?* (2019), Cory Doctorow and Rebecca Giblin's *Chokepoint Capitalism* (2022), Brett Scott's *Cloudmoney* (2022), and Judith Meyer, who taught him about algorithms and cloud technology. He says he disagreed with Wark's "vectoralist" class because he saw the vector as a mutation of capital rather than its usurper. He also records that the phrase "cloud capital" came from a 1969 *Star Trek* episode, "The Cloud Minders", about a ruling class living above the clouds and workers beneath them.

Earlier layers are named in chapter 1 of *Technofeudalism*. His father's left-wing politics and interest in metallurgy, his mother's account of uncompensated effort, Hesiod's Five Ages, the *Communist Manifesto*, Marx's 1856 speech, Einstein's "Why Socialism?" (May 1949) and Keynes's *General Theory* all appear as explicit sources for his method and vocabulary. He invokes David Ricardo on rent, Rosa Luxemburg's "Socialism or barbarism?", and Richard Goodwin's predator–prey model in the 2026 *Jacobin* reply ([reply](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2026/09/26/the-marxist-case-for-the-technofeudal-hypothesis-jacobin/)). His co-authors on the Modest Proposal, Stuart Holland and James K. Galbraith, and the latter's father J. K. Galbraith, form a separate line of influence on his policy thinking.

## Influence and legacy

Within left-wing economics and politics, Varoufakis's footprint has three distinct channels. The Modest Proposal was published in several versions between 2010 and 2013 and was later reproduced in his own 2015 Project Syndicate article; its co-authorship with James K. Galbraith placed it inside a wider heterodox policy network. DiEM25, launched in February 2016, and MeRA25 gave his ideas an organisational home, and his books have been widely translated: the author's books page lists German, Greek, Czech, Italian, Spanish, French, Serbian and Finnish editions of *The Global Minotaur* ([books page](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/books)), while his dedicated *Global Minotaur* page names German, Greek, Italian, Spanish, Czech and Finnish editions ([book page](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/the-global-minotaur/)) and the Istanbul Policy Center profile says "ten languages" ([institutional profile](https://ipc.sabanciuniv.edu/en/people/yanis-varoufakis)). The three counts differ and none was independently checked.

The technofeudalism thesis has generated a substantial scholarly debate within a few years of publication. The SAGE record for Gane's critique showed 19,835 views and downloads and a Crossref citation count of 13 when seen in search results; those figures were not independently verified. The Cambridge article by Garrod and the special issue in *Area Development and Policy* with contributions by Saito, Sasaki and Wainwright show that the thesis is being engaged formally rather than merely reviewed in the press. The 2026 *Jacobin* reply is itself evidence that he treats this criticism as significant enough to answer at length.

His reception is contested. Gane argues that declaring capitalism dead "is politically significant" because it removes neoliberal privatisation and the regulatory powers of the state from view ([Gane, full text](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/188261/1/Capitalism-is-Capitalism-not-technofeudalism%20pdf.pdf)). Morozov argues that feudal language lets the left "whitewash" actually existing techno-capitalism, a formulation read in Durand's response rather than in Morozov's own paywalled text. Varoufakis himself notes in the 2026 reply that Steve Bannon embraced the book and that some Marxists responded with anger; he treats both as reasons to make the argument more precise, not as reasons to abandon it.

## Criticisms and controversies

The most direct scholarly rejection from within Marxist political economy comes from Kohei Saito of the University of Tokyo and the New Institute, and Ryuji Sasaki of Rikkyo University, in "Rentier capitalism, technofascism and the destruction of the common", *Area Development and Policy* 11(1), 2026, pp. 1–15, published online 13 October 2025. Their abstract says the technofeudalism discourse "fails to account for the ongoing dynamism of capitalist investment and competition" and argues instead that "rentier capitalism constitutes the most advanced and contradictory form of contemporary capitalism". They read Marx's ground-rent theory and his late notebooks to argue that rent and monopoly do not mark a departure from capitalism. The abstract was seen in a search-result excerpt because the publisher's page returned a 403; authors, affiliations and dates were verified through Crossref ([Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1080/23792949.2025.2557911)); the full paper was not opened. Joel Wainwright, in a response in the same issue, says he "largely agree[s]" with Saito and Sasaki and adds that the techno-feudal hypothesis "also fails to provide a compelling description of contemporary politics" because its feudal metaphor misses differences in political subjectivity ([abstract and notes](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23792949.2025.2557909)). A. K. Norris and Tavo Espinosa, independent researchers, make a related economic case in the *Review of Radical Political Economics* (published online 5 August 2026, DOI 10.1177/04866134261452322): their Crossref abstract says they counter "the argument that the technology industry's revenues constitute rents rather than profits", finding that Big Tech's revenues span industrial and commercial profit ([Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/04866134261452322)). Only the abstract was opened; the full text was not.

Nicholas Gane, Professor of Sociology at Warwick, published "Capitalism is Capitalism, Not Technofeudalism" in the *Journal of Classical Sociology* 25(4), 2025, pp. 353–368, first published online 9 August 2024 ([full open-access article](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/188261/1/Capitalism-is-Capitalism-not-technofeudalism%20pdf.pdf); [repository record](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/188261)). His central claim is that "capitalism has not been displaced by a resurgence of feudalism, but is taking new forms that, increasingly, lie outside the powers of nation-states and of social democracy". His detailed objections are worth separating. First, using Max Weber, he argues that Varoufakis does not identify ties of fealty, a status order or the characteristic instability of the lord–vassal relation, and that Amazon remains a listed company whose shares trade and which faces competing marketplaces. Second, rent has not displaced profit: Amazon's own annual report measures success by shareholder value, revenue, profitability and returns on invested capital, and double-entry bookkeeping still records profit. Third, Varoufakis's proposed resistance — withdrawing labour, boycotting Amazon, withholding pension contributions from funds holding cloud capital — presupposes precisely the market choices and share-price vulnerabilities that technofeudalism is said to have abolished. Gane also objects that Varoufakis dismisses neoliberal governance as an "uninteresting hodgepodge", leaving no account of the state policies that enabled privatisation.

Evgeny Morozov's *New Left Review* essay "Critique of Techno-Feudal Reason" (NLR 133/134, January–April 2022) was opened only to the paywall ([opening section](https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii133/articles/evgeny-morozov-critique-of-techno-feudal-reason)); the opened portion places Varoufakis among a broad set of left and right writers drawn to feudal language and says his version "seems to be mostly about the perverse macroeconomic effects of quantitative easing". The stronger formulations — "Capitalism is moving in the same direction it always has been, leveraging whatever resources it can mobilize—the cheaper, the better", and the warning that invoking techno-feudalism risks "whitewashing" techno-capitalism's reputation — were read in Cédric Durand's open response in NLR 136, which quotes Morozov directly ([Durand](https://newleftreview.org/issues/ii136/articles/cedric-durand-scouting-capital-s-frontiers)). Durand himself does not simply reject the feudal hypothesis: he argues that it is compatible with older Marxist critiques and should be tested empirically, but he agrees that the label must not become a substitute for analysing actually existing capitalist relations.

Two further peer-reviewed critiques were opened at varying depth. Elliot Goodell Ugalde's "Beyond techno-feudalism: A monopoly-capital analysis of artificial intelligence investment" (*Capital & Class*, published online 24 January 2026) argues from Ellen Meiksins Wood and Robert Brenner that monopoly and rent are products of capitalist competition, not evidence of a post-capitalist rupture; only a search-result excerpt was available, not the full text. Joel Z. Garrod's article in *Finance and Society* (published online 26 December 2025, volume 12, issue 2, pp. 304–321) says there are already "good critiques of the technofeudal thesis" because "tech firms are still capitalist firms – profit-maximizers concerned with revolutionizing the means of production – and rent has not overtaken profit as the dominant form of accumulation", while arguing that the focus on technofeudalism distracts from fascism and the global enforcement of corporate private property ([full text](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/finance-and-society/article/technofeudalism-or-capitalism-same-as-it-ever-was-placing-the-blockchain-in-global-capitalism/3B699270D241F031A2DCCB988DE83E95)).

Controversies about tone and reception also matter. Critics note that Varoufakis's claim has been embraced by figures on the right, a fact he acknowledges in his 2026 reply ([reply](https://www.yanisvaroufakis.eu/2026/09/26/the-marxist-case-for-the-technofeudal-hypothesis-jacobin/)). Gane records his criticism of identity politics on the left as part of his argument that the "libertarian Marxist" position has no strategy for state regulation. In *Raise Your Soul* he discloses that he was assaulted in 2024 and says his political struggles had met "three consecutive electoral defeats in the space of one year"; these are first-person statements in a book he describes as fictionalised history and were not independently corroborated. Finally, the claim that he was banned from Germany was presented on his own site as a dispute requiring clarification; the underlying German decision was not opened, so no finding is made here.

## Related thinkers

- **Karl Marx** — the central reference point: the labour/labouring-power distinction, the falling rate of profit, ground rent, and Luxemburg's "socialism or barbarism?" all appear in his own text.
- **J. K. Galbraith and James K. Galbraith** — the first supplies "technostructure" and the alternative Bretton Woods; the second is his co-author on the Modest Proposal, alongside Stuart Holland.
- **David Ricardo** — supplies the rent argument that Varoufakis reworks for digital platforms; Richard Goodwin supplies the predator–prey framework in the 2026 paper.
- **Shoshana Zuboff, Cédric Durand, McKenzie Wark, Cory Doctorow, Rebecca Giblin and Brett Scott** — contemporaries cited as influences in the book's acknowledgements, with Wark's "vectoralist" class explicitly rejected.
- **Nicholas Gane, Evgeny Morozov, Kohei Saito, Ryuji Sasaki, Joel Wainwright, A. K. Norris, Tavo Espinosa, Elliot Goodell Ugalde and Joel Z. Garrod** — writers who have engaged him critically in the sources opened.
- **[Michael Hudson](/michael-hudson)** — overlapping ground in rent, finance and the critique of capitalism; the dossier records no direct intellectual influence between them and Varoufakis and asserts none here.
