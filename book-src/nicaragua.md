Within two or three years of the Sandinista victory of July 1979, the commanders who had made it were asked to explain how. Marta Harnecker, a Chilean journalist, interviewed Dora María Téllez, who led the seizure of the National Palace in 1978 and the rising in León ([*Pueblos en Armas*](https://www.marxists.org/espanol/harnecker/89861.pdf)). Téllez described insurrection as something assembled patiently:

> "la insurrección es una telaraña que hay que tejer" — Dora María Téllez, FSLN commander, interview first published March 1981, in Marta Harnecker, *Pueblos en Armas*. Original Spanish; my translation: "the insurrection is a spiderweb that has to be woven". [Marxists Internet Archive: harnecker 89861](https://www.marxists.org/espanol/harnecker/89861.pdf)

The FSLN, the Sandinista National Liberation Front, overthrew the dictatorship of Anastasio Somoza, weakened by the 1972 earthquake that destroyed much of Managua and by a broad opposition coalition. The interviews preserve the victors' own account, close enough to be specific and early enough to be candid:

> "En 1961, de la conjugación de varios grupos armados, surge el Frente Sandinista" — Humberto Ortega, FSLN commander, interview 1980–82. Original Spanish; my translation: "In 1961, from the combination of several armed groups, the Sandinista Front arose". [Marxists Internet Archive: harnecker 89861](https://www.marxists.org/espanol/harnecker/89861.pdf)

Ortega, a National Directorate member and commander of the final offensive, placed the Front's creation in several armed groups and described its split into three tendencies in 1976 and 1977 as a failure of organisation under repression. The rising of September 1978 failed militarily, but Téllez said it taught the movement what the web required:

> "las masas nunca se te van a levantar si no creen que van a tomar el poder" — Dora María Téllez, interview first published March 1981. Original Spanish; my translation: "the masses will never rise unless they believe they are going to take power". [Marxists Internet Archive: harnecker 89861](https://www.marxists.org/espanol/harnecker/89861.pdf)

She argued that militias, communications and hospitals had to be woven together before the 1979 offensive, and that in Monimbó, an indigenous community, crowds rose spontaneously in response to killings before the party could direct them. All the interviewees were winning commanders; opponents, peasants and ordinary fighters do not appear.

## Governing and teaching

Two later interviews show how the movement tried to govern. Luis Carrión, a National Directorate member and Vice-Minister of the Interior, traced Christian participation to Vatican II and Medellín; where other popular organisation was repressed, small Catholic base communities became centres of local leadership:

> "comienzan a hablar, no sólo de temas cristianos, sino de los problemas económicos y sociales que los están agobiando" — Luis Carrión, FSLN National Directorate member, interview 14 November 1986. Original Spanish; my translation: "they begin to speak not only of Christian matters but of the economic and social problems weighing on them". [Marxists Internet Archive: harnecker 90187](https://www.marxists.org/espanol/harnecker/90187.pdf)

Carlos Carrión described a later effort to replace vertical command with mass participation under the pressure of war. Both were senior officials justifying party policy, and no critical Christian or bishops' voice appears.

The best documented social programme was the National Literacy Crusade of 1980, whose aim included *concientización*, the raising of political consciousness. The official record gives 95,582 participants, 406,056 people taught and 59 literacy workers dead, and states the result as:

> "reduciendo la tasa de analfabetismo de un 50.35% a un 12.96%" — Nicaragua Ministry of Education, official page on the National Literacy Crusade. Original Spanish; my translation: "reducing the illiteracy rate from 50.35% to 12.96%". [Source: mined.gob.ni](https://www.mined.gob.ni/cruzada-nacional-de-alfabetizacion/)

José Luis Rocha, a former brigadista, recalled a Popular Literacy Army of about 100,000, of whom 60,000 went to the mountains, working five months:

> "The National Literacy Crusade turned into an intensive five-month course—from March 23 to August 23, 1980." — José Luis Rocha, former brigadista, Envío, September 2000. [Source: revistaenvio.org](https://www.revistaenvio.org/articulo/1446)

Violeta Delgado joined Sandinista youth at ten and spent four months teaching adults away from home:

> "And the next year, I went to teach people how to read and write. I was eleven years old." — Violeta Delgado, oral history interview, June 2011. [Source: backend.production.deepblue-documents.lib.umich.edu](https://backend.production.deepblue-documents.lib.umich.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/e4b4ff02-4127-4d07-a62e-354edc00c2bc/content)

Another volunteer, known by a pseudonym and interviewed by her son, was fourteen; she remembered military rules, male squad leaders, harassment and lice, but also the care of host families and the satisfaction of hearing pupils read:

> "I remember we all had a mass hysteria. One of us started crying, and we all started crying." — "Joan of Arc", brigadista, interview translated by Rafael Santiago Meza Duriez, published 11 June 2025. [Source: clacsberkeley.blog](https://clacsberkeley.blog/2025/06/11/she-taught-me-trova-i-learned-about-the-revolution-and-education/)

The last two accounts were recorded decades later and the pseudonym prevents corroboration; they qualify the heroic public memory rather than measuring it. Doris Tijerino, an early militant who rose to head the Sandinista police, described having her ideas credited to male subordinates in a *testimonio*, a first-person testimony recorded by Margaret Randall:

> "I asked that man, right there in front of all those people, if after twenty years of revolutionary militancy they'd just discovered that I can think." — Doris Tijerino, testimonio quoted by Katy Evans from Margaret Randall's *Sandino's Daughters Revisited*, *Burkhardt Review* 2(2), 2019. [Source: openjournals.bsu.edu](https://openjournals.bsu.edu/burkhardtreview/article/download/1601/987/2255)

## The war and the World Court

The Contra war produced unusual evidence in the judgment of the International Court of Justice, the United Nations' principal court, of 27 June 1986 in *Nicaragua v. United States*. The Court found it established that the United States authorised the mining of El Bluff, Corinto and Puerto Sandino in early 1984 without warning to shipping, and largely financed, trained, armed and organised the FDN, the main Contra force. It rejected collective self-defence, found breaches of customary international law including non-intervention, and set out Common Article 3 of the 1949 Geneva Conventions as a minimum standard.

A former CIA officer, David MacMichael, gave oral evidence that significant arms shipments from Nicaragua to Salvadorean rebels over a long period could not have escaped detection while he served as a senior estimates officer between 1981 and 1983:

> "In any significant manner over this long period of time I do not believe they could have done so." — David MacMichael, former CIA officer, oral evidence September 1985, printed in the ICJ judgment of 27 June 1986, para. 134. The webfetch text shows an OCR glyph error ("1" for "I") here normalised. [Source: icj-cij.org](https://www.icj-cij.org/node/103143)

He qualified his answer about late 1980 and early 1981. Edgar Chamorro, a former FDN leader who left at the end of 1984, swore an affidavit describing CIA operatives directing the force; the Court's summary reads:

> "Mr. Chamorro attributes virtually a power of command to the CIA operatives: he refers to them as having 'ordered' or 'instructed' the FDN" — International Court of Justice, judgment of 27 June 1986, para. 104, summarising Edgar Chamorro's affidavit. [Source: icj-cij.org](https://www.icj-cij.org/node/103143)

He described pressure to seize territory in 1982 and 1983, changes of tactics against farms, supplies of aircraft, and the despatch of "UCLAs" from a CIA mother ship while the FDN claimed responsibility. These are claims tested in a legal forum, but the United States withdrew from the merits phase, its witnesses did not appear, and the Court noted that:

> "the evidence of the witnesses presented by the Applicant at the hearings was not tested by cross-examination" — International Court of Justice, judgment of 27 June 1986, para. 67. [Source: icj-cij.org](https://www.icj-cij.org/node/103143)

It treated government testimony with reserve. The judgment is a finding of state responsibility, not a full history of the war.

## The vote and the memory

The election of 4 November 1984 was the clearest conflict in the record. A Latin American Studies Association delegation, self-funded but accredited by the Supreme Electoral Council, observed it. In four days 93.7 percent of the estimated voting-age population registered, about 75 percent of registered voters cast ballots, the FSLN won 63 percent of votes cast and 67 percent of valid votes, and opposition parties took 36.5 percent of Assembly seats. The observers reported:

> "We observed no evidence of irregularities in the voting or vote-counting process." — Latin American Studies Association observer delegation, report of 19 November 1984. [Source: williamgbecker.com](http://www.williamgbecker.com/lasa_1984.pdf)

They judged the opposition boycott a deliberate choice, and found that the governing party had also used its position unfairly:

> "The FSLN took substantial advantage of its incumbent position and, in some ways, abused it." — Latin American Studies Association observer delegation, 19 November 1984. [Source: williamgbecker.com](http://www.williamgbecker.com/lasa_1984.pdf)

An electoral official recalled the scale:

> "We've done it all in only eight months," said Roberto Everstz. "In a normal situation all this would have required up to three years." — Roberto Everstz, CSE official, quoted by Envío, January 1990, recalling October 1984. [Source: revistaenvio.org](https://www.revistaenvio.org/articulo/2578)

There were 3,892 polling places and 40,000 trained volunteers; sixteen could not open or function normally because of Contra activity. The government paper *Barricada* reported mass, orderly turnout above 80 percent, while an opposition candidate quoted in a later hostile compilation described thin crowds in Managua. Geography reconciles part of the difference, since forty registration centres could not open in contested zones, but the disagreement is real. The delegation had accepted credentials from the council it assessed, and both papers were partisan.

Much of this testimony was given after the events. The Harnecker interviews came within a few years, the literacy memoirs twenty later, the oral histories thirty or forty; testimonio was a political genre shaped by interviewers and by the need to speak to an international audience, and retrospective accounts carry the pressure of the Ortega-Murillo authoritarian turn. A revolution that claimed legitimacy through literacy and elections can be heard arguing its case here, with the claims of its opponents and the court beside it; none can recover the private motives of those who lived through it.

## How historians have read it

1. **Martin Carnoy and Carlos Alberto Torres, *Education and Social Transformation in Nicaragua 1979–1989* (1990).** A study of education as part of revolutionary transformation, with the literacy campaign as a central case; broadly sympathetic to its educational aims and useful as a baseline, not as a neutral verdict. [DOI: 315](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400860692.315)
2. **Katy Evans, "Construyendo la Mujer Nueva: The Image and Reality of the Revolutionary 'New Woman'" (2019).** Argues from comics, testimonios and women's accounts that the FSLN's formal equality was not matched in practice. [DOI: BR.2.2.65 81](https://doi.org/10.33043/BR.2.2.65-81)
3. **Rafael Santiago Meza Duriez, "'It stays with me.' Mística, struggle and lifelong learning during the Nicaraguan Literacy Crusade" (2026).** Based on life-history interviews with four former brigadistas; finds that the campaign's revolutionary "mística" combined compliance with agency and later disillusionment. [DOI: 2026.2688775](https://doi.org/10.1080/03057925.2026.2688775)
4. **John Beverley and Marc Zimmerman, *Literature and Politics in the Central American Revolutions* (University of Texas Press, 1990).** A study of testimonio and revolutionary culture, important for judging how much of the above is a genre with conventions. [Source: blogs.bodleian.ox.ac.uk](https://blogs.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/taylorian/2016/12/02/the-pring-mill-collection-nicaragua-part-iii/)
5. **Maxine Molyneux, "The Politics of Abortion in Nicaragua: Revolutionary Pragmatism, or Feminism in the Realm of Necessity?", *Feminist Review* 29 (Summer 1988).** Argues that revolutionary pragmatism limited feminist gains; agrees broadly with Evans about the gap between promise and outcome.
6. **Ulrike Hanemann, "Nicaragua's Literacy Campaign" (UNESCO, 2006).** An assessment for the EFA Global Monitoring Report, cited here as an international source for the literacy figures. [Source: clacsberkeley.blog](https://clacsberkeley.blog/2025/06/11/she-taught-me-trova-i-learned-about-the-revolution-and-education/)

Broad agreement exists on the scale of the literacy campaign and on United States involvement with the Contras; the Court's finding and international assessments point the same way. Live disagreement concerns the fairness of the 1984 election, the scale of Contra abuses, the degree of CIA control over Counter-revolutionary tactics, and whether women's gains were substantive or formal. Those disputes should be presented with their evidence, not averaged out.

## Limits of the record

The surviving testimony is uneven. FSLN leaders are well represented; ordinary militants, peasants, Miskito and Sumo people of the Atlantic coast, and rank-and-file Contras are largely absent. Women appear in several sources, but two of the three oral histories were recorded decades later and one uses a pseudonym. Casualty and literacy figures come mainly from government or movement sources. The Front's founding documents of the early 1960s were not available, so Ortega's retrospective account is the nearest substitute; the Tricontinental influence cannot yet be documented from its own texts. The Court's verbatim hearing records and Chamorro's full affidavit were not opened, so those witnesses are known only through the judgment, an OCR text with damaged spacing. No physical archive was visited and Spanish sources need checking against printed editions.

The record cannot establish private motives, exact casualty totals, or how far the CIA controlled day-to-day Counter-revolutionary tactics beyond the instances the Court found proved. It cannot say what the insurrection felt like to people who did not take part, or what the war cost those who bore it without leaving testimony. Credible accounts exist in Spanish-language archives and in collections not opened here, and would have to be consulted before the participant record could be called complete.
