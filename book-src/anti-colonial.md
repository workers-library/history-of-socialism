On the morning of 10 February 1927, in the Palais d'Egmont in Brussels, a delegate from the Chinese Nationalist Party, or Kuomintang, addressed a congress that had gathered anticolonial activists from across the world. Liao Huanxing told the hall: "Our relations to the imperialists to-day are so critical, that the latter are using all means with which to attack us. We must form ourselves strongly and firmly into an international united front against world imperialism." (Liao Huanxing, Kuomintang, Brussels, 10 February 1927, as translated in Inprecorr. [Source: revolutionsnewsstand.com](https://revolutionsnewsstand.com/2024/03/04/gainst-colonial-oppression-in-brussels-from-international-press-correspondence-vol-7-no-16-february-25-1927)) A directory of delegates listed thirty Chinese, Koreans, Indo-Chinese and Dutch East Indians, alongside Africans and Latin Americans. A reporter for the New York *Daily Worker* called the gathering "the long-hoped-for and long-dreaded coming together of oppressed peoples with workers of the oppressing nations": "The long-hoped-for and long-dreaded coming together of oppressed peoples with workers of the oppressing nations has become a world-wide reality." (Manuel Gomez, *Daily Worker*, 10 March 1927. [Source: revolutionsnewsstand.com](https://revolutionsnewsstand.com/2025/10/07/delegates-from-subject-nations-and-brussels-planned-freedom-by-manuel-gomez-from-the-daily-worker-vol-4-no-48-march-10-1927)) To see how such a room could exist, it is necessary to go back to the years just after the First World War, when two different ways of linking socialism to anticolonial politics were being worked out.

## The colonial question enters the programme

The Communist International, the body founded in 1919 to coordinate communist parties, put the colonies on its agenda at its second congress in 1920. Lenin's draft theses of 5 June placed oppressed and oppressor nations at the centre of the argument: "The real meaning of the demand for equality consists in its being a demand for the abolition of classes." (V. I. Lenin, draft theses, 5 June 1920, translation by Julius Katzer. [Marxists Internet Archive: jun 05](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1920/jun/05.htm)) He called for support for bourgeois-democratic liberation movements in backward countries while insisting that communists keep their own organisation, and he warned against Pan-Islamism. The argument that super-profits from the colonies helped sustain reformism among workers in the imperialist countries belongs to Lenin's *Imperialism* and to Roy's supplementary theses, not to the draft theses of 5 June. "The Communist International must enter into a temporary alliance with bourgeois democracy in the colonial and backward countries" (V. I. Lenin, draft theses, 5 June 1920, translation by Julius Katzer. [Marxists Internet Archive: jun 05](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1920/jun/05.htm)), but only on those terms.

The text was contested from the start. M. N. Roy, an Indian revolutionary who had helped found communist parties in India and Mexico, put supplementary theses on 25 July from the standpoint of India and the other large Asian peoples: "The super-profits made in the colonies form one of the main sources of the resources of contemporary capitalism." (M. N. Roy, supplementary theses, 25 July 1920, translator unnamed in the edition. [Source: redsails.org](https://redsails.org/theses-on-the-national-and-colonial-questions)) Roy wanted communists to build workers' and peasants' organisations rather than follow the national bourgeoisie, the native middle class that led the independence movements. Lenin called his theses important. The two documents therefore record an argument inside the International, not a single settled line. They come to us in translations of differing provenance, and the edition that prints Roy's text carries its own political commentary; the wording must not be merged with the Soviet-published translation of Lenin's draft.

The theses also reached people far from the congress. Hồ Chí Minh recalled in 1960 how, as a young man in Paris, he had joined the French Socialist Party and asked which International sided with colonial peoples: "If you do not condemn colonialism, if you do not side with the colonial people, what kind of revolution are you waging?" (Hồ Chí Minh, 1960, recalling the 1920s, translator unnamed. [Source: redsails.org](https://redsails.org/the-path-which-led-me-to-leninism/)) He said a comrade gave him Lenin's theses, printed in *L'Humanité*, and that reading them repeatedly showed him the way; he then voted at Tours for affiliation to the Third International. His account was written forty years later for a Soviet commemoration, and the arc from uncertainty to certainty suits the occasion, but it shows what the documents meant to one reader.

## Petitioning the powers

At the same moment W. E. B. Du Bois was pursuing a different method. His memorandum of 1 January 1919, printed in *The Crisis* two months later, designed a Pan-African congress as a pressure group at the Paris Peace Conference. He proposed hearers of testimony, statements from the great powers, representation of 250 million people of African descent in the League of Nations, and a permanent secretariat. Its programme called for "Development of autonomous government along lines of native custom, with the object of inaugurating gradually an Africa for the Africans." (W. E. B. Du Bois, memorandum, 1 January 1919. [Source: dareyoufight.org](https://www.dareyoufight.org/Volumes/17/05/memorandum_to_m_diagne_and_others_on_a_panafrican_congress.html))

The first congress met in Paris in 1919. Du Bois, who organised it, wrote weeks afterwards that Clemenceau's government had granted permission while American and British authorities blocked delegates, and that the congress petitioned the Peace Conference: "As it was the Congress cost $750. Yet with this meagre sum a Congress of fifty-eight delegates, representing sixteen different Negro groups, was assembled." (W. E. B. Du Bois, "My Mission", May 1919. [Source: dareyoufight.org](https://www.dareyoufight.org/Volumes/18/01/my_mission.html)) Its resolution on equal rights was carefully qualified: "Wherever persons of African descent are civilized and able to meet the tests of surrounding culture, they shall be accorded the same rights as their fellow citizens" (resolution of the first Pan-African Congress, reproduced by W. E. B. Du Bois, May 1919. [Source: dareyoufight.org](https://www.dareyoufight.org/Volumes/18/01/my_mission.html)). By the fourth congress, held in New York in 1927, Du Bois was presenting a chronology of four meetings and printing resolutions that demanded land, self-rule in Africa and the withdrawal of American troops from Haiti. "The attitude of all the imperial powers who own Africa is fundamentally wrong. They are seeking profit, not men; they want trade and industry more than civilization and spiritual uplift." (Fourth Pan-African Congress resolutions, reproduced by W. E. B. Du Bois, October 1927. [Source: dareyoufight.org](https://www.dareyoufight.org/Volumes/34/08/panafrican_congresses.html)) Another resolution struck a different note: "We thank the Soviet Government of Russia for its liberal attitude toward the colored races and for the help which it has extended to them from time to time." (Fourth Pan-African Congress resolutions, reproduced by W. E. B. Du Bois, October 1927. [Source: dareyoufight.org](https://www.dareyoufight.org/Volumes/34/08/panafrican_congresses.html)) These are the organiser's own accounts, with no dissenting voices, and the reformist wording of some resolutions sits uneasily beside the language of Brussels five months earlier.

## The hall in Brussels

The congress of February 1927 lasted nine sessions and adopted ten resolutions unanimously. Nehru chaired a session and read an Indian resolution; Senghor spoke for the Negro Defence Committee, Gumede for the Zulus, Hatta for Indonesia, and an Indian and a Chinese delegate issued a joint declaration. The credentials committee counted 174 delegates from 37 countries. Manuel Gomez, writing for the Communist Party paper in New York, put the number at "upwards of 180" from "no less than 40", and later scholarship has counted 174 people from 134 organisations in 34 countries. The two precise counts agree on 174 people; they differ over the denominator (37 countries, 34 countries, 134 organisations, or 31 states, colonies and regions). Gomez's 'upwards of 180' is an approximate first-day press estimate datelined 11 February, before the credentials report. These are not three conflicting headcounts. Gomez reported A. Fenner Brockway of the Independent Labour Party saying: "If war breaks out, we shall be on the side of China, and against England." (A. Fenner Brockway, Brussels, 10 February 1927, as reported by Manuel Gomez. [Source: revolutionsnewsstand.com](https://revolutionsnewsstand.com/2025/10/07/delegates-from-subject-nations-and-brussels-planned-freedom-by-manuel-gomez-from-the-daily-worker-vol-4-no-48-march-10-1927))

The congress founded a permanent League Against Imperialism and for National Independence. Its manifesto named Amritsar, Wanhsien and Netherlands India among recent outrages, appealed not only to colonial peoples but to peasants, intellectuals and the middle classes of the imperialist countries, and declared: "Under these conditions, this Brussels conference has decided to found the 'League Against Imperialism and for National Independence'." (manifesto of the Brussels congress, 1927, translator unnamed. [Source: revolutionsnewsstand.com](https://revolutionsnewsstand.com/2023/12/02/7-no-17-march-3-1927)) It closed with a call that gave this chapter its title: "Oppressed Peoples and Oppressed Nations, Unite!" (closing line of the Brussels manifesto, 1927. [Source: revolutionsnewsstand.com](https://revolutionsnewsstand.com/2023/12/02/7-no-17-march-3-1927)) An executive was elected that included Nehru, Liao, Senghor, Hatta and the organisers Münzenberg and Fimmen. But the report came from Inprecorr, the International's own bulletin, which stressed pro-Soviet speeches and the success of the congress, and Gomez wrote for a party paper. The coalition was short-lived. After 1928 the International turned toward expelling social democrats and bourgeois nationalists; the League suffered a police raid in 1933, moved to London and dissolved in 1937. Historians disagree about whether this was a defeat imposed from Moscow or the exhaustion of a coalition that had always been fragile.

## Workers, and a name for the world

The Profintern, the Red International of Labour Unions, a separate revolutionary trade-union body, pressed the worker-centred strategy. George Padmore, a Trinidadian communist who ran its committee for Black workers, set out the case in a pamphlet of 1931. On South Africa he marshalled the Native Land Act of 1913, the pass laws, the colour bar and wage differentials: "Politically the country is under the complete domination of English and Boer imperialists who exploit the native population in the most brutal manner." (George Padmore, 1931. [Marxists Internet Archive: negro toilers ch01](https://www.marxists.org/archive/padmore/1931/negro-toilers/ch01.htm)) He quoted governors in East Africa on taxation as a way of forcing Africans into wage work. He claimed the Profintern was "The Profintern is the first real International of Trade Unions, because the workers of all nationalities and races, regardless of colour or creed, have rallied to its banner." (George Padmore, 1931. [Marxists Internet Archive: negro toilers ch06](https://www.marxists.org/archive/padmore/1931/negro-toilers/ch06.htm)) Its programme attacked Garveyism and called for unions built with white workers in the imperialist countries. Padmore denounced Du Bois and other Black leaders as reformists. This was a factional document: its judgements were political attacks, its figures were assembled for a multiplying argument, and a table in the transcription contains an evident error whose number is not repeated here. Padmore later broke with the International, so the pamphlet records a position he abandoned rather than a settled view.

The vocabulary used to describe this world came later, and from an unexpected quarter. In *L'Observateur* on 14 August 1952 the French demographer Alfred Sauvy wrote of two worlds confronting each other and a third, the most important, made up of the underdeveloped countries. Both blocs competed for it, and the Cold War froze social change. He argued that cheap medical technology had broken the old cycle of mortality without the investment needed to sustain it, and he compared the third world to the Third Estate of the French Revolution: "Car enfin ce Tiers Monde ignoré, exploité, méprisé comme le Tiers Etat, veut, lui aussi, être quelque chose." (Alfred Sauvy, 14 August 1952. [Source: homme-moderne.org](http://homme-moderne.org/societe/demo/sauvy/3mondes.html); translated in the source as: "For after all, this Third World, ignored, exploited, despised like the Third Estate, wants, too, to be something.") A note by Sauvy says he had used the phrase "three worlds" in a Brazilian review in 1951 but first wrote "Tiers Monde" in this article. He was a liberal demographer, not an anticolonial activist; the tone is paternalistic and there is no anticolonial voice in the essay. It is a convenient point of origin for a term, not a theory of liberation.

## Limits of the record

Almost all the surviving English text is mediated. The Brussels speeches reach us through Inprecorr or the *Daily Worker*, in translations whose originals and translators are usually unnamed, and the digital editions used here are transcriptions rather than the scans of the original issues. French, German, Chinese, Indonesian and Vietnamese sources were not read, and the League's central papers at the International Institute of Social History in Amsterdam, along with the Nehru Archive and the Du Bois Papers, were not consulted. Several texts that matter most, including C. L. R. James's *The Black Jacobins* and the International's 1928 colonial theses, were available only as documents that could not be opened. The testimony is uneven and politically shaped. Delegates, organisers and pamphleteers are well represented; the peasants, mineworkers and women they claimed to speak for are barely audible. Padmore's figures were compiled for a polemic, Sauvy's essay contains no anticolonial voices, Ho Chi Minh's recollection was written for a Soviet anniversary, and the manifesto's claims about world opinion were never tested. None of these sources can show how far the League changed events in any colony, and the material here does not support a claim that it did. The biographies of Du Bois, Ford and Ho Chi Minh, and the later history of Pan-African and Third World politics, belong to other chapters; this account follows only the organisations and the arguments.

## How historians have read it

1. Michele Louro, Carolien Stolte, Heather Streets-Salter and Sana Tannoury-Karam, eds., *The League Against Imperialism: Lives and Afterlives* (Amsterdam University Press, 2020). The opening chapter argues that the League deserves study in its own right as a meeting place for people of very different politics; the volume has chapters on Latin America, Algeria, the Arab Levant, China, India, South Africa and the "Third World project". It treats the 1927 congress as a contact zone rather than a Comintern puppet. [Cambridge University Press: 576E35EF7EA85D98EF071D70ABAA6770](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/league-against-imperialism/league-against-imperialism-lives-and-afterlives/576E35EF7EA85D98EF071D70ABAA6770)
2. Disha Karnad Jani, "The league against imperialism, national liberation, and the economic question", *Journal of Global History* 17(2), 2022, pp. 210–232. Argues that the League's distinctive politics lay in linking political independence to control of land, labour and resources; documents the 1928 shift, the 1933 police raid, the move to London and the 1937 dissolution. Open access: [political independence to control of land, labour and resources; documents the 1928 shift](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-global-history/article/league-against-imperialism-national-liberation-and-the-economic-question/1B8A2D985A9C75A5063CF947D60DA7EC)
3. Fredrik Petersson's two-volume study of the League, described by Jani (2022) as examining Willi Münzenberg's role and the group's operations from 1925 to 1933 from extensive Comintern archive research. This is the archival counterweight to the more interpretive chapters above; its exact titles and dates should be checked before citation.
4. Charles Forsdick and Christian Høgsbjerg, eds., *The Black Jacobins Reader* (Duke University Press, 2017). Contains new scholarship and rare primary documents on James's 1938 book, its production and its place in debates about decolonisation and anti-colonial theory: [ss, 2017). Contains new scholarship and rare primary documents on James's 1938 book](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv11smtct)
5. Domenico Losurdo, *Class Struggle: A Political and Philosophical History* (2016). Argues that the Third International did not live up to its programme and that the anti-colonial revolutions of the later twentieth century fused social and national conflict in ways its founders had not anticipated; a corrective to treating 1920 as the moment the theory was finished.
