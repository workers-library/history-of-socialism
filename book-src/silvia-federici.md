Silvia Federici is an Italian-born Marxist feminist whose writing put unpaid women's work at the centre of how capitalism sustains itself. Three linked ideas carry through her work. Social reproduction is the everyday work of bearing and raising children, cooking, cleaning and caring for the sick and elderly that keeps a workforce fed and renewed; Federici argues that most of it is done by women without pay. Primitive accumulation is the violent process by which capitalism first gathered wealth, driving people off the land, colonising other countries and enslaving people, and she argues that it never really stopped. The commons are resources such as land, forests and water that a community holds and governs together rather than as private property. Her best-known book, *Caliban and the Witch*, argues that the witch-hunts of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries were part of that process. This profile sets out what she claimed, what can be checked in her published texts, and where historians and other socialists objected.

## Biography

Federici was born in Parma, Italy, in 1942. In an interview with Rachel Andrews for *The White Review* she recalled that her earliest years were spent on a farm because Parma was being bombed at night, that her mother was pregnant with her sister when Mussolini declared war, and that her father, a philosophy teacher, was stopped by a Nazi squad in 1943 and released after giving his profession. She described Parma as a centre of anti-fascist resistance and said a communist high-school teacher was one of her strongest early influences. She read for a degree in modern languages and literature, then enrolled in philosophy; a summer school in Edinburgh in 1966 led American students to urge her to apply to Buffalo, and she went to the University of Buffalo in 1967 on a Fulbright scholarship, stating that she wanted to leave the restrictions of her family in Italy ([White Review interview](https://www.thewhitereview.org/feature/interview-with-silvia-federici)). The finding aid for her papers at Brown University records that her doctorate at the State University of New York at Buffalo was titled *The Development of Lukács' Realism*; the dissertation itself was not opened for this profile. Hofstra's faculty profile confirms a PhD and MA from SUNY Buffalo and a BA from the Università degli Studi di Bologna ([RIAMCO finding aid](https://www.riamco.org/render?eadid=US-RPB-ms2017.023&view=biography); [Hofstra faculty profile](https://www.hofstra.edu/faculty-staff/faculty-profile.html?id=442)).

She travelled to Mexico in 1968 shortly after the Tlatelolco massacre and wrote her first article in English about it. She moved to New York in 1970, began organising with feminist groups in Buffalo in 1970–71, and read Mariarosa Dalla Costa's *Women and the Subversion of the Community* (1971), which she calls the foundation document of the Wages for Housework campaign. She attended the Padua meeting in 1972 at which the International Feminist Collective was formed and worked with the New York Wages for Housework Committee from 1972 to 1977. In the White Review interview she described opening a small storefront in Brooklyn in autumn 1975, and said the committee dissolved two years after New York City's 1975 bankruptcy and the austerity that followed ([White Review interview](https://www.thewhitereview.org/feature/interview-with-silvia-federici); [Viewpoint interview](https://viewpointmag.com/2015/04/15/witchtales-an-interview-with-silvia-federici); [Interference Archive event record](https://interferencearchive.org/event/wages-for-housework-a-conversation-with-silvia-federici/)).

In the preface to *Caliban and the Witch* she writes that shortly after the 1984 publication of *Il Grande Calibano* she left the United States for a teaching post in Nigeria and remained nearly three years. She dates the decisive period to 1984–86, when the debt crisis and negotiations with the IMF and World Bank brought structural adjustment, what she calls a "War Against Indiscipline", and a campaign denouncing women's "vanity and excessive demands". She credits the encounter with Women in Nigeria, the country's first feminist organisation, with helping her understand Nigerian women's defence of their resources ([Caliban and the Witch, Preface](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/silvia-federici-caliban-and-the-witch)). Institutional biographies place her at the University of Port Harcourt and record that she co-founded the Committee for Academic Freedom in Africa in 1991 and helped found the Radical Philosophy Association's anti-death-penalty project in the mid-1990s ([Leuphana fellow profile](https://www.leuphana.de/en/research-centers/cdc/people/fellows/silvia-federici.html); [PM Press author page](https://blog.pmpress.org/authors-artists-comrades/silvia-federici/)).

PM Press and other institutional hosts give her teaching at Hofstra University from 1987 to 2005; Hofstra lists her as Professor Emerita of New College, while several sources call her Professor Emerita of Social Sciences. She lives in Brooklyn and is married to the philosopher George Caffentzis, her frequent collaborator. Her personal and professional papers, dated 1922–2023 with the bulk from 1970 to 2023, are held at Brown University's John Hay Library as the Silvia Federici papers (Ms.2017.023), in fifteen series covering biographical material, career development, correspondence, Wages for Housework, and printed material ([Hofstra faculty profile](https://www.hofstra.edu/faculty-staff/faculty-profile.html?id=442); [Brown University Library collection record](https://library.brown.edu/collatoz/info.php?id=554); [RIAMCO finding aid](https://www.riamco.org/render?eadid=US-RPB-ms2017.023&view=scope)).

## Intellectual tradition

Federici writes from an autonomist Marxist feminist current rather than from a party or state socialist tradition. In the Viewpoint interview she says her feminism was "an amalgam" of the Italian workers'-autonomy and unemployed movements, the anti-colonial movement, the United States civil rights and Black Power movements, and the National Welfare Rights Movement, a largely Black movement of women demanding state support for their children. The Brown finding aid likewise describes her scholarship as developing from the 1960s anti-colonial, civil rights, student and autonomist Marxist movements. She was a member of the Midnight Notes Collective, whose 1990 publication *The New Enclosures* framed a new planetary wave of accumulation, and she co-founded the Committee for Academic Freedom in Africa with George Caffentzis ([Viewpoint interview](https://viewpointmag.com/2015/04/15/witchtales-an-interview-with-silvia-federici); [RIAMCO finding aid](https://www.riamco.org/render?eadid=US-RPB-ms2017.023&view=scope)).

Her relation to existing feminist and Marxist schools is critical. In the preface to *Caliban and the Witch* she says she rejected radical feminism for explaining patriarchy through transhistorical cultural structures independent of production and class, and rejected socialist feminism for treating women primarily as waged workers while failing to see reproduction as a source of value and exploitation. Her own position, built from Dalla Costa and Selma James, is that the exploitation of women is central to capitalist accumulation because women produce and reproduce labour-power. She names the resulting regime "the patriarchy of the wage": the male wage commands women's unwaged reproductive work. Against Marxist orthodoxy that treated women's subordination as a feudal residue, she treats sexual hierarchy as historically produced inside capitalism. She also sets herself against traditions that celebrate capitalism as a progressive force: in the introduction she says she does not accept that Marx's account of primitive accumulation was a necessary step towards human liberation when viewed from the lives of women.

Her use of Michel Foucault is similarly double-edged. She takes from him the idea that power works through the body and repeats his term "Great Confinement", but argues in *Caliban and the Witch* that his history of sexuality omitted the witch-hunt, ignored sexual difference and treated the body as constituted by discourse without identifying the source of power. She argues that his account of bio-power makes sense only when placed in the context of the capitalist need to accumulate and reproduce labour-power. She is not writing as a neutral historian of ideas: her stated purpose is to recover a memory of resistance she thinks necessary for finding an alternative to capitalism ([Caliban and the Witch, Introduction](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/silvia-federici-caliban-and-the-witch)).

## Major contributions

The first contribution is the concept of social reproduction as a central terrain of class struggle. In *Wages Against Housework* she argues that housework is not a personal service but work that produces the workforce, and that calling it love conceals its economic function. Her central claim in that pamphlet is that the demand for a wage is a political perspective rather than a request for a larger income: "To say that we want money for housework is the first step towards refusing to do it, because the demand for a wage makes our work visible". She extends this in *Revolution at Point Zero*, a collection of essays written between 1974 and 2016, to the international restructuring of reproductive work, the globalisation of care work and sex work, the crisis of elder care, affective labour and the politics of the commons ([Wages Against Housework, full text](https://libcom.org/library/wages-against-housework-silvia-federici); [PM Press, *Revolution at Point Zero*, second edition](https://pmpress.org/product/revolution-at-point-zero-housework-reproduction-and-feminist-struggle-second-edition)).

The second contribution is a feminist rewriting of primitive accumulation. In the introduction to *Caliban and the Witch* she says her account departs from Marx by adding three phenomena absent from his version: a new sexual division of labour that subordinates women's reproductive function to the reproduction of the workforce, a patriarchal order built on women's exclusion from waged work, and the mechanisation of the proletarian body, which for women became a machine for producing new workers. She places the witch-hunts of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries at the centre of that process, arguing that the persecution of witches in Europe and the New World was as important for capitalism as colonisation and the expropriation of the European peasantry from its land. She also argues that the most violent features of primitive accumulation return in every phase of capitalist globalisation rather than receding with capitalist maturity.

The third contribution is the claim that the body is for women what the factory is for the male waged worker: "the primary ground of their exploitation and resistance". In *Beyond the Periphery of the Skin*, published by PM Press/Kairos in January 2020 and built on three lectures given in San Francisco in 2015, she applies that framework to contemporary body politics, reproductive technologies, state violence and the "reproduction crisis" generated by the neoliberal turn ([PM Press, *Beyond the Periphery of the Skin*](https://pmpress.org/product/beyond-the-periphery-of-the-skin-rethinking-remaking-and-reclaiming-the-body-in-contemporary-capitalism)).

The fourth contribution is a feminist politics of the commons. With George Caffentzis she published "Commons against and beyond capitalism" in *Community Development Journal* in 2014, contrasting commons with capitalist relations and warning that commons can be co-opted to supply low-cost reproduction unless that danger is actively resisted. In *Re-enchanting the World*, published by PM Press/Kairos in November 2018 with a foreword by Peter Linebaugh, she argues that reproductive work is the material basis of life and the first terrain on which people can practise self-government; that phrasing is reported in a scholarly blog of a 2014 talk rather than checked against the book itself ([Oxford Academic record for the 2014 article](https://academic.oup.com/cdj/article/49/suppl_1/i92/307214); [PM Press, *Re-enchanting the World*](https://pmpress.org/product/re-enchanting-the-world-feminism-and-the-politics-of-the-commons); [Autonomies report](https://autonomies.org/2014/06/the-politics-of-the-commons-silvia-federici/)).

## Analysis of capitalism

Federici treats capitalism as a system that must continually create and enforce inequality rather than one that gradually fulfils a productive promise. In *Caliban and the Witch* she calls capitalism "the counter-revolution that destroyed the possibilities that had emerged from the anti-feudal struggle" and insists that the violence of its origin was not a one-off prelude but a recurring condition. Her account of the transition begins with the peasant struggles and commons of late medieval Europe, moves through land privatisation, the price revolution, poor relief and the criminalisation of the poor, and arrives at the sexual division of labour and the "patriarchy of the wage". The wage itself is, in her analysis, a mechanism of hierarchy: it recognises the male worker as a worker while hiding the unwaged labour that sustains him and produces the next generation of workers.

Her best-known claim is that the witch-hunt was a mechanism of capitalist development. In *Caliban and the Witch* she writes: "What has not been recognized is that the witch-hunt was one of the most important events in the development of capitalist society and the formation of the modern proletariat." She argues that the campaign of terror weakened peasant resistance to gentry and state assaults at a moment when the peasant community was already disintegrating under land privatisation, taxation and state control; that it "deepened the divisions between women and men"; and that it destroyed practices and subjects incompatible with capitalist work discipline, "thus redefining the main elements of social reproduction". She says the persecution of witches occurred at the same time as the colonisation and extermination of the populations of the New World, the English enclosures, the beginning of the slave trade and the "bloody laws" against vagabonds, and she rejects the Enlightenment idea that the witch-hunt was the last spark of a dying feudal world. She rejects a simple explanation in terms of greed too, noting that the goods of accused women, who were mostly poor, could not compare with the riches of the Americas.

In an interview with Verónica Gago she states the same argument in compressed form: "Witch-hunts were instrumental to the construction of a patriarchal order in which the bodies of women, their work, and their sexual and reproductive powers were placed under the control of the State and transformed into economic resources." She compares the accusation of witchcraft to the legal category of treason and, in our own age, terrorism, because its vagueness allowed it to punish any form of protest. She says that "in the same way that the enclosures expropriated communal lands from the peasantry, witch-hunts expropriated bodies from women". These are her claims; the scholarly objections to them are recorded below.

She extends the argument to the present. In *Witches, Witch-Hunting, and Women* (PM Press, October 2018) she describes contemporary violence against women as a structural element of new forms of capitalist accumulation, rooted in enclosure, land dispossession and the remoulding of women's reproductive activity. In the White Review interview she links accusations of witchcraft in parts of Africa to women's access to land, competition over scarce resources and collaboration between local chiefs and mining or oil companies, and discusses witch-detention camps in northern Ghana. With Alice Markham-Cantor she wrote in *Scientific American* in 2023 that sustained periods of witch-hunting share a backdrop of social and economic dislocation, citing her own 2004 book and later publications. That article was located through a search result rather than opened, and is listed with that limitation ([PM Press, *Witches, Witch-Hunting, and Women*](https://pmpress.org/product/witches-witch-hunting-and-women); [White Review interview](https://www.thewhitereview.org/feature/interview-with-silvia-federici); [Scientific American article](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/how-social-turmoil-has-increased-witch-hunts-throughout-history)).

## Analysis of socialism

Federici does not offer a blueprint for a socialist state, and no such blueprint should be attributed to her. Her preferred institutions are built from below: commons, self-governing communities of equals, collective control over the means of both production and reproduction, and the redistribution of reproductive work through struggle rather than through state provision alone. In *Wages Against Housework* she distinguishes between asking the state to organise collective services and communities organising them themselves and then demanding that the state pay for them. She warns that handing children to the state for fifteen or twenty-four hours a day extends state control rather than freeing women. In the Viewpoint interview she says the demand for a wage was a strategy to change power relations between women and capital, not a claim that waged work was the goal, and describes the struggle for domestic work as a central anticapitalist struggle.

Her most systematic statement of a postcapitalist institution is the commons. In the 2014 article with Caffentzis, commons are described as seeds of a society beyond state and market, with an explicit warning about co-optation as a low-cost welfare substitute. In "Feminism and the Politics of the Commons", published in the *FORMER WEST* volume in 2016, she writes from the standpoint of "the planet's commoners" and argues that women, as the primary subjects of reproductive work, have depended most on communal natural resources and suffered most from their privatisation ([FORMER WEST PDF](https://archive2.bakonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/FEDERICI_Feminism-and-the-Politics-of-the-Commons_FORMER-WEST-2016.pdf)). The publication of *Revolution at Point Zero* was widely read as an argument that reproductive work is "ground zero" of revolutionary practice; Brown's finding aid uses that phrasing, and it appears in a scholarly report of her 2014 Madrid talk ([RIAMCO finding aid](https://www.riamco.org/render?eadid=US-RPB-ms2017.023&view=scope); [Autonomies report](https://autonomies.org/2014/06/the-politics-of-the-commons-silvia-federici/)).

What she expects of these institutions is that they break the link between women's unpaid work and the market's supply of labour-power, restore collective control over time, resources and the body, and create forms of cooperation that do not depend on the wage. She remains sceptical of reforms that leave the sexual division of labour intact: in the White Review interview she says that sharing housework between partners changes relations with men but not the relation to capitalism or the devaluation of housework, because no serious social debate has addressed what would replace the thousands of women who currently do that work. The absence of a detailed transitional programme or state-level economic plan is itself a feature of her work and is reported as such.

## Recommended reading

The works below are starting points for her argument, not a measure of her whole output; the full bibliography above lists the rest.

- *Wages Against Housework* (1975) — This is the best starting point for her claim that housework produces the workforce and that demanding a wage makes the work visible as a political issue rather than a request for more income.
- *Caliban and the Witch: Women, the Body and Primitive Accumulation* (2004) — It is the clearest place to see her feminist rewriting of primitive accumulation and her claim that the witch-hunts broke peasant resistance and helped form the modern proletariat.
- *Revolution at Point Zero: Housework, Reproduction, and Feminist Struggle* (2012) — This is where she extends the idea of social reproduction to the international restructuring of care work, sex work, elder care and affective labour, making it the best place to follow the argument into the present.
- "Commons against and beyond capitalism" (2014) — It is the clearest starting point for her postcapitalist institution, since it presents the commons as seeds of a society beyond state and market while warning that they can be co-opted as low-cost welfare provision.
- *Beyond the Periphery of the Skin: Rethinking, Remaking, and Reclaiming the Body in Contemporary Capitalism* (2020) — This is the best entry to her claim that the body is for women what the factory is for the male waged worker, applied here to reproductive technologies, state violence and the reproduction crisis of the neoliberal turn.

## Intellectual influences

Federici names her influences directly in the sources opened. In the preface to *Caliban and the Witch* she identifies Mariarosa Dalla Costa's *Women and the Subversion of the Community* (1971) and Selma James's *Sex, Race and Class* (1975) as the most influential documents for her own position, and credits Leopoldina Fortunati as her collaborator on the research from the mid-1970s and on *Il Grande Calibano*. In the White Review interview she says Dalla Costa's pamphlet became the foundation document of the campaign, that she did not encounter Mary Inman's *In Woman's Defense* (1939) until she was compiling the New York archive, and that Dolores Hayden's *The Grand Domestic Revolution* (1981) supplied the historical precedent of nineteenth-century wages-for-housework demands. She told Verónica Gago that reading Barbara Ehrenreich and Deirdre English's pamphlet *Witches, Midwives & Nurses* (1972) was a "Boom!" moment: it placed the witch-hunts in the sixteenth century and connected them to the slave trade and enclosures.

In the introduction to *Caliban and the Witch* she acknowledges Joan Kelly's essay on the Renaissance, Carolyn Merchant's *The Death of Nature* (1980), Fortunati's *L'Arcano della Riproduzione* (1981), Merry Wiesner's *Working Women in Renaissance Germany* (1986), Maria Mies's *Patriarchy and Accumulation on a World Scale* (1986), Irene Silverblatt's *The Moon, the Sun, and the Witches* (1987), Hilary Beckles's *Natural Rebels* (1995) and Barbara Bush's *Slave Women in Caribbean Society* (1990). She treats Marx's *Capital* as a framework she is revising, Foucault as a theorist she partly adopts and partly criticises, and E.P. Thompson's "Great Confinement" framing via Foucault as a point of comparison. In the White Review she says her political formation also came from anti-fascist resistance in Parma, the student and anti-war movements in the United States, the 1968 Tlatelolco massacre and the Nigerian women's movement she encountered in the 1980s. The Brown finding aid records a doctoral dissertation on Lukács' realism, but no source opened for this profile states how she later judged that early work.

## Influence and legacy

*Caliban and the Witch* is her most widely circulated work. Penguin reissued it as a Modern Classic on 29 July 2021 and describes it as a cult classic; Princeton, the University of Illinois and other hosts report translations into twenty or more languages ([Penguin product page](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/321857/caliban-and-the-witch-by-federici-silvia/9780241532539); [University of Illinois event record](https://calendars.illinois.edu/detail/852?eventId=33479144)). Its reception is not limited to approval. The journal *Gender, Place & Culture* devoted part of its volume 25, issue 9 (2018) to "Interventions in *Caliban and the Witch* and *Revolution at Point Zero*", with contributions by Sutapa Chattopadhyay, Fiona Jeffries and Salvatore Engel-Di Mauro. The 2017 publication of the edited archive *Wages for Housework: The New York Committee 1972–1977* brought the movement's pamphlets, flyers, photographs and internal papers back into circulation, and reviewers in *Labor*, *The Nation* and *Boston Review* treated it as a significant recovery of a neglected strand of 1970s feminism.

Her vocabulary has entered contemporary debates on care, migration and the commons. The White Review interview records the slogan "They say it is love. We say it is unwaged work" being painted on walls in Buenos Aires in 2018 and then erased by police, after which local women reproduced it more widely. Reviews of *Witches, Witch-Hunting, and Women* in *Feminist Review*, *Red Pepper*, *The Sociological Review* and *Anarchist Studies* show the book reaching beyond academic Marxism. A 2025 issue of *Magic, Ritual, and Witchcraft* organised a discussion forum on "Revitalizing the Feminist Historiography of Witchcraft", in which Federici's work was the explicit target of criticism as well as the occasion for reopening the history of witch trials ([Project MUSE issue record](https://muse.jhu.edu/issue/56146)).

## Criticisms and controversies

The most substantial criticism from a historian of early modern Europe is Rita Voltmer's article "Federici's Witches: Old Male Myths in New Feminist Garb?", published in *Magic, Ritual, and Witchcraft* 20(2), Fall 2025, pp. 156–171. The article's existence, authorship and pagination are verified from the Project MUSE table of contents and from Voltmer's own publication list at the University of Trier. Voltmer is a specialist in the history of witch trials: Trier lists her edited *Demonology and Witch-Hunting in Early Modern Europe* (Routledge, 2020) and her 2014 encyclopedia entry "Witch Hunts", and records a 2023 lecture titled "The witches of Silvia Federici or how feministic myths of the 19th and 20th century are surviving". The full text of the 2025 article is behind restricted access and was not opened for this profile. Its arguments are known here only through a February 2026 article by the historian Amy Freeborn, which quotes Voltmer at length; that article is an opinion piece and is not used as evidence for the historical claims themselves. As reported there, Voltmer argues that Federici presents an international conspiracy by church and state to destroy women's knowledge of nature, contraception and sexuality; that this narrative derives largely from Jacob Grimm and Jules Michelet and reached Federici through Matilda Joslyn Gage, Margaret Murray and second-wave feminism; that Federici's account has not been revised in the light of forty years of research; and that current scholarship finds no coordinated conspiracy, shows that women frequently denounced other women, and does not support the claim that midwives and healers were the typical victims. She calls this *Gebrauchsgeschichte*, the political use of historical narrative without regard for accuracy, and concludes that conspiracy myths about the trials abuse the people actually slandered, tortured and killed. This is a reported summary of Voltmer's position, not a substitute for reading the article ([Project MUSE issue record](https://muse.jhu.edu/issue/56146); [University of Trier publication list](https://www.uni-trier.de/universitaet/fachbereiche-faecher/fachbereich-iii/faecher/geschichte/profil/fachgebiete/geschichtliche-landeskunde-1/teamxxx/rita-voltmer/veroeffentlichungen); [Freeborn article](https://freerangehistory.substack.com/p/re-examine-witch-as-symbolic-feminist-figure)).

A second scholarly objection is Ann Ferguson's review in *Wagadu: A Journal of Transnational Women's & Gender Studies* 3 (Spring 2006). Ferguson describes Federici's chapter on the European witch-hunt as taking "a Marxist functionalist point of view" and argues that an alternative functionalist explanation, that the Catholic Church used the hunts to consolidate power against gender-egalitarian heresies, is never adequately dismissed. She also contrasts Federici's account with Carolyn Merchant's and questions whether the explanation is too single-causal. The review's PDF endpoint was located and its text appeared in search results; the fetch tool could not parse the PDF, so this is a search-preview reading rather than a direct opening of the file ([Wagadu review record](https://digitalcommons.cortland.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1108&context=wagadu)).

Other critiques are recorded with their status. Andreas Bieler, a political economist, argued in a 2019 scholarly blog post that Federici ignores local and national particularities and treats enclosures as a uniform European process, invoking Robert Brenner's account of distinct agrarian paths in England, France and eastern Europe. This is a reasoned academic criticism but a blog, not a journal article. Safiyyah Esat's 2024 review in the *Essex Student Journal* praises the book but faults what she calls cherry-picking, a lack of self-awareness about Federici's own political commitments, and insufficient acknowledgement of earlier Marxist scholars; this is a student publication and carries less weight than the Voltmer or Ferguson critiques. A further critique from within the communist left, Gilles Dauvé's "Federici versus Marx", was located on libcom.org but its text was not opened, so no claim is made about its contents. Federici herself concedes a limit of the evidence in *Witches, Witch-Hunting, and Women*, where, according to a review in *The Sociological Review*, she acknowledges that "most of the evidence is circumstantial" for the link between enclosures and witch-hunting. That concession is reported here through the review, which quotes a page number that has not been independently checked ([Bieler post](https://www.ppesydney.net/witch-hunts-and-the-birth-of-capitalism-reflections-on-caliban-and-the-witch); [Essex Student Journal review](https://publications.essex.ac.uk/esj/article/347)).

Bibliographic controversies are minor but real. Institutional speaker biographies variously date *Caliban and the Witch* to 1998, 2004 or 2017 editions, and several invert the title of *Witches, Witch-Hunting, and Women*; publisher records support 2004 and the publisher's title order. Page ranges for the 2008 *Journal of International Women's Studies* article also differ between two opened bibliographies. None of these discrepancies affects the substance of her argument, but they matter for citation ([Kunstinstituut Melly profile](https://www.kunstinstituutmelly.nl/en/people/4439-silvia-federici); [PM Press product page](https://pmpress.org/product/caliban-and-the-witch)).

## Related thinkers

Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James are the closest theoretical predecessors; Federici explicitly builds on their claim that women's unwaged labour produces labour-power, and both appear in the Wages for Housework archive she edited. Leopoldina Fortunati was her co-author on *Il Grande Calibano* and an influence through her own work on reproduction. George Caffentzis is her husband, collaborator on the commons article and fellow member of Midnight Notes. Maria Mies and Vandana Shiva are aligned thinkers on globalisation, ecofeminism and the international division of labour; Federici acknowledges Mies in the introduction to *Caliban and the Witch*, and PM Press reproduces Mies's praise of her work. Peter Linebaugh, a historian and student of E.P. Thompson, wrote the foreword to *Re-enchanting the World* and supplied a blurb for *Caliban and the Witch*; Federici thanks him in the book's acknowledgements. Barbara Ehrenreich and Deirdre English supplied the early pamphlet that directed her to the witch-hunts. Carolyn Merchant and Brian Levack are historians whose explanations she sets against her own; she notes that Levack refrains from a general theory of the witch-hunt and lists legal and institutional preconditions instead. Rita Voltmer, Ann M. Little and Michael Ostling are among the historians whose recent work revises or contests the feminist historiography of witchcraft. Angela Davis and Ellen Meiksins Wood are listed in the same author collection and would be natural cross-links when their profiles exist, but no claim of direct intellectual exchange with either is made here.
