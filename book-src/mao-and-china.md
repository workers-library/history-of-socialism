Mao Zedong led the Communist Party of China (CPC) through a revolution that ended in 1949 and then governed the People's Republic until his death in 1976. His career spans the fall of the Qing dynasty, the wars against Japan and the Nationalists, the building of a socialist state, and two of the twentieth century's largest human catastrophes. Maoism, the body of ideas associated with his name, was developed in the Chinese countryside and later adapted by movements on other continents.

Any account of these years has to say where its evidence comes from. The official narrative is dominated by a document adopted on 27 June 1981, when Deng Xiaoping and his allies were settling the question of how the Party should judge its own past. The Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of Our Party Since the Founding of the People's Republic of China was drafted under Deng and Hu Yaobang, with Hu Qiaomu leading the drafting group. Its communique described the task as "the historic mission of setting to rights things which have been thrown into disorder in the guiding ideology of the Party" ([Communique of the Sixth Plenary Session](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/03.htm)). The Resolution gives dates, figures and verdicts, and it remains the authoritative Party account. It is also a retrospective judgement produced for a political purpose. Deng told the drafters that "affirmation of the historical role of Comrade Mao Zedong and explanation of the necessity to uphold and develop Mao Zedong Thought" was "the most essential point" ([Deng's remarks on successive drafts, 1980-81](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/deng-xiaoping/1981/15.htm)). This chapter uses the Resolution as evidence of what the Party decided to remember, and sets its claims beside the work of historians who have used census data, local archives, contemporary reporting and interviews. Where a claim is contested, the sources behind it are named and the method behind them is explained.

## The making of a revolutionary, 1893 to 1919

Mao was born in 1893 in Shaoshan, Hunan Province. The world he entered was one in which, in the Party's later words, "the Revolution of 1911 led by Dr. Sun Yat-sen, the great revolutionary forerunner, overthrew the Qing Dynasty, thus bringing to an end over 2,000 years of feudal monarchical rule," but where "the semi-colonial and semi-feudal nature of Chinese society remained unchanged" ([1981 Resolution, §2](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). Semi-colonial here means formally independent but subject to foreign economic and political control. Semi-feudal means that landlord ownership and tenant farming persisted alongside ports, railways and modern industry.

Mao gave the same arc in his 1949 essay *On the People's Democratic Dictatorship*. He listed the Opium War of 1840, the search for answers abroad by "Hung Hsiu-chuan, Kang Yu-wei, Yen Fu and Sun Yat-sen," the failure of the 1911 revolution, and the turn caused by the Russian October Revolution of 1917. In his words, "The salvoes of the October Revolution brought us Marxism-Leninism" ([Mao, 30 June 1949](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-4/mswv4_65.htm)). Marxism-Leninism here means the body of theory derived from Karl Marx and Vladimir Lenin as interpreted by the Communist International, the Moscow-directed organisation that guided communist parties abroad.

The decisive generational event was the May Fourth Movement of 1919, a student and worker protest against the Versailles settlement that gave German concessions in China to Japan. Mao's own periodisation treats it as the boundary between the old and new types of bourgeois-democratic revolution. A bourgeois-democratic revolution is one aimed at ending feudal rule and establishing a market society; Mao called the pre-1919 version "old" because it was led by the bourgeoisie, the property-owning commercial class, and "new" because, after 1919, "the political leader of China's bourgeois-democratic revolution was no longer the bourgeoisie but the proletariat" ([*On New Democracy*, §V](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_26.htm)). Proletariat means the industrial working class. In the same essay Mao calls "the new revolutionary process of the past twenty years (counting from the May 4th Movement of 1919)" the content of China's new-democratic revolution ([*On New Democracy*, §IV](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_26.htm)).

## Party, united front and split, 1921 to 1927

The Communist Party of China was founded in 1921 "under the influence of the October Revolution in Russia and the May 4th Movement in China and with the help of the Communist International led by Lenin". At its founding it "had less than sixty members" ([1981 Resolution, §2](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). Mao helped organise local Party groups in this period.

From 1924 to 1927 the Party took part in the First United Front with Sun Yat-sen's Kuomintang, or Nationalist Party, in the campaign the Resolution calls the Northern Expedition. A united front is a temporary alliance of classes or parties against a common enemy, in which the ally keeps its own organisation and aims. The political basis was the manifesto of the Kuomintang's First National Congress of 1924, whose "Principle of Democracy" described "a democratic system shared by all the common people and not privately owned by the few," a formulation Mao quotes approvingly in 1949 ([Mao, 1949](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-4/mswv4_65.htm)).

During this period Mao wrote *Analysis of the Classes in Chinese Society* (March 1926). An editorial note says he wrote it "to combat two deviations then to be found in the Party": the Right opportunism of Chen Duxiu, "concerned only with co-operation with the Kuomintang and forgot about the peasants," and an opposing deviation. Right opportunism in this vocabulary means accommodation to the stronger party at the cost of one's own aims, not a position on a modern left-right spectrum ([Selected Works, Vol. I](http://www.marxists.org/ebooks/mao/mao-selected-works-1.pdf)).

In March 1927 Mao completed a 32-day investigation in five Hunan counties and wrote the *Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan*. The editorial note frames it as an answer to critics who "dared not support the great revolutionary struggles of the peasants" ([Mao, March 1927](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_2.htm)). Its opening image was that "several hundred million peasants will rise like a mighty storm, like a hurricane, a force so swift and violent that no power, however great, will be able to hold it back". The dictum that "a revolution is not a dinner party" also became canonical ([*Quotations from Chairman Mao*, ch. 2](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/ch02.htm)). The report is itself a piece of evidence about peasants' activity: Mao says he "called together fact-finding conferences in villages and county towns, which were attended by experienced peasants... and I listened attentively to their reports" ([Mao, 1927](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_2.htm)). It records what one investigator chose to hear, not a survey of every village.

The 1927 split is described thus in the Resolution: "the Kuomintang controlled by Chiang Kai-shek and Wang Jingwei betrayed the policies of Kuomintang-Communist co-operation and of anti-imperialism and anti-feudalism decided on by Dr. Sun Yat-sen and, in collusion with the imperialists, massacred Communists and other revolutionaries". The Party, inexperienced and "dominated by Chen Duxiu's Right capitulationism," suffered a disastrous defeat. Membership fell from more than 60,000 to a little over 10,000 ([1981 Resolution, §3](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

## The rural base areas and the Long March, 1927 to 1937

The Nanchang Uprising of 1927 "fired the opening shot for armed resistance against the Kuomintang reactionaries". A meeting of the Central Committee on 7 August "decided on the policy of carrying out agrarian revolution and organizing armed uprisings". Mao led the Autumn Harvest Uprising in the Hunan-Jiangxi border area. This uprising "gave birth to the First Division of the Chinese Workers' and Peasants' Revolutionary Army and to the first rural revolutionary base area in the Jinggang Mountains," where Zhu De's forces joined him. The Jiangxi central base area and the Red Army's First Front Army under Mao and Zhu De "played the most important role" ([1981 Resolution, §3](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). An encirclement campaign was a Nationalist attempt to surround and destroy one of these base areas.

Theoretical work of this period includes *Why Is It That Red Political Power Can Exist in China?* (October 1928), *The Struggle in the Chingkang Mountains* (November 1928), *On Correcting Mistaken Ideas in the Party* (December 1929), *A Single Spark Can Start a Prairie Fire* (January 1930) and *How to Differentiate the Classes in the Rural Areas* (October 1933) ([Selected Works, Vol. I, contents](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/index.htm)). *A Single Spark* answered pessimistic comrades: "our forces, although small at present, will grow very rapidly," and "All China is littered with dry faggots which will soon be aflame" ([Mao, 5 January 1930](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_6.htm)).

Because of "Wang Ming's 'Left' adventurist leadership," meaning an impatient policy of premature insurrection, the struggle against the fifth encirclement campaign failed and the First Front Army began "the 25,000-li Long March". The Red Army fell from 300,000 to about 30,000 and Party membership from 300,000 to about 40,000. In January 1935 the Political Bureau met at Zunyi during the march. It "established the leading position of Comrade Mao Zedong in the Red Army and the Central Committee of the Party... It was a vital turning point in the history of the Party" ([1981 Resolution, §3](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The figures and the judgement are the Resolution's; other historians have described the same sequence as a retreat that became a legend of endurance.

## Yan'an and the war against Japan, 1937 to 1945

At the end of 1935 Mao reported on tactics against Japanese aggression. The meeting "criticized the mistaken view in the Party that the Chinese national bourgeoisie could not be an ally of the workers and peasants in the common fight against Japan, and it decided on the tactics of a national united front" ([Selected Works, Vol. I](http://www.marxists.org/ebooks/mao/mao-selected-works-1.pdf)). The national bourgeoisie means domestic capitalists who were not tied to foreign firms. The Xi'an Incident of 12 December 1936, in which Generals Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng detained Chiang Kai-shek, and its peaceful settlement "played a crucial historical role in bringing about renewed co-operation between the Kuomintang and the Communist Party" ([1981 Resolution, §3](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

During the eight-year War of Resistance, the Eighth Route Army and New Fourth Army, the reorganized Red Army, "became the mainstay in the war of resistance". They conducted guerrilla warfare behind enemy lines and built anti-Japanese base areas, while the Party maintained "its independence and initiative within the united front" ([1981 Resolution, §3](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). Yan'an, in northern Shaanxi, became the Party's centre from 1937 to 1947.

The Yan'an Rectification Movement, launched in 1942, was "a movement of Marxist education... a tremendous success". On this basis the Seventh Plenary Session of the Sixth Central Committee adopted the *Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of Our Party* in 1945. The Seventh National Congress followed, achieving "unprecedented ideological, political and organizational unity and solidarity" ([1981 Resolution, §3](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). At that congress Mao delivered *On Coalition Government* (24 April 1945), whose programme included "Abolish the Kuomintang One-Party Dictatorship, Establish a Democratic Coalition Government" ([Mao, 1945](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-3/mswv3_25.htm)).

How the Party won rural support in these years is a genuine question in modern scholarship, not a matter settled by a Party resolution. Chalmers Johnson's *Peasant Nationalism and Communist Power* (Stanford University Press, 1962) argued that the Japanese invasion and harsh occupation policies created a nationalist awakening among peasants, and that the Communist Party gained followers by leading resistance rather than by class redistribution ([Stanford University Press](https://www.sup.org/books/asian-studies/peasant-nationalism-and-communist-power)). Johnson built his case on captured Japanese wartime records and on a comparison with communist mobilisation in Yugoslavia. Mark Selden's *The Yenan Way in Revolutionary China* (Harvard University Press, 1971) challenged that claim. Selden argued that policies of land redistribution, rent and interest reduction, cooperatives and the "three-thirds system" of local government, under which communists took roughly a third of seats and allowed other groups a share, won peasant support before and during the war, and he used base-area documents and local studies as evidence ([review of Selden](https://ucsdmodernchinesehistory.wordpress.com/2010/01/29/396)). The argument is about which evidence carries weight: wartime intelligence records in one case, base-area administrative records in the other. Selden returned to the subject in a revised edition, *China in Revolution: Yenan Way Revisited* (first published 1995; Routledge edition 2016), reconsidering his original themes in light of later research ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.4324/9781315286419/china-revolution-mark-selden)).

Suzanne Pepper shifted the frame again in *Civil War in China: The Political Struggle, 1945-1949* (2nd edition, Rowman & Littlefield, 1999). Using contemporary newspapers, government records and policy documents, she argued that the Nationalists lost the cities as well as the countryside through inflation, corruption and mishandling of students, workers and intellectuals, and that Communist policies in the occupied cities won support from these groups ([Inlibra record and DOI](https://www.inlibra.com/en/document/view/detail/uuid/93a56d7c-36a4-3e9a-9345-575dc8c001f8)). These works disagree about causes and about the reach of their evidence. They do not disagree about the outcome: by 1949 the Nationalist government had lost the mainland.

## Civil war and the founding of the People's Republic, 1945 to 1949

After Japan's surrender, the Chiang Kai-shek government, "with the aid of U.S. imperialism, flagrantly launched an all-out civil war". With the support of the Liberated Areas, the student and worker movements, and the democratic parties, the People's Liberation Army fought the three-year War of Liberation. Liberated Areas means territory under Communist control. After the Liaoxi-Shenyang, Beiping-Tianjin and Huai-Hai campaigns and the crossing of the Changjiang, the Resolution says the army "wiped out a total of 8,000,000 Chiang Kai-shek troops" and that "The end result was the overthrow of the reactionary Kuomintang government and the establishment of the great People's Republic of China. The Chinese people had stood up" ([1981 Resolution, §3](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The troop figure is the issuing body's claim, and other accounts use different measures of losses.

Mao's theoretical summation on the eve of victory was *On the People's Democratic Dictatorship* (30 June 1949), written for the Party's 28th anniversary. Its conclusion: "To sum up our experience and concentrate it into one point, it is the people's democratic dictatorship under the leadership of the working class (through the Communist Party) and based upon the alliance of workers and peasants" ([Mao, 1949](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-4/mswv4_65.htm)). The People's Republic was founded on 1 October 1949. The Preamble of the current Constitution recalls that "after engaging in protracted, arduous and tortuous struggles, armed and in other forms, the Chinese people of all ethnic groups led by the Communist Party of China with Chairman Mao Zedong as its leader finally overthrew the rule of imperialism, feudalism and bureaucrat-capitalism, won a great victory in the New Democratic Revolution, and founded the People's Republic of China" ([PRC Constitution, Preamble](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/lawsregulations/201911/20/content_WS5ed8856ec6d0b3f0e9499913.html)).

## New Democracy and the mass line

New Democracy was Mao's theory of a two-stage revolution: first a bourgeois-democratic revolution under working-class leadership, then a socialist revolution. The idea drew on the Marxist-Leninist theory of the democratic dictatorship, adapted to a country that was formally independent but dominated by foreign powers and by landed property. Its founding text states the premise directly:

> "In the course of its history the Chinese revolution must go through two stages, first, the democratic revolution, and second, the socialist revolution, and by their very nature they are two different revolutionary processes. Here democracy does not belong to the old category-- it is not the old democracy, but belongs to the new category-- it is New Democracy."
>
> Source: [Mao, *On New Democracy*, §III (January 1940)](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_26.htm)

Mao derived this from China's social character and from the changed world epoch. "Clearly, it follows from the colonial, semi-colonial and semi-feudal character of present-day Chinese society that the Chinese revolution must be divided into two stages. The first step is to change the colonial, semi-colonial and semi-feudal form of society into an independent, democratic society. The second is to carry the revolution forward and build a socialist society" ([*On New Democracy*, §IV](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_26.htm)). *The Chinese Revolution and the Chinese Communist Party* (December 1939) states the relation of the stages: "The democratic revolution is the necessary preparation for the socialist revolution, and the socialist revolution is the inevitable sequel to the democratic revolution. The ultimate aim for which all communists strive is to bring about a socialist and communist society" ([Mao, 1939](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_23.htm); [*Quotations*, ch. 3](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/ch03.htm)).

The innovative core is the theory of the state appropriate to the first stage. Mao distinguished "republics under bourgeois dictatorship," "republics under the dictatorship of the proletariat" and "republics under the joint dictatorship of several revolutionary classes". The third, he wrote, is "the transitional form of state to be adopted in the revolutions of the colonial and semi-colonial countries" ([*On New Democracy*, §V](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_26.htm)). A joint dictatorship means that several classes share rule, with the workers' party holding the leading role. Concretely, the new-democratic republic is "under the joint dictatorship of all anti-imperialist and anti-feudal people led by the proletariat," with "the state system, a joint dictatorship of all the revolutionary classes and the system of government, democratic centralism" ([*On New Democracy*, §V](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_26.htm)). Democratic centralism is the principle that bodies are elected from below but must carry out decisions of the higher levels. Its economy combines state ownership of "the big banks and the big industrial and commercial enterprises," with "state enterprises... of a socialist character" as "the leading force in the whole national economy". It also allows "land to the tiller" and a rich-peasant economy, while stating that "socialist agriculture will not be established at this stage" ([*On New Democracy*, §VI](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_26.htm)).

The 1981 Resolution, while criticising Mao's later errors, lists New Democracy as the first of six fields in which Mao Zedong Thought made original contributions: "a revolution against imperialism, feudalism and bureaucrat-capitalism waged by the masses of the people on the basis of the worker-peasant alliance under the leadership of the proletariat" ([1981 Resolution, §29(1)](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). Mao himself insisted the first stage "will need quite a long time and cannot be accomplished overnight. We are not utopians" ([*On New Democracy*, §VIII](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_26.htm)). In a 1944 interview he repeated that "What China needs now is democracy and not socialism" ([Mao's interview with Gunther Stein, 1944](http://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-6/mswv6_38.htm)).

Mao Zedong Thought means the Party's name for Mao's ideas as a system, defined in the Resolution as "the product of the integration of the universal principles of Marxism-Leninism with the concrete practice of the Chinese revolution... a crystallization of the collective wisdom of the Chinese Communist Party" ([§28](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The mass line is its working method. The Resolution calls three points "the living soul" of Mao Zedong Thought: "to seek truth from facts, the mass line, and independence" ([§30](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). In plain language, the mass line means gathering the scattered ideas of ordinary people, working them into a policy, and taking that policy back to the people for discussion and action.

Its classical statement is *Some Questions Concerning Methods of Leadership* (1 June 1943): "There are two methods which we Communists must employ in whatever work we do. One is to combine the general with the particular; the other is to combine the leadership with the masses." The famous formulation follows:

> "Take the ideas of the masses and concentrate them, then go to the masses, persevere in the ideas and carry them through, so as to form correct ideas of leadership - such is the basic method of leadership."
>
> Source: [*Quotations from Chairman Mao Tse-tung*, ch. 11, "The Mass Line"](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/ch11.htm)

The same text insists that "all correct leadership is necessarily 'from the masses, to the masses'". It adds that "However active the leading group may be, its activity will amount to fruitless effort by a handful of people unless combined with the activity of the masses". At the same time, "if the masses alone are active without a strong leading group to organize their activity properly, such activity cannot be sustained for long" ([*Quotations*, ch. 11](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/ch11.htm)). Mao divided the masses "into three parts, the relatively active, the intermediate and the relatively backward". Leaders must "unite the small number of active elements around the leadership and... to raise the level of the intermediate elements and to win over the backward elements" ([*Quotations*, ch. 11](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/ch11.htm)). The philosophical premise is stated in *On Coalition Government*: "The people, and the people alone, are the motive force of world history" ([Mao, 1945](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-3/mswv3_25.htm)).

## People's war and the united front

Protracted people's war means a long revolutionary war in which a weak force builds base areas in the countryside, avoids decisive battle until conditions change, and eventually encircles the cities. The Resolution summarises Mao's military writings, including *Problems of Strategy in China's Revolutionary War*, *Problems of Strategy in Guerrilla War Against Japan*, *On Protracted War* and *Problems of War and Strategy*, and credits him with "the comprehensive concept of building a people's army and of building rural base areas and waging people's war by employing the people's army as the main force and relying on the masses" and with "raising guerrilla war to the strategic plane" ([1981 Resolution, §29(3)](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

The "road" of the revolution appears explicitly in a 1972 *Peking Review* study of *A Single Spark Can Start a Prairie Fire*: "Under the leadership of the Communist Party, build rural base areas and use the countryside to encircle and finally capture the cities. This revolutionary road was correct because it was based on a scientific analysis of contradictions in Chinese society and of the revolutionary situation and balance of class forces" ([Writing Group of the Yunnan Provincial Committee, 1972](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/peking-review/1972/PR1972-07a.htm)). The strategic justification was that the enemy was strong in the cities while the revolutionary forces were weak. The revolution had to build "red political power" in the countryside first, a phenomenon Mao called "a phenomenon that has never occurred anywhere else in the world" ([*Selected Military Writings*, 1928](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/military-writings/mao-selected-military-writings-1963.pdf)).

*On Protracted War* (May 1938), delivered as lectures at the Yenan Association for the Study of the War of Resistance, refuted "the theory of national subjugation" and "the theory of quick victory" and set out three stages:

> "The first stage covers the period of the enemy's strategic offensive and our strategic defensive. The second stage will be the period of the enemy's strategic consolidation and our preparation for the counter-offensive. The third stage will be the period of our strategic counter-offensive and the enemy's strategic retreat."
>
> Source: [Mao, *On Protracted War*, "The Three Stages of the Protracted War" (1938)](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_09.htm)

At the level of whole wars the strategy was protracted and defensive on interior lines, meaning supply lines inside one's own territory. At the level of individual campaigns it was "one of 'quick decision offensive warfare on exterior lines'" ([*Selected Military Writings*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/military-writings/mao-selected-military-writings-1963.pdf)). Exterior lines means operating outside the enemy's own base area. The political premise is that "the richest source of power to wage war lies in the masses of the people" and that "war is politics with bloodshed" ([*Quotations*, ch. 5](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/ch05.htm) and [ch. 8](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/ch08.htm)). The Resolution also records the associated principles: "the Party commands the gun and not the other way round", the Three Main Rules of Discipline and Eight Points for Attention, which set out how soldiers were to treat civilians and property, political, economic and military democracy, and unity between officers and soldiers and between army and people ([1981 Resolution, §29(3)](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

The united front was both a domestic class tactic and an international strategy. Domestically it rested on Mao's division of the bourgeoisie into the big bourgeoisie, tied to foreign capital, and the national bourgeoisie, which Mao described as having revolutionary leanings but as prone to wavering. The proletariat was to draw the national bourgeoisie into a front under its leadership, "and in special circumstances to include even part of the big bourgeoisie in the united front, so as to isolate the main enemy to the greatest possible extent". While in the front, "the proletariat must preserve its own independence and pursue the policy of 'unity, struggle, unity through struggle'" ([1981 Resolution, §29(1)](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). *On New Democracy* gives the class basis: the national bourgeoisie has a "dual character", "Possible participation in the revolution on the one hand and proneness to conciliation with the enemies of the revolution on the other" ([*On New Democracy*, §V](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_26.htm)).

Mao's 1949 summary names the united front as one of "the three main weapons with which we have defeated the enemy": "A well-disciplined Party armed with the theory of Marxism-Leninism, using the method of self-criticism and linked with the masses of the people; an army under the leadership of such a Party; a united front of all revolutionary classes and all revolutionary groups under the leadership of such a Party" ([Mao, 1949](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-4/mswv4_65.htm)). Internationally, "Internally, arouse the masses of the people... Externally, unite in a common struggle with those nations of the world which treat us as equals" ([Mao, 1949](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-4/mswv4_65.htm)).

## Class analysis, contradiction and the state

Mao's early and lasting problem was how a Marxist party in a country where "the peasantry and other sections of the petty bourgeoisie constituted the majority of the population, while the proletariat was small in number yet strong in combat effectiveness" could build a proletarian party and revolution ([1981 Resolution, §29(6)](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The 1927 Hunan report is his answer in practice. Its conclusion challenges every revolutionary to choose: "To march at their head and lead them. To trail behind them, gesticulating and criticizing. Or to stand in their way and oppose them" ([Mao, 1927](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_2.htm)).

In *The Chinese Revolution and the Chinese Communist Party* (1939), Mao locates the peasantry historically: "The ruthless economic exploitation and political oppression of the peasants by the landlord class forced them into numerous uprisings... It was the class struggles of the peasants, the peasant uprisings and peasant wars that constituted the real motive force of historical development in Chinese feudal society". He also notes that "every peasant revolution failed" without "correct leadership such as the proletariat and the Communist Party provide today" ([Mao, 1939](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-2/mswv2_23.htm)). Class differentiation within the countryside was worked out in *How to Differentiate the Classes in the Rural Areas* (October 1933) ([Selected Works I](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/index.htm)). In 1949 Mao writes that the worker-peasant alliance "comprise[s] 80 to 90 per cent of China's population" and "is the main force in overthrowing imperialism and the Kuomintang reactionaries". On the transition, "The serious problem is the education of the peasantry... Without socialization of agriculture, there can be no complete, consolidated socialism" ([Mao, 1949](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-4/mswv4_65.htm)). In 1957 he identifies "the overwhelming majority of the poor and lower-middle peasants who constitute more than 70 per cent of the rural population" as active supporters of the cooperatives ([Mao, 1957](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/mswv5_58.htm)).

*On Contradiction* and *On Practice*, both written in 1937 as lectures at the Anti-Japanese Military and Political College in Yenan, are Mao's philosophical essays. Here contradiction means the opposing forces inside any process that drive change; materialist dialectics is the view that these conflicts, rather than ideas, drive historical development. Both essays were aimed at "the serious error of dogmatist thinking" in the Party. Dogmatism means treating Marxist texts as fixed formulas, while empiricism means relying on fragmentary experience without theory. *On Practice* was written "to expose the subjectivist errors of dogmatism and empiricism... from the standpoint of the Marxist theory of knowledge". The dogmatists "rejected the experience of the Chinese revolution, denying the truth that 'Marxism is not a dogma but a guide to action'" ([Mao, *On Practice*, July 1937](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_16.htm)). Its epistemological core, the theory of how knowledge is gained:

> "The truth of any knowledge or theory is determined not by subjective feelings, but by objective results in social practice. Only social practice can be the criterion of truth. The standpoint of practice is the primary and basic standpoint in the dialectical materialist theory of knowledge."
>
> Source: [Mao, *On Practice* (1937)](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_16.htm)

Knowledge moves "from perceptual knowledge" through "rational knowledge" and back to revolutionary practice: "Practice, knowledge, again practice, and again knowledge. This form repeats itself in endless cycles, and with each cycle the content of practice and knowledge rises to a higher level" ([*On Practice*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_16.htm)).

*On Contradiction* (August 1937) opens: "The law of contradiction in things, that is, the law of unity of opposites, is the basic law of materialist dialectics... Lenin often called this law the essence of dialectics; he also called it the kernel of dialectics" ([Mao, *On Contradiction*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-1/mswv1_17.htm)). Mao's distinctive emphasis is on the particularity of contradiction, meaning the features unique to a given conflict. He writes that "it is precisely in the particularity of contradiction that the universality of contradiction resides", by which he means that general patterns exist only inside specific cases ([*Quotations*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/quotes.htm)). He also identifies the principal contradiction, the conflict that determines the others at a given stage: "The fundamental cause of the development of a thing is not external but internal; it lies in the contradictoriness within the thing" ([*Quotations*, "Revolutionary Civil War Period"](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/quotes.htm)).

He insists that contradictions can change character: "some contradictions, which were originally non-antagonistic, develop into antagonistic ones, while others which were originally antagonistic develop into non-antagonistic ones" ([*Quotations*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/quotes.htm)). Antagonistic conflicts are resolved by force, non-antagonistic ones by persuasion. This thesis was later used in *On the Correct Handling of Contradictions Among the People* to argue that the contradiction between the working class and the national bourgeoisie "if properly handled, can be transformed into a non-antagonistic one and be resolved by peaceful methods" ([Mao, 1957](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/mswv5_58.htm)).

In *On the People's Democratic Dictatorship* (30 June 1949) Mao defines the state in a formula the Resolution calls an enrichment of "the Marxist-Leninist theory of the dictatorship of the proletariat," the doctrine that the working class rules through its party during the transition to socialism ([1981 Resolution, §29(2)](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)):

> "The combination of these two aspects, democracy for the people and dictatorship over the reactionaries, is the people's democratic dictatorship."
>
> Source: [Mao, *On the People's Democratic Dictatorship* (1949)](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-4/mswv4_65.htm)

"Who are the people?" Mao asks; at that stage "they are the working class, the peasantry, the urban petty bourgeoisie and the national bourgeoisie". The dictatorship "needs the leadership of the working class. For it is only the working class that is most farsighted, most selfless and most thoroughly revolutionary." "Dictatorship does not apply within the ranks of the people. The people cannot exercise dictatorship over themselves". Within the people, "the method we employ is democratic, the method of persuasion, not of compulsion" ([Mao, 1949](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-4/mswv4_65.htm)). On the choice between the Soviet and American camps, an interlocutor accused Mao of leaning to one side. Mao replied "Exactly." He added that the experience of Sun Yat-sen and of the Party had taught them to lean to one side, and that "Sitting on the fence will not do, nor is there a third road." The supposed third road meant a middle path between imperialism and socialism ([Mao, 1949](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-4/mswv4_65.htm)).

In 1957 Mao developed the doctrine of two types of contradiction. "We are confronted with two types of social contradictions -- those between ourselves and the enemy and those among the people. The two are totally different in nature." The former "entail drawing a clear distinction between ourselves and the enemy, and the latter entail drawing a clear distinction between right and wrong." Among the people the method is "the democratic method, the method of discussion, criticism, persuasion and education," epitomised in the 1942 formula "unity -- criticism -- unity," or "learn from past mistakes to avoid future ones and cure the sickness to save the patient" ([Mao, *On the Correct Handling of Contradictions Among the People*, 27 February 1957](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/mswv5_58.htm)). He adds that "the basic contradictions" in socialist society "are still those between the relations of production and the productive forces and between the superstructure and the economic base". Relations of production means who owns and controls economic resources; productive forces means labour, tools and technology; the economic base is the system of production, and the superstructure is law, politics and ideas built on it. Mao held that these contradictions are "fundamentally different in character" from those of capitalist society, and "can be ceaselessly resolved by the socialist system itself" ([Mao, 1957](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/mswv5_58.htm)).

## Land reform and the transition to socialism, 1949 to 1956

The first phase is summarised in the Resolution. The new state "cleared the mainland of bandits and the remnant armed forces of the Kuomintang reactionaries, peacefully liberated Tibet, established people's governments at all levels". It "confiscated bureaucrat-capitalist enterprises and transformed them into state-owned socialist enterprises". It "carried out agrarian reform in the new liberated areas, suppressed counter-revolutionaries, and unfolded the movements against the 'three evils'... and against the 'five evils'". The three evils were corruption, waste and bureaucracy; the five were bribery, tax evasion, theft of state property, cheating on government contracts and stealing of economic information. The latter was "a movement to beat back the attack mounted by the bourgeoisie" ([1981 Resolution, §10](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). This was done at the same time as "the great war to resist U.S. aggression and aid Korea". By the end of 1952 industrial and agricultural production had reached record levels ([1981 Resolution, §10](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). These are the Resolution's judgements about its own early record.

The First Plenary Session of the Chinese People's Political Consultative Conference, an advisory body, adopted the Common Programme, which "served as a provisional constitution after the founding of New China" ([Report to the Second Plenary Session of the Seventh Central Committee, March 1949](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-4/mswv4_58.htm)). The First National People's Congress enacted the first PRC Constitution in September 1954 ([1981 Resolution, §14](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

The Resolution states that "On the proposal of Comrade Mao Zedong in 1952, the Central Committee of the Party advanced the general line for the transition period, which was to realize the country's socialist industrialization and socialist transformation of agriculture, handicrafts and capitalist industry and commerce step by step over a fairly long period of time". It then delivers its own verdict: "As is borne out by history, the general line for the transition period set forth by our Party was entirely correct" ([1981 Resolution, §11](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The reasoning given is that after land reform "the contradiction between the working class and the bourgeoisie and between the socialist road and the capitalist road became the principal internal contradiction". Poor and lower-middle peasants "had just acquired land but lacked other means of production" and "had a genuine desire for mutual aid and co-operation" ([1981 Resolution, §11](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

The forms of transition were gradual: "state orders... state monopoly of the purchase and marketing of the products of private enterprise... joint state-private ownership". These culminated in "the peaceful redemption of the bourgeoisie, a possibility envisaged by Marx and Lenin," meaning that former capitalists were compensated over time rather than dispossessed without payment. For agriculture the forms ran "temporary or all-the-year-round mutual-aid teams, to elementary agricultural producers' co-operatives of a semi-socialist nature and then to advanced agricultural producers' co-operatives of a fully socialist nature" ([1981 Resolution, §12](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The Resolution also notes that "from the summer of 1955 onwards, we were over-hasty... the changes were too fast" ([1981 Resolution, §12](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

In economic construction, "under the First Five-Year Plan (1953-57), we likewise scored major successes through our own efforts and with the assistance of the Soviet Union and other friendly countries". The document then gives a narrower time span: "Between 1953 and 1956, the average annual increases in the total value of industrial and agricultural output were 19.6 and 4.8 per cent respectively" ([1981 Resolution, §13](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). A separate paragraph should not be read as assigning those percentages to the whole plan period.

In April 1956 Mao delivered *On the Ten Major Relationships*, "in which he initially summed up our experiences in socialist construction and set forth the task of exploring a way of building socialism suited to the specific conditions of our country" ([1981 Resolution, §13](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The ten relationships concern heavy industry versus light industry and agriculture, coastal versus interior industry, economic versus defence construction, the state versus production units and producers, central versus local authorities, Han versus minority nationalities, Party versus non-Party, revolution versus counter-revolution, right versus wrong, and China versus other countries ([Mao, 1956](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/mswv5_51.htm)). The guiding "basic policy... is of mobilizing all positive factors, internal and external, to serve the cause of socialism". The text repeatedly invokes Soviet errors as a warning: "Do you want to follow the detours they have made?" On Stalin it gives the famous assessment: "It is the opinion of the Central Committee that Stalin's mistakes amounted to only 30 per cent of the whole and his achievements to 70 per cent, and that all things considered Stalin was nonetheless a great Marxist" ([Mao, 1956](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/mswv5_51.htm)).

The Eighth Congress of September 1956 declared that the socialist system had been basically established. It also declared that "the principal contradiction within the country was no longer the contradiction between the working class and the bourgeoisie but between the demand of the people for rapid economic and cultural development and the existing state of our economy and culture" ([1981 Resolution, §15](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

## The Hundred Flowers and the Anti-Rightist Campaign, 1956 to 1957

The policy of "letting a hundred flowers blossom and a hundred schools of thought contend," meaning that critics would be allowed to speak and rival views to compete, was advanced after a January 1956 conference on intellectuals. The Resolution's own words call it a policy that "spelled out the correct policy regarding intellectuals and the work in education, science and culture" ([1981 Resolution, §14](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). That is the document's verdict, not an independent finding. In 1957 a rectification campaign was launched and the masses were urged to offer criticisms.

The Resolution's account of what followed: "In the rectification campaign a handful of bourgeois Rightists seized the opportunity to advocate what they called 'speaking out and airing views in a big way' and to mount a wild attack against the Party and the nascent socialist system in an attempt to replace the leadership of the Communist Party. It was therefore entirely correct and necessary to launch a resolute counter-attack. But the scope of this struggle was made far too broad and a number of intellectuals, patriotic people and Party cadres were unjustifiably labelled 'Rightists'" ([1981 Resolution, §17](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). Deng Xiaoping's later formulation, in remarks on drafting the Resolution, was that the anti-Rightist struggle "was necessary, but it was broadened too much" ([Deng, *On Opposing Wrong Ideological Tendencies*, 1981](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/deng-xiaoping/1981/166.htm)). Mao's *On the Correct Handling of Contradictions Among the People*, delivered 27 February 1957 and published 19 June, was delivered during this campaign ([Mao, 1957](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/selected-works/volume-5/mswv5_58.htm)).Gail Hershatter's oral histories with 72 elderly women in four Shaanxi villages, gathered with Gao Xiaoxian between 1996 and 2006, show how such campaigns entered daily life. Her informants remember agricultural work, work points, marriage, childbirth and activism, and they often date events by their children's births rather than by national campaign names, a pattern she calls "campaign time" in men's accounts. Because these are recollections recorded decades later, they reveal how the campaigns were remembered as well as how they were experienced ([Gail Hershatter, *The Gender of Memory: Rural Women and China's Collective Past*, University of California Press, 2011](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/the-gender-of-memory/hardcover)).

## The Great Leap Forward and the famine, 1958 to 1961

The Second Plenum of the Eighth Central Committee adopted in 1958 the general line for socialist construction. The Resolution judges: "The line and its fundamental aspects were correct in that it reflected the masses' pressing demand for a change in the economic and cultural backwardness of our country. Its shortcoming was that it overlooked objective economic laws." It continues:

> "'Left' errors, characterized by excessive targets, the issuing of arbitrary directions, boastfulness and the stirring up of a 'communist wind', spread unchecked throughout the country... More important, it was due to the fact that Comrade Mao Zedong and many leading comrades... had become smug about their successes, were impatient for quick results and overestimated the role of man's subjective will and efforts."
>
> Source: [1981 Resolution, §17](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)

The Resolution records that Mao himself, from the end of 1958 to the early stage of the Lushan Meeting in July 1959, "led the whole Party in energetically rectifying the errors which had already been recognized". In the later part of the meeting, however, "he erred in initiating criticism of Comrade Peng Dehuai and then in launching a Party-wide struggle against 'Right opportunism'" ([1981 Resolution, §17](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). Its own euphemism is that "our economy encountered serious difficulties between 1959 and 1961, which caused serious losses to our country and people" ([§17](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). Those "serious difficulties" were a nationwide famine.

Historians have measured the famine using methods quite different from the Resolution's self-criticism. Basil Ashton, Kenneth Hill, Alan Piazza and Robin Zeitz compared data from the 1953, 1964 and 1982 censuses with population registers and food-supply figures. In 1984 they concluded that the crisis of 1958 to 1961 caused "almost 30 million premature deaths," that fertility fell sharply, and that grain output dropped by more than 25 percent in 1960 and 1961. They found both natural disasters and government policy among the causes, and noted that food was not distributed to where it was needed ([Ashton, Hill, Piazza and Zeitz, "Famine in China, 1958-61," *Population and Development Review* 10, no. 4, 1984, 613-645, DOI 10.2307/1973284](https://pure.johnshopkins.edu/en/publications/famine-in-china-1958-61/)). James Kai-sing Kung, writing in *The Cambridge Economic History of China* in 2022, describes the Great Leap Famine as the deadliest in human history, with "some 30 million, or 5 percent of a population of 660 million" dead in three years. His chapter emphasises excessive grain procurement, career incentives for local cadres, communal dining and weather as interacting causes ([Kung, "The Political Economy of China's Great Leap Famine," in Debin Ma and Richard von Glahn, eds., *The Cambridge Economic History of China*, Cambridge University Press, 2022, 642-684, DOI 10.1017/9781108348485.019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-economic-history-of-china/political-economy-of-chinas-great-leap-famine/32E5530258C063FE060C1A8C60A23C05)).

Dali Yang's *Calamity and Reform in China* (Stanford University Press, 1996) connects the political causes of the famine to the rural reforms after Mao's death, arguing that the memory of the disaster helped persuade ordinary people and officials to dismantle the communes ([Stanford University Press](https://www.sup.org/books/asian-studies/calamity-and-reform-china)). Yang Jisheng, a journalist at Xinhua from 1968 to 2001 whose father died in the famine, spent roughly twenty years assembling documentary evidence and published *Mubei* in 2008. Its English version, *Tombstone: The Great Chinese Famine, 1958-1962* (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2012), edited and condensed from the Chinese, estimates that 36 million people starved to death and attributes the disaster to the political system's refusal to let bad news reach the top ([Macmillan publisher page](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374533991/tombstone); [Pacific Affairs review](https://pacificaffairs.ubc.ca/book-reviews/tombstone-the-great-chinese-famine-1958-1962-by-yang-jisheng-translated-from-the-chinese-by-stacy-mosher-and-guo-jian-edited-by-edward-friedman-guo-jian-and-stacy-mosher-introduction-by-edward)).

The death toll remains contested, and the arguments turn on method. Frank Dikötter's *Mao's Great Famine* (Walker & Company, 2010), based on more than a thousand local documents, claims "at least 45 million" premature deaths between 1958 and 1962, including at least 2.5 million people beaten or tortured to death ([author's synopsis](http://www.frankdikotter.com/books/maos-great-famine/synopsis.html)). Cormac Ó Gráda, reviewing the book in *Population and Development Review* in 2011, argued that Dikötter reached his figure by taking Cao Shuji's estimate of 32.5 million and adding half again, and that he assumed a normal death rate of 10 per thousand, which Ó Gráda called implausibly low for a poor country so soon after 1949. Ó Gráda placed the specialist demographic range at the time between 18 and 32.5 million and criticised the book's use of numbers without discussion of their provenance ([Ó Gráda, "Great Leap into Famine: A Review Essay," *Population and Development Review* 37, no. 1, 2011, 191-202, DOI 10.1111/j.1728-4457.2011.00398.x](https://web.archive.org/web/20230718140302/https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1728-4457.2011.00398.x)). Some later syntheses use 36 million as a middle estimate ([Our World in Data, "Famines"](https://ourworldindata.org/famines)). The honest statement is that the famine killed tens of millions, that demographic estimates based on censuses cluster around 30 million, and that higher figures depend on assumptions about baseline mortality and on how lost births are treated.

Disagreement also concerns responsibility. Thomas P. Bernstein's study of Mao's own reading of reports in 1958 and 1960, published in *The China Quarterly* in 2006, concludes that Mao knew by late 1958 that overwork, hunger and epidemics were killing peasants, and that after the Lushan confrontation with Peng Dehuai he revived the radical policies he had briefly criticised. Bernstein calls this "a study in wilfulness" ([Bernstein, "Mao Zedong and the Famine of 1959-1960: A Study in Wilfulness," *The China Quarterly* 186, 2006, 421-445, DOI 10.1017/S0305741006000221](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/mao-zedong-and-the-famine-of-19591960-a-study-in-wilfulness/3CF47A28C732B4365D2413C88813DC6E)). Chris Bramall, using county-level data on mortality, output, rainfall and temperature for Sichuan, argues that weather played only a minor role and that central radicalism mattered, but that differences between counties were largely random and point to variations in how local cadres applied central policy ([Bramall, "Agency and Famine in China's Sichuan Province, 1958-1962," *The China Quarterly* 208, 2011, 990-1008, DOI 10.1017/S030574101100110X](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/abs/agency-and-famine-in-chinas-sichuan-province-19581962/F39E361A42764CBB9F94071C2CB822E0)). These are competing explanations built from different evidence, not a contest between party loyalty and its opponents.

The Resolution preserves Mao's corrective positions during the subsequent readjustment: "there must be no expropriation of the peasants; that a given stage of social development should not be skipped; that equalitarianism must be opposed; that we must stress commodity production, observe the law of value and strike an over-all balance in economic planning; and that economic plans must be arranged with the priority proceeding from agriculture to light industry and then to heavy industry" ([1981 Resolution, §16](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The winter of 1960 brought the policy of "readjustment, consolidation, filling out and raising standards". The enlarged 7,000-person Central Work Conference of January 1962 made a preliminary summing-up ([1981 Resolution, §17](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

Felix Wemheuer's interviews in Henan show that the famine remains a live and contested memory. Villagers brought up starvation, terror and suffering, but shaped their accounts so as to preserve harmony in the village, while local cadres rejected the official distribution of responsibility. Wemheuer relates the gap between these memories to the separation of urban and rural China ([Wemheuer, "Dealing with Responsibility for the Great Leap Famine in the People's Republic of China," *The China Quarterly* 201, 2010, 176-194, DOI 10.1017/S0305741009991123](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/china-quarterly/article/dealing-with-responsibility-for-the-great-leap-famine-in-the-peoples-republic-of-china/550C79E7B5FC769402092A3C53398EFC)).

## The Sino-Soviet split and the critique of "modern revisionism"

The CPC's critique of Nikita Khrushchev's Communist Party of the Soviet Union was conducted through a formal polemic. "Modern revisionism" was the CPC's term for what it saw as a departure from Marxist-Leninist principle by the Soviet leadership. In the [1984 Declaration of the Revolutionary Internationalist Movement](https://foreignlanguages.press/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/RIM-%E2%80%93-Declaration-1984.pdf), a retrospective account, its starting point was the June 1963 *Proposal Concerning the General Line of the International Communist Movement*, the "25-point letter". It was "an all-round and public condemnation of revisionism and a call to the genuine Marxist-Leninists of all countries. The contemporary Marxist-Leninist movement has as its origin this historic appeal and the polemics that accompanied it."

The CPSU replied with an Open Letter of 14 July 1963. The editorial departments of *People's Daily* and *Red Flag* published nine comments between September 1963 and 1964 ([Sino-Soviet Split Document Archive](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sino-soviet-split/index.htm)). The substance, as the CPC stated it, concerned "peaceful transition," meaning a parliamentary road to socialism, "peaceful coexistence," meaning relaxed relations with capitalist states, the significance of nuclear weapons, and the national liberation movement. The comments set out these positions:

> Comment VIII, *The Proletarian Revolution and Khrushchov's Revisionism* (31 March 1964), argues: "In the history of the international communist movement the betrayal of Marxism and of the proletariat by the revisionists has always manifested itself most sharply in their opposition to violent revolution and to the dictatorship of the proletariat and in their advocacy of peaceful transition from capitalism to socialism" ([Maoist Documentation Project transcription](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sino-soviet-split/cpc/prolrev.htm)).
>
> Comment V, *Two Different Lines on the Question of War and Peace* (19 November 1963), identifies "the heart of the theory of the leaders of the CPSU on war and peace" as "their thesis that the emergence of nuclear weapons has changed everything and has changed the laws of class struggle" ([source](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sino-soviet-split/cpc/2-lines.htm)).
>
> Comment III, *Is Yugoslavia a Socialist Country?* (26 September 1963), warns: "So long as imperialism exists, there is apparently no ground for saying that the danger of the restoration of capitalism in the socialist countries has been eliminated" ([source](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sino-soviet-split/cpc/yugoslavia.htm)).
>
> Comment VII states the CPC's conclusion that "the unity of the socialist camp and the international communist movement can be safeguarded and strengthened only by remaining loyal to Marxism-Leninism and proletarian internationalism and by opposing modern revisionism and modern dogmatism" ([4 February 1964](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/polemic/splitters.htm)).

These texts were official Party positions when published. They are evidence of what the CPC argued, not records of how Soviet leaders understood themselves.

## The Cultural Revolution, 1966 to 1976

The Cultural Revolution is the point at which the retrospective Party verdict and the movement's own defenders diverge most sharply. The 1981 Resolution describes it as lasting "from May 1966 to October 1976" and as "responsible for the most severe setback and the heaviest losses suffered by the Party, the state and the people since the founding of the People's Republic". It states that it "was initiated and led by Comrade Mao Zedong" ([1981 Resolution, §19](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The Resolution summarises his theses: that "many representatives of the bourgeoisie and counter-revolutionary revisionists had sneaked into the Party, the government, the army and cultural circles"; that "Party persons in power taking the capitalist road had formed a bourgeois headquarters inside the Central Committee"; and that "the cultural revolution was in fact a great political revolution in which one class would overthrow another, a revolution that would have to be waged time and again." These theses "appeared mainly in the May 16 Circular" and in the 1969 Ninth Congress political report. In the Resolution's words, they "were incorporated into a general theory", the "theory of continued revolution under the dictatorship of the proletariat" ([§19](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

Its verdict is blunt. "Comrade Mao Zedong's principal theses for initiating this revolution conformed neither to Marxism, Leninism nor to Chinese reality. They represent an entirely erroneous appraisal of the prevailing class relations and political situation in the Party and state" ([§20](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). "Practice has shown that the 'cultural revolution' did not in fact constitute a revolution or social progress in any sense" ([§21(4)](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). "Chief responsibility... does indeed lie with Comrade Mao Zedong. But after all it was the error of a great proletarian revolutionary" ([§22](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The Resolution identifies three stages: to the Ninth Congress in April 1969, to the Tenth Congress in August 1973, and to October 1976. It records the Lin Biao clique's attempted coup in 1970 and 1971, the rise of the "Gang of Four," and Mao's criticisms of Jiang Qing and others ([§21](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

Official Mao-era texts state the opposing case in the movement's own terms. Lin Biao's report to the Ninth Congress (April 1969) says: "The current Great Proletarian Cultural Revolution is absolutely necessary and most timely for consolidating the dictatorship of the proletariat, preventing capitalist restoration and building socialism". It calls the Cultural Revolution "a great political revolution personally initiated and led by our great leader Chairman Mao under the conditions of the dictatorship of the proletariat, a great revolution in the realm of the superstructure" ([Lin Biao, 1969](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/lin-biao/1969/04/01.htm)). Here superstructure means the legal, political and cultural institutions resting on the economic base. A *Peking Review* article of 1975 puts the theoretical claim in these terms:

> "In the entire historical period of socialism, there are still struggles between progress and retrogression, innovation and conservatism, restoration and counter-restoration. The dictatorship of the proletariat can only be consolidated by continuing the revolution."
>
> Source: [*Peking Review*, "Socialism Is the Class Dictatorship of the Proletariat" (11 April 1975)](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/peking-review/1975/PR1975-15a.htm)

Hua Guofeng's statement of 25 December 1976, delivered while Mao-era positions still held official sway, gives the most compact definition. Mao "pointed out in clear-cut terms that, after the socialist transformation of the ownership of the means of production has in the main been completed and in the entire historical period of socialist society, there are still classes, class contradictions and class struggle, there is the struggle between the socialist road and the capitalist road, and there is the danger of capitalist restoration" ([Hua Guofeng, 1976](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hua-guofeng/1976/12/25.htm)). Lin Biao's 1969 report and Hua's 1976 speech were official positions of their day, not later dissident currents.

Post-1976 anti-revisionist currents took a different stand. The 1984 RIM Declaration describes "the reactionary coup d'état in China following the death of Mao Tsetung and the treacherous betrayal of Enver Hoxha" and treats the Cultural Revolution as part of the "historic appeal" of the anti-revisionist movement ([RIM Declaration, 1984](https://foreignlanguages.press/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/RIM-%E2%80%93-Declaration-1984.pdf)). A 1981 anti-revisionist assessment calls the 1981 Resolution "a compromised document" whose "revisionist essence" lay in its treatment of Mao's line ([Cynthia Lai, *Workers Viewpoint*, 1981](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/ncm-7/cwp-cpc-reversal.htm)). These are statements of political currents with institutional locations and dates; their claims about the Cultural Revolution are not the same kind of evidence as a demographic series or an archival study.

Historians have written a different kind of account. Roderick MacFarquhar and Michael Schoenhals's *Mao's Last Revolution* (Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 2006), drawing on Chinese documentary materials gathered over decades, argues that Mao deliberately used the Red Guards against the party and that tens of thousands of officials were humiliated, tortured or killed before the army restored order, often brutally ([Harvard University Press](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674027480); [JSTOR, DOI 10.2307/j.ctvk12s4j](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvk12s4j)). Maurice Meisner's *Mao's China and After* (3rd edition, Free Press, 1999) treats the Cultural Revolution as the outcome of Mao's continuing-revolution theory and of tensions between party bureaucracy and mass mobilisation, and places it within the entire arc of the People's Republic ([Simon & Schuster](https://web.archive.org/web/20260210085440/https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Maos-China-and-After/Maurice-Meisner/9780684856353)).

The experience of ordinary people is documented in memoirs and interviews. Ma Bo's *Blood Red Sunset* (Chinese edition 1987, English translation by Howard Goldblatt, Viking, 1995) is the memoir of an educated youth who went to Inner Mongolia, joined other young people in writing a petition in their own blood, took part in "re-education" campaigns against herders and others labelled class enemies, was denounced as a counterrevolutionary after criticising a leader, and was imprisoned. It became a bestseller in China and a key text of the genre. It is a retrospective self-defence by a former participant and victim, and literary scholars have noted that Ma presents himself mainly as a victim even when he describes his own violence ([Penguin Random House](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/324126/blood-red-sunset-by-ma-bo); [study of Red Guard memoirs](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/55e34fd6-fb1e-4cc4-8faf-8a42bb8d6a77/content)). The CR/10 oral history project at the University of Pittsburgh records short video interviews with people who lived through the Cultural Revolution and with younger people who learned of it later, preserving differing memories rather than a single verdict ([CR/10 project](https://culturalrevolution.pitt.edu/)). For workers, Andrew Walder's *Communist Neo-Traditionalism: Work and Authority in Chinese Industry* (University of California Press, 1986), based on official sources and intensive interviews with Hong Kong residents who had worked in mainland factories, describes permanent employment, enterprise provision of welfare and a relation of personal dependence between workers and management. Walder's interviews were with people who had left China, a limit he sets out in an appendix on method ([UC Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/communist-neo-traditionalism/pdf)).

The Lin Biao affair is part of this sequence. The Resolution states that "in 1970-71 the counter-revolutionary Lin Biao clique plotted to capture supreme power and attempted an armed counterrevolutionary coup d'etat... Comrades Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai ingeniously thwarted the plotted coup" ([§21(2)](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). Mao died on 9 September 1976. In October 1976 the Jiang Qing clique, the "Gang of Four," was overthrown, an event the Resolution says "saved the Party and the revolution from disaster" ([§25](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). The two years that followed included the "two whatevers" controversy, a dispute over the slogan that Party policy should follow whatever Mao decided and whatever he said. It was resolved at the Third Plenary Session of the 11th Central Committee in December 1978, which "began to correct conscientiously and comprehensively the 'Left' errors of the 'cultural revolution' and earlier", "discarded the slogan 'Take class struggle as the key link'", and "made the strategic decision to shift the focus of work to socialist modernization" ([1981 Resolution, §26](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

## The Three Worlds theory, 1974

The theory attributed to Mao is grounded in a specific conversation. On 22 February 1974, speaking with Zambian President Kenneth Kaunda:

> "Mao: Who belongs to the First World? ... I hold that the U.S. and the Soviet Union belong to the First World. The middle elements, such as Japan, Europe, Australia and Canada, belong to the Second World. We are the Third World... All Asian countries, except Japan, belong to the Third World. All of Africa and also Latin America belong to the Third World."
>
> Source: [Mao Zedong, "On the Question of the Differentiation of the Three Worlds," in *Mao Zedong on Diplomacy* (1998), transcribed by the Encyclopedia of Anti-Revisionism On-Line](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/china/mao-3-worlds.htm)

The official elaboration came after Mao's death, in a *People's Daily* editorial of November 1977, *Chairman Mao's Theory of the Differentiation of the Three Worlds Is a Major Contribution to Marxism-Leninism*. It argues that "in appearance, this theory of Chairman Mao's seems to involve only relations between countries and between nations in the present-day world, but, in essence, it bears directly on the vital question of present-day class struggle on a world scale. In the final analysis, national struggle is a matter of class struggle" ([Editorial Department of *Renmin Ribao*, 1977](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/ncm-5/theory-3-worlds/section1.htm)). The editorial identifies the two superpowers as the common enemies, the Third World as the main force, and the Second World as a force that can be united with, in a five-part series summarised by the [Editorial Departments of *Renmin Ribao* and *Hongqi*, 1977](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/ncm-5/cpml-3.htm). The Resolution's §22 endorses this: in his later years Mao "outlined the correct strategy of the three worlds and advanced the important principle that China would never seek hegemony" ([1981 Resolution, §22](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)).

The theory was contested within the Marxist-Leninist camp. The Albanian leadership "rejected the TTW and did so publicly at their 7th Congress (November 1-7, 1976)" ([CPA (M-L), *Australian Communist*](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/australia/cpaml-3-worlds.pdf)). A 1980 critique by Robert Seltzer and Irwin Silber in *Line of March* argued that the formulation "first advanced in public fashion in 1974" by Deng Xiaoping was "a total lack of class content" and "glaring empiricism" ([Seltzer and Silber, *Line of March*, 1980](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/ncm-5/lom-3.htm)).

## The 1981 Resolution and the politics of memory

The Resolution was adopted unanimously by the Sixth Plenary Session of the 11th Central Committee, meeting 27 to 29 June 1981. It was drafted under Deng Xiaoping and Hu Yaobang, with Hu Qiaomu leading the drafting group. Deng's instructions are themselves primary evidence of the balance sought: "First, affirmation of the historical role of Comrade Mao Zedong and explanation of the necessity to uphold and develop Mao Zedong Thought. This is the most essential point. We must hold high the banner of Mao Zedong Thought not only today but in the future" ([Deng's remarks on successive drafts, 1980-81](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/deng-xiaoping/1981/15.htm)).

Its core appraisal reads:

> "Comrade Mao Zedong was a great Marxist and a great proletarian revolutionary, strategist and theorist. It is true that he made gross mistakes during the 'cultural revolution', but, if we judge his activities as a whole, his contributions to the Chinese revolution far outweigh his mistakes. His merits are primary and his errors secondary."
>
> Source: [1981 Resolution, §27](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)

The Resolution's definition of Mao Zedong Thought remains the official one: "the product of the integration of the universal principles of Marxism-Leninism with the concrete practice of the Chinese revolution... a crystallization of the collective wisdom of the Chinese Communist Party" ([§28](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). It enumerates six fields of original contribution: the new-democratic revolution; socialist revolution and construction; the building of the revolutionary army and military strategy; policy and tactics; ideological, political and cultural work; and Party building ([§29](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). Its "living soul" is "to seek truth from facts, the mass line, and independence" ([§30](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/01.htm)). Hu Yaobang's speech of 1 July 1981 presented the Resolution as "a new milestone for our Party and state in the course of bringing order out of chaos, carrying on our cause and forging ahead" ([Hu Yaobang, 1981](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/documents/cpc/history/02.htm)).

The PRC Constitution's Preamble, as amended through 2018, states that "Both the victory in China's New Democratic Revolution and the successes in its socialist cause have been achieved by the Chinese people of all ethnic groups under the leadership of the Communist Party of China and the guidance of Marxism-Leninism and Mao Zedong Thought by upholding truth, correcting errors, and surmounting many difficulties and obstacles". It lists Mao Zedong Thought alongside Deng Xiaoping Theory, the Theory of Three Represents, the Scientific Outlook on Development and Xi Jinping Thought as guiding ideologies ([PRC Constitution](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/lawsregulations/201911/20/content_WS5ed8856ec6d0b3f0e9499913.html)).

At the 130th anniversary commemoration on 26 December 2023, Xi Jinping called Mao "a great Marxist, and a great proletarian revolutionary, strategist, and theorist", "a great trailblazer in adapting Marxism to the Chinese context", and "the core of the Party's first generation of central leadership". He said Mao "laid the groundwork of China's socialist modernization" and that "Mao Zedong Thought is the precious spiritual asset of our Party and will continue to guide what we do for a long time to come" ([Xinhua report, 28 December 2023](https://english.www.gov.cn/news/202312/28/content_WS658cae7ec6d0868f4e8e28fa.html)). I use the official English wording here. In the Chinese text of the speech published in *People's Daily*, Xi also says that it cannot be denied that Mao "made detours" in exploring the road of socialist construction, especially in launching and leading the Cultural Revolution, and repeats the 1981 formula that Mao's achievements come first and his mistakes second (translated from the Chinese text in [*People's Daily*, 26 December 2023](http://politics.people.com.cn/n1/2023/1226/c1024-40147163.html)). The 1981 judgement and the 2023 commemoration thus stand in a direct line of descent.

## Maoism beyond China

*Quotations from Chairman Mao Tse-tung* was "published from 1964 to about 1976 and widely distributed during China's Cultural Revolution". "Billions were produced, the most popular versions being in bright red covers in small sizes that could be easily carried, becoming commonly known internationally as The Little Red Book" ([MIA ebook edition, 2019](https://www.marxists.org/ebooks/mao/Quotations_from_Chairman_Mao_Tse-tung.pdf)). Lin Biao's foreword to the second edition (16 December 1966) explains its intended function: "We have compiled Quotations from Chairman Mao Tse-tung in order to help the broad masses learn Mao Tse-tung's thought more effectively." It also says "Once Mao Tse-tung's thought is grasped by the broad masses, it becomes a source of strength and a spiritual atom bomb of infinite power" ([Lin Biao, 1966](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/lin-biao/1966/12/16.htm)). A 7 July 1967 issue of *Peking Review* reported that "over 800,000 copies of the treasured red book... have been distributed in 117 countries and regions in the past eight months", in 14 languages, and that "revolutionary people and progressive organizations in many countries have undertaken their own translation and publication work" ([*Peking Review*, Vol. 10, No. 28, 1967](https://www.marxists.org/subject/china/peking-review/1967/PR1967-28.pdf)). These figures are reported by that issue of the magazine; the source is an image scan that cannot be searched as text. The book is organised thematically, with Party, class struggle, socialism and communism, the mass line, people's war, and criticism and self-criticism among its chapters ([chapter index](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/mao/works/red-book/index.htm)).

The current that later called itself Marxist-Leninist-Maoism traces its organisational line to the anti-revisionist polemics of the 1960s. The 1984 Declaration of the Revolutionary Internationalist Movement, adopted by delegates and observers at the Second International Conference of Marxist-Leninist Parties and Organisations, with participants including the Communist Party of Peru, the Revolutionary Communist Party USA, the Communist Party of India (Marxist-Leninist) and the Nepal Communist Party (Mashal, rendered "Marshal" in the Declaration), states: "At the present juncture of world history, the international proletariat has to take up the challenge of forming its own organisation, an International of a new type based on Marxism-Leninism-Mao Tsetung Thought" ([RIM Declaration, March 1984](https://foreignlanguages.press/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/RIM-%E2%80%93-Declaration-1984.pdf); [list of participating organisations, *A World to Win*, 1985](https://www.bannedthought.net/International/RIM/AWTW/1985-1/AWTW-01-DeclarationAdAndMembers.pdf)).

A doctrinal shift followed. The Communist Party of Peru, led by Abimael Guzmán, known as Chairman Gonzalo, argued that "Mao Tse-tung Thought" did not "give proper weight to the contributions of Mao" and that the science should be called Marxism-Leninism-Maoism. In its view, "the contributions of Mao are on the level, of the same magnitude, as those of the other great revolutionary leaders and theoreticians, Marx and Lenin" ([RCP USA, *Marxism-Leninism-Maoism*](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/ncm-7/rcp-maoism.pdf); [Committee Sol Peru - London, 1994](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/uk.ebbingtide/maoism-two-lines.pdf)). RIM's later documents, including *Long Live Marxism-Leninism-Maoism* (1993) and the *Millennium Resolution*, are described by a 2012 MLM resolution as "important theoretical contributions to the advancement of the Movement", which also notes internationalist support for the people's wars in Peru and Nepal ([CPI (M-L) Naxalbari et al., 1 May 2012](https://www.marxists.org/subject/india/cpiml-naxalbari/Reso2-120501.pdf)). A 2011 assessment notes that RIM "advanced to the height of adopting Marxism-Leninism-Maoism and playing a key role in gaining wider adherence to this ideological position within the international Maoist movement". The same assessment records that the movement later became "defunct for all practical purposes" ([CPI (M-L) Naxalbari, *Maoist Road* no. 1, 2011](https://www.marxists.org/subject/india/cpiml-naxalbari/2011/situation-in-rim-maoistroad-n1.pdf)). These statements come from the movements' own documents and record their internal debates.

The arguments over Mao's record are not only about the past. They turn on what counts as evidence: a party's retrospective verdict, a census series, a local archive, a village interview, a memoir written years later. The Resolution supplies an official framework and a political judgement. Demographers, archival historians and oral historians supply different kinds of evidence and different degrees of confidence. An account that names those differences tells the reader more than one that simply sets two political verdicts against each other.
