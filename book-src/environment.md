Friedrich Engels walked the industrial districts of Manchester in the 1840s and wrote down what he saw. The river below the town was not scenery but a consequence of how production was organised:

> "At the bottom flows, or rather stagnates, the Irk, a narrow, coal-black, foul-smelling stream, full of debris and refuse, which it deposits on the shallower right bank."
>
> Source: [Friedrich Engels, *The Condition of the Working Class in England*, written 1844–45, English edition 1887](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1845/condition-working-class/ch04.htm)

Engels was a young businessman living in the city, and his book mixes his own observation with official reports. It is a witness's polemic, not a measurement. Marx, in *Capital*, described work as a process in which people and nature act on one another, an exchange of matter that goes on in every society ([Marx, *Capital*, Volume I, Chapter 7](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1867-c1/ch07.htm)). In an unfinished essay written in 1876 Engels warned that interventions in nature bring unforeseen consequences, from deforestation to lost soil, and connected that warning to the way production is organised:

> "Let us not, however, flatter ourselves overmuch on account of our human victories over nature. For each such victory nature takes its revenge on us."
>
> Source: [Frederick Engels, "The Part Played by Labour in the Transition from Ape to Man", written 1876, translated by Clemens Dutt (1934)](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1876/part-played-labour/index.htm)

None of this describes an actually existing socialist state. These are nineteenth-century texts, translated and selectively written, which later readers used to argue that pollution was structural rather than accidental. Whether they amount to a theory of ecology is still disputed.

## Water, fish and a sea that disappeared

Cotton did more damage to the Aral Sea than any single factory. Irrigation diverted the rivers that fed it, and a fishing economy at Muynak came apart. Jasmurad Kenesbay, a fisherman and state-farm member, remembered the record catch of 1957 and the end of commercial fishing by 1982. A state farm is a farm owned and run by the government.

> "I don't think I will ever fish again," ... "As the sea dies, so does this village. Our entire economy has been destroyed and so have our lives."
>
> Source: [Jasmurad Kenesbay, fisherman at Muynak, quoted by Don Hinrichsen, 1995. Ellipsis marks the reporter's intervening words](https://serc.carleton.edu/files/sp/library/cases/examples/aral_sea_description_circa.pdf)

The reporter recorded exposed seabed, salt and pesticide dust, and a cannery still processing frozen fish shipped in from the Baltic. Almas Tolvashev had been a captain at Moynaq:

> "I was the first Muslim captain in Moynaq and my ship was the Volga... There were 250 ships here. I used to catch 600-700 kilos of fish every day. Now there is no sea."
>
> Source: [Almas Tolvashev, former fishing captain, interviewed by the BBC, 2018. Ellipsis marks omitted words](https://www.bbc.com/news/business-44159122)

Soviet authorities denied that the sea was disappearing until the USSR broke up, according to the same report. Both men speak decades after the events, through journalists, and neither is a measurement of the catch.

Criticism also came from inside the system. Soviet scientists and writers campaigned to protect Lake Baikal, and Douglas Weiner's archival study shows that scientist-led nature societies survived from the Stalin years onward, insisting they were patriots rather than dissidents. The campaigning letters and decrees themselves were not opened for this account; they reach us only in Weiner's English translation, and are not quoted here as primary text. By 1989 the shrinking Aral, dead sturgeon in the Volga and effluent from the Baikal pulp mill were being reported in the Soviet press. Fyodor Morgun, chairman of the new State Committee for Environmental Protection, said:

> "We have started too late," Morgun told the group. "Our air is not up to the proper mark, our soil is polluted, and our forests are affected."
>
> Source: [Fyodor Morgun, chairman of Goskompriroda, quoted by Dick Thompson, TIME, 2 January 1989](https://time.com/archive/6701510/planet-of-the-year-the-greening-of-the-u-s-s-r/)

New fines, criminal proceedings and public protests were appearing under glasnost, the late-Soviet policy that permitted open criticism. Morgun was speaking in a new post to a foreign magazine, an official admitting a failure after the fact.

## The zone

In April 1986 a reactor at Chernobyl exploded. The record of the clean-up is unusually consistent on one point: the men sent in were not told what radiation they had received. Valentin Kramarevskyy, one of the liquidators, the people drafted into the clean-up, put it this way:

> "But I am still convinced that with 90% of liquidators the dose does not correspond to what they had received. Both ways."
>
> Source: [Valentin Aleksandrovich Kramarevskyy, Chernobyl History Workshop oral history, translated from Russian](http://www.1986.org.ua/en/archive/memories)

Doses were averaged across a platoon, and individual dosimeters were unreliable. Natalia Manzurova, a radiation biologist who worked in the zone from 1987, said the instruments could not be read by the people wearing them and were handed in at the end of a shift:

> "These instruments could not be read by the people using them – they were, instead, turned in after work to be read by a radiation specialist. We were not told what our doses were."
>
> Source: [Natalia Manzurova, liquidator, memoir excerpt published 2019](https://beyondnuclearinternational.org/2019/04/21/hard-duty-in-the-chernobyl-zone)

She describes twelve-hour shifts, shared bunks, the evacuation of Pripyat, contaminated household goods bulldozed into graves, and her own thyroid surgery. Soldiers were used on the reactor roof, called green robots in the accounts collected by Svetlana Alexievich. Arkady Filin, a liquidator and history teacher, remembered the camp:

> "You immediately found yourself in this fantastic world, where the apocalypse met the stone age. We lived in the forest, in tents, 200km from the reactor, like partisans."
>
> Source: [Arkady Filin, liquidator and history teacher, in Svetlana Alexievich, *Voices from Chernobyl*, extract 2005, translated from Russian, translator unnamed in the extract](https://r.jina.ai/https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2005/apr/25/energy.ukraine)

These are retrospective interviews, translated with no translator named, from collections curated by activists and historians. They agree about secrecy and protection, but they cannot settle the number of people harmed or the relation between a recorded dose and a particular illness.

## Elsewhere, and a limit to growth

Environmental damage was not only a socialist record. At Love Canal in New York state, Lois Gibbs found chemicals buried near her home, canvassed the neighbourhood and formed an association of more than a thousand families. In testimony to a congressional committee in 1979 she gave a date and a place:

> "This is especially alarming since on Friday, March 9th, thick, black, oily leachate was found running off the north section of the canal onto the street and into the storm sewers."
>
> Source: [Lois M. Gibbs, congressional testimony, 21 March 1979](https://library.buffalo.edu/content/dam/library/pdf/special-collections/archives/Lois%20M.%20Gibbs,%20March%2021,%201979%20%2815%20pages%29.pdf)

Leachate is liquid that has passed through waste and picked up chemicals. Going door to door with a petition to close the school, Gibbs later recalled finding illness in nearly every household:

> "It seemed as though every home on 99th Street had someone with an illness."
>
> Source: [Lois Gibbs, *Love Canal: My Story*, 1982](http://www.andreasaltelli.eu/file/repository/LOVE_CANAL.pdf)

She was an advocate speaking to a sympathetic committee, and the illness clusters she described were contested at the time.

At Minamata in Japan, industrial mercury poisoning was denied for years. Masami Ogata hid his own condition, was refused compensation ten times, and was certified in 2007 as the 2,266th patient. His grandfather died of the acute disease and his sister was born with the form affecting unborn children. He became a storyteller at the disease museum:

> "Despite being turned down 10 times, I believed all throughout my appeals that the world would allow me to be happy if I continued to plead my case with honesty and sincerity."
>
> Source: [Masami Ogata, written statement for the Minamata Convention conference, 2013, English translation uncredited in the document](https://www.env.go.jp/en/chemi/mercury/mcm/attach/ip04a_storytellers.pdf)

W. Eugene Smith and Aileen M. Smith lived in Minamata for three years, photographed patients and demonstrations, and accompanied victims to court ([Magnum Photos](https://magnumphotos.com/arts-culture/society-arts-culture/w-eugene-smith-minamata-warning-to-the-world)). Their photographs made a movement visible; the chemical causation was established elsewhere.

In 1972 a report by an MIT team for the Club of Rome, a group the report called an invisible college founded by Aurelio Peccei, offered another vocabulary:

> "If the present growth trends in world population, industrialization, pollution, food production, and resource depletion continue unchanged, the limits to growth on this planet will be reached sometime within the next one hundred years."
>
> Source: [Meadows, Meadows, Randers and Behrens, *The Limits to Growth*, 1972](https://collections.dartmouth.edu/archive/text/meadows/diplomatic/meadows_ltg-diplomatic.html)

The report modelled five interacting trends, and its authors called such predictions only in the most limited sense; the model was criticised for its assumptions about resources and technology. *Limits to Growth* put the problem in growth itself. Socialist ecology placed it in the social relations of production. The Aral and Baikal cases can be read either way: both were products of state plans that treated water and forest as free inputs.

## How historians have read it

- **Douglas R. Weiner, *A Little Corner of Freedom: Russian Nature Protection from Stalin to Gorbachëv* (1999).** [Source: publishing.cdlib.org](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/a-little-corner-of-freedom-russian-nature-protection-from-stalin-to-gorbachev.html); Using Soviet archives and interviews with movement veterans, Weiner argues that an independent, scientist-led nature-protection movement survived inside the USSR and helped force the Baikal issue onto the public agenda. He sets this against the older totalitarian view, quoted in his introduction, that no interest group independent of the Party hierarchy could exist. This is the key work for the claim that environmental dissent was not simply imported from the West.
- **John Bellamy Foster, *Marx's Ecology: Materialism and Nature* (2000).** [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL218101W); Foster reads Marx's notebooks and published texts as a systematic materialist ecology and treats the metabolic passages as central rather than incidental. His interpretation supports using the theoretical texts as foundations, but it is an interpretation: the case that Marx was an ecologist remains a live argument, and a competing scholarly critique was not verified here.
- **Marshall I. Goldman, *The Spoils of Progress: Environmental Pollution in the Soviet Union* (1972; MIT Press paperback 1975).** [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL2810312W); An early Western study that treats Soviet pollution as a result of the priorities and incentives of Soviet industry. It broadly agrees with Pryde on the scale of the problem and differs from Weiner in giving less weight to the internal political force of the conservationists.
- **Philip R. Pryde, *Environmental Management in the Soviet Union* (1991).** [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL4439311W); A geographical and policy survey covering air, water, land and conservation, including the Volga basin. Pryde documents the gap between Soviet environmental law and enforcement; his work is the administrative counterpart to the witness testimony above.
- **Serhii Plokhy, *Chernobyl: The History of a Nuclear Catastrophe* (2018).** [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL20669892W); A history built around the accident, the cover-up and the liquidation, using archival and interview material. It is the standard recent account to set beside Alexievich's literary oral history: the two agree on secrecy and inadequate protection but differ in method and in how far they follow individual testimony.

Broad scholarly agreement: Soviet industrial policy produced severe environmental damage, and official secrecy made it worse; the Aral, Baikal and Chernobyl cases are not presented by credible historians as inventions of foreign propaganda. Live disagreement: how much independent public opinion could achieve inside the Soviet system, how far Marx's writings amount to an ecological theory, and how to weigh liquidator testimony about doses and illness against official records. These are disagreements about evidence and interpretation, not equal claims.

## Limits of the record

The gravest gap is the primary documentary record for Baikal and Volga. Weiner quotes and dates the 1958 letter "In Defense of Baikal", Taurin's 1959 article, the 1966 letter "Baikal Waits" and a 1960 protection decree, but those newspapers and decrees were not opened. Language is a second barrier. No Russian or Ukrainian oral history was opened in the original; every Chernobyl quotation is a posted English translation with no translator named. Japanese victim organisations' own publications and court records were not opened, nor were Karakalpak, Uzbek or Russian accounts of the Aral. The surviving testimony is uneven and politically shaped: the Chernobyl archive and the anti-nuclear publication are curated, Love Canal's accessible voice became the movement's national spokeswoman, and Minamata's is a museum storyteller at a treaty conference. Each is genuine testimony, but none is a sample. Health claims, dose estimates and death-toll figures remain contested, and several sources report figures without sourcing them. This was a URL and text check only, with no physical archive access, no original print of *The Limits to Growth* and no full Alexievich or Smith book. Nothing here can recover the private reasoning of planners, or the views of the cotton farmers, plant managers and local officials who carried out the irrigation orders. No independently measured figure for deaths or illness in any of the four zones can be drawn from it.
