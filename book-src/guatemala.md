On 27 June 1954 President Jacobo Árbenz Guzmán went on the government radio station and read a speech written by his secretary, Manuel Fortuny. The audio is lost; the text survives in a later transcription. Exile aircraft were flying over the country. Árbenz told listeners what he thought was happening:

> "No me han acorralado los argumentos del enemigo, sino los medios materiales con lo que cuenta para la destrucción de Guatemala."
>
> Source: [Jacobo Árbenz, resignation broadcast, 27 June 1954, transcription no. 0020, HistoriaGT](https://www.historiagt.org/transcripciones/item/107-discursoarbenz)

He named the United Fruit Company, the American corporation that dominated the banana trade and the railway to the coast, and American "monopolies":

> "Han tomado pretexto al comunismo. La verdad es muy otra. La verdad hay que buscar en los intereses financieros de la compañía frutera."
>
> Source: [Jacobo Árbenz, resignation broadcast, 27 June 1954, HistoriaGT](https://www.historiagt.org/transcripciones/item/107-discursoarbenz)

He handed power to Colonel Carlos Enrique Díaz rather than let the country be destroyed. It was the end of the only democratically elected government Guatemala knew before 1996.

## The land question

The government had come to power after the October Revolution of 1944, an uprising that toppled the junta of Jorge Ubico's successor Federico Ponce Vaides. Guatemala elected two civilian presidents, wrote a constitution and legalised unions and a communist party. Its central promise was land. Decree 900 of 17 June 1952 abolished the forms of forced labour that bound rural workers to estates:

> "Quedan abolidas todas las formas de servidumbre y esclavitud, y por consiguiente prohibidas las prestaciones personales gratuitas de los campesinos"
>
> Source: [Decree 900, Agrarian Reform Law, Article 2, 17 June 1952](https://es.wikisource.org/wiki/Decreto_900,_Ley_de_Reforma_Agraria_%28Guatemala%29)

It exempted holdings of roughly ninety hectares, protected indigenous communal land, defined large uncultivated or rented estates as subject to expropriation with twenty-five-year bonds, and gave the peasant confederation seats on the agrarian committees. A law is not proof of implementation, but the Commission for Historical Clarification, the truth commission set up under the 1994 peace agreement, later recorded that by 1954 more than 138,000 peasant families, mostly indigenous, had benefited.

## Washington's decision

The reform alarmed Washington. CIA meetings began in November 1951. An internal paper of 4 September 1953 recorded that an earlier plan to arm exiles led by Carlos Castillo Armas had failed when the State Department refused an export licence:

> "All went well until the shipment had actually left the warehouse en route to New Orleans. The Department of State refused an export license"
>
> Source: [CIA draft paper, 4 September 1953, quoted in FRUS 1952–1954, Guatemala](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54Guat/d1)

The replacement operation, PBSUCCESS, was large and mostly political. A CIA memorandum of 1975 listed 85 trained exiles, clandestine radio, a three-million-dollar budget and nearly eighty air missions in a fortnight:

> "There were approximately 80 missions flown during the 14–29 June 1954 period, by various type aircraft such as C–47's, F–47's and Cessnas"
>
> Source: [CIA memorandum, "CIA's Role in the Overthrow of Arbenz", 12 May 1975, FRUS 1952–1954, Guatemala, document 287](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54Guat/d287)

Declassified files also show preparation for political killing. The agency's training material included a practical guide:

> "A hammer, axe, wrench, screw driver, fire poker, kitchen knife, lamp stand, or anything hard, heavy and handy will suffice."
>
> Source: [CIA, "A Study of Assassination", undated, declassified 1997, quoted by the National Security Archive](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB4/)

It warned:

> "no assassination instructions should ever be written or recorded"
>
> Source: [CIA, "A Study of Assassination", undated, National Security Archive](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB4/)

A memorandum of 31 March 1954 asked for a list of government leaders, party members and people of tactical importance whose removal was judged necessary for military action. Another split targets between those to be killed and those to be imprisoned or exiled. Every name on the released copies was deleted, so the lists prove a targeting system existed and that Castillo Armas knew of it, but not who was chosen. CIA headquarters had been compiling names for elimination as early as January 1952, and the option was still under consideration on the day Árbenz resigned ([Haines, *CIA and Guatemala Assassination Proposals 1952–1954*, 1995](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0000135796.pdf); [National Security Archive, *CIA and Assassinations: The Guatemala 1954 Documents*](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB4/index.html)). Press management was part of the operation. On 22 June the CIA told its station:

> "encourage your diplomatic colleagues report factually about absence bombing and especially fabrication of evidence and other Toriello lies"
>
> Source: [CIA cable drafted by Frank Wisner, 22 June 1954, FRUS 1952–1954, Guatemala, document 221](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54Guat/d221)

The claim that there was no bombing conflicted with other accounts and with the agency's own list of air missions.

## The invasion and the resignation

Castillo Armas's movement was not simply a CIA creation. Its Plan of Tegucigalpa, published in December 1953, promised social security, family wages and rural education while pledging to purge communist influence from unions and schools:

> "Por todos los medios y con todos los recursos combatiremos el comunismo internacional."
>
> Source: [*Plan de Tegucigalpa*, Movimiento de Liberación Nacional, 23 December 1953, transcription no. 0002, HistoriaGT](https://www.historiagt.org/transcripciones/item/52-plantegucigalpa)

It also promised an agrarian law that would distribute state land while leaving agricultural enterprises intact, protecting the estates Decree 900 had reached.

On 20 June 1954 Guatemala's representative told the UN Security Council what his government understood. Jorge Castillo Arriola gave an hour-by-hour account of bombings and border crossings, named air bases in Honduras and displayed what he said were letters between Castillo Armas and the Somoza family:

> "The battle for Guatemala has begun. The battle which has threatened us for so long has started."
>
> Source: [Jorge Castillo Arriola, UN Security Council, meeting 675, 20 June 1954, translated from Spanish in the UN record](https://un-project.org/meeting/S-PV-675/)

He took care to make no accusation against the United States. The Council referred the matter to the Organization of American States.

Ernesto Guevara, a twenty-five-year-old Argentine doctor then living in Guatemala, described aircraft machine-gunning the city in letters to his mother and tried to reach the fighting:

> "I volunteered for the front, but they didn't pay me a blind bit of notice…"
>
> Source: [Ernesto Guevara to his mother, June 1954, published translation](https://www.thetedkarchive.com/library/ernesto-che-guevara-the-diaries-of-ernesto-che-guevara)

After the resignation:

> "The persecution began at once and the embassies filled up with people seeking asylum."
>
> Source: [Ernesto Guevara to his mother, late June 1954, published translation](https://www.thetedkarchive.com/library/ernesto-che-guevara-the-diaries-of-ernesto-che-guevara)

His letters are the impressions of a committed participant, edited decades later, and some of his assessments were wrong; on the machine-gunning and the asylum seekers they agree with the ambassador. The Commission for Historical Clarification later recorded that the army high command refused to arm popular organisations and held its air force back, which makes the CIA's language of an "unblemished triumph" hard to reconcile with the record.

## What followed

The men who took power rewrote the agrarian law, banned the left and closed political life ([CEH, *Guatemala: Memoria del Silencio*, 1999, para. 321](https://www.centrodememoriahistorica.gov.co/descargas/guatemala-memoria-silencio/guatemala-memoria-del-silencio.pdf); [Guatemala Memoria Viva, "1954–1957"](http://idgt.url.edu.gt/guatemalamemoriaviva/1954-1957-un-nuevo-orden-en-desorden)). The Commission, reporting in 1999 on the roots of the civil war that ended in 1996, treated the coup as a decisive break: the invasion's effect was psychological rather than military, and surviving peasants described abuses in the countryside. Its figures of 9,000 to 14,000 detentions and 2,000 to 5,000 executions were analysts' estimates, not verified counts.

A line often attributed to Castillo Armas says: "If it is necessary to turn the country into a cemetery in order to pacify it, I will not hesitate to do so." The sourced version belongs to President Carlos Arana Osorio in 1971, seventeen years later, and cannot be used as evidence about 1954.

## How historians have read it

- **Piero Gleijeses, *Shattered Hope: The Guatemalan Revolution and the United States, 1944–1954* (Princeton University Press, 1991/92).** [Internet Archive snapshot: shattered hope](https://web.archive.org/web/20260101055559/https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691025568/shattered-hope), Combines archival research with interviews to argue that US intervention was decisive and ruinous but cannot be reduced to one "convenient villain"; the standard account against which the others are measured.
- **Richard H. Immerman, *The CIA in Guatemala: The Foreign Policy of Intervention* (University of Texas Press, 1982).** [Source: utpress.utexas.edu](https://utpress.utexas.edu/9780292710832/), Uses FOIA documents, opened archives and interviews with participants to trace the policy from Truman to Eisenhower; agrees with Gleijeses on the centrality of US intervention and puts more weight on policy-making in Washington.
- **Nick Cullather, *Secret History: The CIA's Classified Account of Its Operations in Guatemala, 1952–1954* (Stanford University Press, 1999; second edition 2006).** [Source: sup.org](https://www.sup.org/books/history/secret-history-second-edition), Publishes the CIA's own internal history with notes and, in the second edition, annotated documents; stresses how poorly the operation was run and how successfully it was mythologised afterwards.
- **Stephen Schlesinger and Stephen Kinzer, *Bitter Fruit: The Story of the American Coup in Guatemala* (1982; revised Harvard University Press, 1999).** A translated passage was consulted at [Source: mrgonzalezhistory.weebly.com](https://mrgonzalezhistory.weebly.com/uploads/1/3/4/8/13483619/bitter_fruit.pdf), Narrative account built from interviews with participants and contemporary reporting; the source for the English text of Árbenz's 19 June radio address, used here as a lead rather than a verified primary text because the PDF was not opened directly.
- **Stephen Kinzer, *Overthrow: America's Century of Regime Change from Hawaii to Iraq* (Henry Holt, 2006).** Popular synthesis that places Guatemala in a long series; useful for comparison, but its compression produces the misattributed cemetery quotation noted above, so it should not be used as a source for that line.
- **Comisión para el Esclarecimiento Histórico, *Guatemala: Memoria del Silencio* (1999).** [Source: undp.org](https://www.undp.org/es/guatemala/publicaciones/guatemala-memoria-del-silencio), Official truth commission report; its interpretation of the "trauma of the 54" and the link between the coup and the later insurgency is widely accepted, while its numerical estimates for 1954 repression remain indicative rather than settled.

Broad agreement: the CIA organised PBSUCCESS, the invasion force was small, psychological operations mattered more than battlefield success, the Guatemalan army declined to defend Árbenz, and the coup opened a long period of military rule. Live disagreement remains over how much weight to give United Fruit's lobbying against Cold War strategy, how many people were killed in the days after June 1954, and how far the revolution's own rhetoric and the existence of the PGT contributed to its isolation.

## Limits of the record

A dated address by Árbenz of 16 June 1954 has not been verified. The speeches that can be placed are the radio address of 19 June, known through a published translation in *Bitter Fruit* and a US embassy despatch neither of which was opened directly, and the resignation broadcast of 27 June. A scholarly article refers to an "informe presentado a la sociedad guatemalteca 16 de junio de 1954" in *Diario de Centro América*, but that issue was not available; the two should not be assumed to be the same document. The "Acción Democrática" material sometimes listed for this episode could not be located in a Guatemalan context and appears to be a conflation with the Venezuelan party. No "liberation army" leaflet or Voice of Liberation script was opened in the original.

Language and archive access are serious constraints. Most usable first-hand material is in Spanish, and some of it, Guevara's letters and the Commission's testimony, exists in translation or through edited republication. Indigenous-language testimony would need translation and careful attribution. No physical archive was visited: the original radio audio, the 1952 to 1954 issues of *El Imparcial* and *Diario de Centro América*, the full CIA PDFs and the photographs and film of the bombardment remain unchecked. Officials, exiles and foreign witnesses are well represented; peasants, indigenous villagers and women organisers appear mainly as categories in commission reports. More than 100,000 pages of PBSUCCESS records are thought to remain closed, roughly 1,400 were released in 1997, and every name on the surviving target lists was deleted.
