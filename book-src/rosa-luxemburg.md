Rosa Luxemburg's arguments were about two limits: the limits of capitalism and the limits of revolutionary parties. She held that capitalism drew on markets and peoples outside its own class relations, that reforms could not replace the abolition of capitalism, and that a socialist movement which denied its own members a voice would reproduce the rule it wanted to end. Most of her writing was produced inside arguments among socialists. This profile follows those arguments in her own texts, records what the opened sources say about her life, and reports the objections made by contemporaries and later writers.

## Biography

Rosa Luxemburg was born on 5 March 1871 in Zamość, then in the Russian-controlled part of Poland, and was killed in Berlin on 15 January 1919. Both dates and places appear in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry and in the Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung's biographical page. The SEP calls her the youngest of five children in an assimilated Jewish family of timber merchants; the Stiftung says her father was a wood merchant and that the family moved to Warsaw when she was two. The SEP adds that a hip illness contracted at about the age of three left her with a limp for life, and that she taught herself to read and write during a year in bed. These are secondary biographical claims, reported as the two sources give them, not facts drawn from a primary document opened for this profile.

Both sources agree that she was drawn to illegal revolutionary groups while at school in Warsaw, left Poland in 1889 under threat of arrest, and enrolled at the University of Zurich. The Stiftung says she first studied natural sciences and then political science and economics, and that she obtained her doctorate in 1897. The SEP says she switched from philosophy to law, wrote a dissertation called "The Industrial Development of Poland", and places her move to Germany in 1898. Paul Mattick, writing in 1978, dates the dissertation to 1898. The discrepancy over 1897 and 1898 has not been resolved in the sources opened here. In Zurich she worked with Leo Jogiches and helped found the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland (SDKP), which later became the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland and Lithuania (SDKPiL). Her opposition to Polish independence within the existing capitalist order was already argued in the dissertation and remained a constant of her politics.

She moved to Germany in 1898 through a marriage of convenience that secured German citizenship, and became known in the SPD through her intervention in the debate over revisionism, the view associated with Eduard Bernstein that the final goal of socialism could be set aside in favour of reforms inside capitalism. The Stiftung records her first major international appearance at the Zurich congress of 1893, where she sought a mandate for the SDKP and was refused, though her ally Julian Marchlewski was admitted and read a speech she had written. The Stiftung's archive FAQ adds that in 1893 she declared at Zurich that "our current goal is not to fight for an independent Polish state, but for political freedom". That wording comes from a later biography quoted by the FAQ, so it is a quotation at second hand.

From 1907 to 1914 she taught economics at the SPD party school in Berlin. The SEP says she taught "Introduction to Economics" and "Marx's Capital" to rank-and-file members. She was repeatedly imprisoned: for anti-war speeches in 1914 and, according to the Stiftung, from July 1916 to November 1918 in Berlin, Wronki and Wrocław. The Junius Pamphlet and the manuscript on the Russian Revolution were written in prison. The Stiftung says she was released on 9 November 1918, edited *Die Rote Fahne* with Karl Liebknecht, helped found the Communist Party of Germany at the turn of 1918–19, and was murdered with Liebknecht on 15 January 1919 by officers and soldiers of Reichswehr units. The SEP gives a fuller account of the final days: a mass demonstration in Berlin in early January, the occupation of railway stations and newspaper offices, the decision of Luxemburg and the Spartacists to urge caution, the appointment of Gustav Noske as interior minister, the Freikorps repression, and the killing of Luxemburg and Liebknecht after their hiding place was found. The SEP also says her body was thrown into the Landwehr Canal and recovered months later. These are scholarly secondary accounts; they agree on the outline but differ in emphasis. The Berlin rising is covered in this book's chapter on the [German Revolution](/german-revolution).

One textual point belongs here because it affects a quotation used later. The sentence about freedom for the person who thinks differently appears in the Marxists Internet Archive translation of chapter 6 of *The Russian Revolution* as part of the chapter's running text. The Stiftung FAQ says the passage is a marginal note on the unfinished 1918 manuscript, not a line in the manuscript's continuous argument, and gives a slightly different English translation: "Freedom is always the freedom of dissenters". Both readings are acknowledged here; the MIA wording is the one quoted in the section on criticisms.

## Intellectual tradition

Luxemburg worked inside Marxism and spent most of her career arguing with other Marxists. The SEP places her in classical Marxism but stresses that she rejected both the parliamentary reformism of the SPD leadership and the party centralism of Lenin's Bolsheviks. She was a founder of the SDKP, a leading figure in the German SPD's left wing, a co-founder of the Spartacus League and of the KPD, and a critic of the SPD's support for war credits in 1914. The Stiftung's "Democratic Socialism" page says that from her standpoint both the reformist SPD and the Bolshevik dictatorship remained within a bourgeois political tradition, because both treated the underprivileged as a constituency to be represented rather than as authors of their own emancipation. That is the Stiftung's interpretation of her position, but it is supported by her own texts opened here.

Her relations to labels were specific. She called herself a social democrat for most of her life and did not live to see the party she helped found turn into a Leninist party. The MIA publication note for *Organizational Questions of the Russian Social Democracy* records that the essay was later reprinted as *Marxism vs. Leninism*, appeared in English in 1934 as *Revolutionary Socialist Organization* and in 1935 as *Leninism or Marxism?*, and was reprinted by the University of Michigan Press in 1961 in a volume titled *The Russian Revolution and Leninism or Marxism?* with an introduction by Bertram Wolfe. That 1961 title is a combined edition of two different works, not her title for the 1918 manuscript. The distinction matters because the two texts are often merged in descriptions of her work.

She also stood outside the mainstream Marxist position on national self-determination. The SEP records that she argued that national liberation under capitalist conditions handed Polish workers from Russian to Polish capital, and that she criticised Lenin's support for the right of nations to secede. Lenin's own critique confirms the dispute: in "The Right of Nations to Self-Determination" he wrote that in her quest for "practicality" she had lost sight of the day-by-day struggle against national privileges. On women's emancipation she rejected what the SEP calls tokenistic feminism confined to the suffrage campaign, while supporting women's suffrage as part of class struggle. The Stiftung FAQ says she regarded women's suffrage primarily from the perspective of class struggle and not as an independent emancipatory demand.

## Major contributions

Her first major intervention was *Reform or Revolution*, written against Bernstein's argument that the final goal was nothing and the movement everything. Her central claim was that social democracy could not be against reforms, but that reforms and revolution had different functions: "The struggle for reforms is its means; the social revolution, its aim". She argued that Bernstein had reversed this relationship and, by abandoning the final aim, had in the end abandoned the movement as well. In chapter 8 she insisted that legal reform and revolution were not two methods that could be chosen "just as one chooses hot or cold sausages", but distinct factors in the development of class society. Every legal constitution was the product of a revolution, while legislation expressed a society already brought into being. She also defended democracy as indispensable to the workers' movement, because democratic rights give the working class the forms in which it can become conscious of its class interests.

The second contribution was her theory of accumulation and imperialism in *The Accumulation of Capital* (1913). By accumulation she meant the reinvestment of surplus value, the extra value workers produce beyond their wages, in order to expand capital. She took Marx's diagrams of enlarged reproduction in volume 2 of *Capital* and argued that they could not explain who buys the portion of surplus value that is accumulated. In a closed society of capitalists and workers, she wrote, the capitalised surplus value "cannot be realised by sale either to workers or to capitalists, but only if it is sold to such social organisations or strata whose own mode of production is not capitalistic". Capital therefore needed non-capitalist strata and countries as markets, as sources of raw materials and labour power, and as a buffer for its contradictions. Imperialism was the political form of this need, and militarism was, in her phrase, "a province of accumulation".

The third contribution was her account of the mass strike, a coordinated stoppage of work by large numbers of workers used as a political weapon and not only as a dispute over wages. In *The Mass Strike, the Political Party and the Trade Unions* (1906) she drew on the 1905 Russian revolution to argue that a mass strike could not be ordered by a party congress or banned by a trade-union prohibition. It was "a historical phenomenon which, at a given moment, results from social conditions with historical inevitability". She treated economic and political struggle as mutually reinforcing, and held that the strike was a form in which workers educated themselves for revolution rather than a technical instrument that could be switched on and off.

The fourth contribution was her critique of Lenin's party model in *Organizational Questions of the Russian Social Democracy* (1904) and her later critique of the Bolsheviks in power. In 1904 she argued that Lenin's centralism mechanically transferred Blanquism, the rule of a small organised nucleus, into a mass workers' movement, separating that nucleus from its social surroundings and requiring blind obedience to a central committee. In the unfinished 1918 manuscript on the Russian Revolution she generalised the point: socialism could not be decreed, and a dictatorship of a party was not a dictatorship of the class.

The fifth contribution, harder to classify, was her insistence that the form of political freedom was part of the content of socialism. The SEP argues that her central concern was freedom understood as individual and collective self-rule, and that she treated democratic practice as a learning process rather than a constitutional add-on. This is less a separate doctrine than the thread that connects her criticisms of Bernstein, Lenin and the Bolsheviks.

## Analysis of capitalism

Luxemburg's account of capitalism starts from surplus value and the compulsion to accumulate. In *Reform or Revolution* she defended Marx's theory of value against Bernstein, insisting that the abstraction of abstract human labour, the idea that different kinds of work count as equal social labour, was not an invention in Marx's head but a real social relation that takes the form of money, and that without the law of value the whole analysis of capitalism becomes unintelligible. She rejected the idea that trade unions or social reforms could gradually abolish exploitation. Trade unions could regulate the terms of the sale of labour power, the capacity to work that a worker sells for wages, but the factors that determine the demand and supply of labour and the level of productivity lay outside their control. At best they imposed the "normal" limit of exploitation at a given moment, not the abolition of exploitation. The state was not "society" but a class state, so labour legislation was a regulation of exploitation in the interests of capital, not a step towards socialism.

In *The Accumulation of Capital* she pushed the same logic to a global scale. Her detailed reading of Marx's reproduction schemas, his diagrams of how capital reproduces itself on an expanding scale, led her to conclude that the diagrams assumed a self-sufficient capitalist world of capitalists and workers. That assumption was useful for abstract analysis but false as a description of capitalist history. Real accumulation, she argued, had always depended on non-capitalist surroundings. Capital needed external buyers for the realised surplus value, means of production and raw materials produced outside capitalist relations, and labour power released from pre-capitalist forms of production. She illustrated this with the English cotton industry's dependence on cotton from the slave-owning American South, Russian serf-produced grain, and colonial rubber, and with the cotton famine during the American Civil War. These are her examples, reported as hers, not independent findings of this profile.

Her analysis of imperialism follows from that dependence. Capital "ransacks the whole world", she wrote, and must acquire an unlimited choice of means of production in order to find productive employment for realised surplus value. Chapter 32 argues that militarism both prepares the ground for capital in non-capitalist societies and creates a new market for armaments, funded largely by indirect taxes on workers and peasants. She described capitalism as the first mode of economy that tries to engulf the entire globe and stamp out other economies, yet is the first that cannot exist on its own: "although it strives to become universal, and, indeed, on account of this its tendency, it must break down because it is immanently incapable of becoming a universal form of production". In the *Anti-Critique* she restated the argument against Otto Bauer: accumulation is impossible in an exclusively capitalist environment, and imperialism is both a way of prolonging capitalism and the surest way of bringing it to an objective limit.

The Junius Pamphlet applies the same framework to war. Written in prison in 1915 and published illegally in Zurich in 1916, it opens by describing the conversion of a European war into a world war and the exposure of bourgeois society as "the ravening beast, the witches' sabbath of anarchy, a plague to culture and humanity". She quoted Engels's claim that bourgeois society stood at the crossroads of transition to socialism or regression into barbarism, and judged that the war was that regression: "The triumph of imperialism leads to the annihilation of civilization." Her conclusion was not that collapse was automatic but that the working class had to learn from its own errors; she called self-criticism "the life's breath and light of the proletarian movement".

## Analysis of socialism

Luxemburg wrote no detailed blueprint of a completed socialist society. In *The Russian Revolution* she said explicitly that the practical realisation of socialism as an economic, social and juridical system lay "completely hidden in the mists of the future"; a party programme offered "a few main signposts" and was mainly negative in character. What she provided instead was a set of institutional principles and a short list of immediate demands.

The first principle was mass self-activity. In *What Does the Spartacus League Want?*, published in *Die Rote Fahne* on 14 December 1918, she wrote that the essence of socialist society was that "the great laboring mass ceases to be a dominated mass, but rather, makes the entire political and economic life its own life and gives that life a conscious, free, and autonomous direction". The transformation could not be decreed by a bureau, committee or parliament. Parliamentary and municipal assemblies were to be replaced by workers' and soldiers' councils, with enterprise councils supervising production and a central council subject to recall by local councils. The programme included disarming the police and officer corps, arming the adult male proletarian population, a six-hour maximum working day, expropriation of large estates, banks, mines and large enterprises, repudiation of war loans, and a revolutionary tribunal. It also stated that the Spartacus League would take power only on the clear will of the majority of the German proletariat.

The second principle was that socialist democracy begins with the seizure of power, not after a period of preparation. In chapter 8 of *The Russian Revolution* she wrote that socialist democracy "begins simultaneously with the beginnings of the destruction of class rule and of the construction of socialism", and was "the same thing as the dictatorship of the proletariat". That dictatorship had to be the work of the class, not "of a little leading minority in the name of the class". The alternative, she argued in chapter 6, was that public life would fall asleep, bureaucracy would become the only active element, and the result would be "not the dictatorship of the proletariat but only the dictatorship of a handful of politicians".

The third principle was that the content of socialism was the satisfaction of human needs rather than accumulation. The last paragraph of *The Accumulation of Capital* states that "the aim of socialism is not accumulation but the satisfaction of toiling humanity's wants by developing the productive forces of the entire globe". Her December 1918 article *The Socialisation of Society* gives a rare concrete sketch: social ownership of land, mines and large enterprises; a general duty to work; abolition of war and luxury industries; shorter hours, healthy workplaces and social provision for children, the sick and the elderly; and technical managers whose orders workers would follow through self-discipline rather than the "whip of hunger". Elsewhere she insisted that the proletarian revolution "hates and despises killing" and did not need terror because it fought institutions rather than individuals, a claim that critics at the KPD founding congress immediately disputed.

On the National Assembly her position changed with circumstances, and the change is documented. In November 1918 she called the assembly an "outmoded legacy of bourgeois revolutions" and argued that the real question was "bourgeois democracy or socialist democracy?" A month later, after the Congress of Councils had voted for elections, she argued for participating in the election in order to fight the assembly from inside and mobilise the masses against it: "we will utilize the elections to the National Assembly to fight against the National Assembly". The SEP says she argued for participation at the KPD founding congress and was outvoted. These texts should not be flattened into a single anti-parliamentary position.

## Recommended reading

These five works are starting points into Luxemburg's arguments, not a measure of her whole output; the complete list stands above.

- *Social Reform or Revolution* (1898–1900) — This is the best place to begin with her first major intervention, which separates reforms as a means from revolution as the aim and defends Marx's value theory against Bernstein.
- *Organizational Questions of the Russian Social Democracy* (1904) — It sets out her case against Lenin's centralism, arguing that a small nucleus cut off from the workers and bound to obey a central committee cannot carry a mass movement, which makes it the clearest entry to her view of party democracy.
- *The Mass Strike, the Political Party and the Trade Unions* (1906) — This is the starting point for her account of the mass strike as a form in which workers educate themselves for revolution rather than an instrument a party can switch on and off, joining economic and political struggle.
- *The Accumulation of Capital: A Contribution to an Economic Explanation of Imperialism* (1913) — Here is the central statement of her economic argument that capital needs non-capitalist markets, raw materials and labour, so that imperialism and militarism become part of accumulation itself.
- *The Russian Revolution* (1918 / 1922) — It is the best place to see her claim that socialist democracy must begin with the destruction of class rule and be the work of the whole class, not of a handful of politicians.

## Intellectual influences

The largest influence was Marx, and the nature of that influence is a matter of her own statements. In *Reform or Revolution* she wrote that anyone who wished to pass as a socialist while declaring war on Marxian doctrine had to begin "by acknowledging himself to be his disciple, by seeking in Marx's own teachings the points of support for an attack on the latter", a description she applied to Bernstein but which also describes her own method. Her work on accumulation is an extended argument with volume 2 of *Capital*, quoting Marx at length in chapters 25 and 26. Engels supplied the "socialism or barbarism" formula that she quoted in the Junius Pamphlet, and she invoked his preface to *The Class Struggles in France* in the debate over legal struggle.

Other influences are visible as objects of argument rather than sources of approval. She engaged Tugan-Baranovsky's reading of Marx's reproduction schemas, Otto Bauer's population-based theory of accumulation, and the Russian "legal Marxists" Struve, Bulgakov and Tugan-Baranovsky in a long historical survey that fills the second section of *The Accumulation of Capital*. Bernstein was the target of her first major book; Lenin and Trotsky were the targets of her organisational and Russian Revolution critiques; Kautsky was a recurring opponent on the mass strike and on revolution.

Personal and political influences are documented in the secondary literature but not in primary texts opened here. The SEP records her partnership with Leo Jogiches and the co-founding of the SDKP, and the Stiftung biography names Jogiches, Kostja Zetkin and Paul Levi as partners. The Stiftung FAQ says she studied botany and zoology, kept a herbarium from 1913 and wrote geological and botanical notes in prison. That is evidence of intellectual interests, but not evidence that those interests shaped her economic or political arguments, so no such claim is made here.

## Influence and legacy

Her influence runs through several distinct channels. Inside Marxism, *The Accumulation of Capital* became a permanent point of reference for debates on underconsumption, crisis and imperialism. The SEP records that Bukharin and Lenin attacked her reading of Marx, that Joan Robinson and Paul Sweezy later treated her as a precursor of effective-demand economics, and that her work is still used in debates on globalisation, finance and migration. Paul Mattick wrote in 1978 that her theory was generally dismissed as an aberration but that with the First World War and the revolutionary upheavals it found a wider response, not so much because of the accumulation analysis as because of her insistence that capitalism had objective limits. Georg Lukács placed *The Accumulation of Capital* alongside Lenin's *State and Revolution* as the two studies that renewed Marxist method after the "vulgarisation" of the Second International. These are judgements by later writers, not settled facts.

Her political legacy is equally contested but more widely institutionalised. The SEP notes that her work has been appropriated both by Western Marxists seeking an alternative to state socialism and by socialist states attracted to her crisis theory and her critique of social democracy, and that her reputation revived with the decline of traditional social-democratic parties and the rise of far-right politics. The Rosa-Luxemburg-Stiftung, which maintains an archive and a permanent exhibition, is itself part of that legacy. Her critique of party rule is routinely cited as an early warning about the concentration of power in twentieth-century socialist states; the SEP endorses that reading, while Mattick argues that it missed the historically new character of state capitalism. Both positions are reported here as interpretations. The question of how centralised institutions formed is treated in this book's chapter on [why states centralised](/why-states-centralised).

Feminist scholarship has revised the older picture of her as indifferent to women's emancipation. The SEP cites work by Dunayevskaya, Ettinger and Haug and argues that she was a fierce champion of women's rights who rejected a suffrage-only feminism detached from the critique of capitalism. The Stiftung FAQ is more guarded: it says she "lived equality in practice rather than dealing theoretically with gender relations" and regarded women's suffrage primarily from the standpoint of class struggle. That disagreement is itself a useful record of the state of the question.

## Criticisms and controversies

**Economic theory.** The controversy began while she was alive. Lenin's private notes of March–April 1913, first published in 1933, record his judgment that "She has got into a shocking muddle. She has distorted Marx". Otto Bauer replied to her book with a population-based theory of accumulation, and she answered him in the *Anti-Critique*, where she accused him of never identifying the other "roots" of imperialism he claimed she had missed and of treating capitalism as conceivable without expansion. Nikolai Bukharin's critique is named in the SEP and in the publishing history of the *Anti-Critique*, which appeared in an English edition paired with Bukharin in 1972; the full text of Bukharin's argument was not opened here.

The central scholarly objection is that Luxemburg treated Marx's reproduction schemas as a description of historical reality rather than as an abstraction, and located capitalism's barrier in the sphere of circulation, in effective demand or the ability of buyers to purchase what is produced, rather than in production, in the tendency of the rate of profit to fall. The SEP reports this criticism and cites Sweezy 1967 among others; Sweezy's essay was not read at source for this profile. Tony Cliff, writing sympathetically, accepted that her diagrams were flawed but rejected the general verdict. He argued that she had looked at only one side of the process, that a rising organic composition of capital, the growing weight of machinery and materials relative to labour, could shift the disproportion to department I, the sector producing means of production, and that her fundamental service was to draw attention to the non-capitalist frontier. Paul Mattick judged her central argument erroneous and based on a misunderstanding of money and capital, while crediting her with restoring the claim that capitalism had objective limits. Lukács defended her against Bauer on methodological grounds, arguing that the critics had missed the question of totality. A common accusation, that she was a crude underconsumptionist, is disputed by the SEP and by Mattick, who note that she herself attacked vulgar underconsumption theory; her position is more precisely a realisation-problem thesis, a problem of selling goods rather than of producing them, tied to non-capitalist markets.

**Party and revolution.** In 1904 she attacked Lenin's conception of the party as "a Jacobin indissolubly joined to the organization of the proletariat", arguing that he confused discipline imposed by the factory and the state with the self-discipline of a class struggling for emancipation, and that an all-powerful central committee would be "the sterile spirit of the overseer". In part two she wrote that "Historically, the errors committed by a truly revolutionary movement are infinitely more fruitful than the infallibility of the cleverest Central Committee". Lenin's counter-arguments are represented in the MIA catalogue of critiques; they are primary texts by an opponent and should be read as such.

Her 1918 critique of the Bolsheviks is her most quoted political text and the one most often detached from its context. She praised the Bolsheviks for acting when others hesitated: "In this sense theirs is the immortal historical service of having marched at the head of the international proletariat". But she argued that the dissolution of the Constituent Assembly was the wrong answer to an out-of-date assembly; the proper response was new elections, not the abolition of popular representation as such. MIA's editors note in a footnote that she was "not quite correct" about the timing of the elections: they were arranged before October but held after it. In chapter 6 she wrote that "Freedom only for the supporters of the government, only for the members of one party – however numerous they may be – is no freedom at all. Freedom is always and exclusively freedom for the one who thinks differently". Her own qualification is often omitted: she attributed the distortions of Bolshevik rule to the world war, German occupation and the failure of the German proletariat, and wrote that "the danger begins only when they make a virtue of necessity and want to freeze into a complete theoretical system all the tactics forced upon them by these fatal circumstances". The Stiftung FAQ places the freedom sentence in the margin rather than the running text and translates it differently, as noted in the Biography section.

**Scholarly disputes about her politics.** The SEP summarises several. Geoff Eley argued that her confidence in the democratising effect of an expanding wave of strikes was itself naïve. Norman Geras argued against readings that make her a fatalist, since she explicitly said socialism would not descend from manna from heaven. Eric Blanc criticised her politics in Poland as rigid and sectarian. The underlying journal articles were not opened, so these are reports of the SEP's accounts, not independent assessments. Mattick criticised her for failing to recognise that the soviets were more than strike committees and for applying social-democratic expectations to a revolution whose social content was not socialist. Lenin's 1916 review of the Junius Pamphlet welcomed it as "a splendid Marxian work" but identified two errors: silence on opportunism and Kautskyism as trends, and the claim that there could be no more national wars in the era of imperialism, which he said ignored national liberation movements in the colonies. This is a primary dispute between Marxists, not a settled verdict.

**Internal inconsistency on violence.** The Spartacus programme says the proletarian revolution "hates and despises killing" and needs no terror, while the same document calls for an armed workers' militia, a revolutionary tribunal and the breaking of bourgeois resistance "with an iron fist and ruthless energy". MIA's footnote records that this passage was attacked at the KPD founding congress as a veiled criticism of Bolshevism. The tension is in the text and is reported here without being smoothed over.

## Related thinkers

**Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels.** They supplied the source of her method and the vocabulary of surplus value, accumulation and the dictatorship of the proletariat. Her disagreement with Marx concerned the completeness of the reproduction schemas, not the validity of the critique of political economy. The historical context is covered in this book's chapter on [Marx and Engels](/marx-and-engels).

**Eduard Bernstein.** The opponent in *Reform or Revolution*. Her argument was not that reforms were useless but that making reforms the aim replaced socialism with the reform of capitalism.

**Vladimir Lenin.** Three distinct disputes: party organisation in 1904, national self-determination in 1914, and the meaning of proletarian dictatorship after 1917. The MIA "On Luxemburg" directory gathers Lenin's texts, including his 1919 speech after her murder and a 1920 speech at a memorial stone-laying; the directory was opened to locate texts and is not itself used as evidence here. The conflict did not erase a shared revolutionary project, and the later history of the party is covered in this book's chapter on [Lenin and Stalin](/lenin-and-stalin).

**Leon Trotsky.** An ally in the 1904 organisational debate and an opponent in the 1918 text, where Luxemburg criticised his argument that elected assemblies always lag behind the masses. Trotsky later wrote on her in 1919, 1932 and 1935.

**Karl Kautsky.** A recurring opponent on the mass strike, on the "unripeness" of Russia, and on parliamentarism. The 1910 essay *Theory & Practice* is explicitly a polemic against his theory of the mass strike.

**Otto Bauer and Nikolai Bukharin.** The two named economic critics answered in and around the *Anti-Critique*. Bauer argued that accumulation adapted to population growth and that capitalism was possible without expansion; she rejected both claims. Bukharin's full critique was not opened for this profile.

**Later Marxists.** Lukács, Cliff, Mattick, Dunayevskaya, Sweezy, Joan Robinson, Geras, Eley and Blanc all wrote on her, and their claims are described above with the sources that report them. She is not interchangeable with Leninism, council communism or social democracy, and later movements that claim her often select one side of her argument.
