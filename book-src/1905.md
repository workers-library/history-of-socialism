On 9 January 1905, by the Russian calendar, a crowd walked through St Petersburg carrying icons and a petition to the Winter Palace. The same day was 22 January in the countries that used the Western calendar; Russia used the Julian calendar, thirteen days behind. The petition had been drafted by a priest, Georgy Gapon, with workers in his association, and it asked for an eight-hour day, a minimum wage of one rouble for unskilled workers and women, and an end to overtime. It also asked for an amnesty, civil freedoms and a Constituent Assembly, an elected national legislature. The language was deliberately suppliant, addressing the tsar as a father, but it ended with a warning: "For us there are but two roads, one leading to liberty and happiness, the other to the tomb." ([St Petersburg workingmen's petition, 9/22 January 1905](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/events/1905/workers-petition.htm)). The text we possess is the one printed in Gapon's 1906 memoir, not the signed sheet, and the petition was never delivered to the tsar. A second translation, credited to Daniel Field, gives a sharper political formula that is absent from the first: "and to that end order that elections to the Constituent Assembly be conducted under universal, secret and equal suffrage" ([petition, Field translation](http://www.historymuse.net/readings/PetitionBloodySunday.htm)). The difference between the two versions is itself evidence that the document circulated in more than one form.

## Nine January

Gapon led the march and later described reaching the Tarakanovskii Canal. "We were not more than thirty yards from the soldiers, being separated from them only by the bridge over the Tarakanovskii Canal, which here marks the border of the city, when suddenly, without any warning and without a moment's delay, was heard the dry crack of many rifle-shots." ([Gapon, eyewitness account written later in 1905](https://alphahistory.com/russianrevolution/eyewitness-account-of-bloody-sunday-1905)). He remembered a boy carrying a church lantern: "A little boy of ten years, who was carrying a church lantern, fell pierced by a bullet, but still held the lantern tightly and tried to rise again, when another shot struck him down." ([Gapon, same account](https://alphahistory.com/russianrevolution/eyewitness-account-of-bloody-sunday-1905)). Gapon had reason to present himself as a leader swept along by events rather than a man who had walked a crowd into troop lines, and his statement that no warning was audible bears directly on the dispute over whether the marchers were warned.

Alexandra Kollontai, then a young Social Democrat, was in the street with the demonstrators. "Bloody Sunday, 1905, found me in the street. I was going with the demonstrators to the Winter Palace, and the picture of the massacre of unarmed, working folk is for ever imprinted on my memory." ([Kollontai, autobiography written 1924](https://spartacus-educational.com/LRUSkollontai.htm)). She recalled trusting, expectant faces and the dead and wounded, and said the killings destroyed belief that the tsar could deliver justice. Her passage was written nineteen years later for a Soviet encyclopedia project, and its cadences may reflect later revolutionary convention. Robert S. McCormick, the United States ambassador, telegraphed Washington on 23 January that "No evidence of evil intent on the part of the workingmen was manifested nor was there any evidence of hostile intent by the crowds beyond jeering at the officers and soldiers." ([McCormick telegram, 23 January 1905](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1905/d784)). An eyewitness told him an order to fire had been given in the Alexander Park, where he put the killed and wounded at about sixty. His telegram also repeated the false claim that the petition had been written by a German socialist, an error relayed from a large employer. Such a mistake shows how badly elites understood what they were facing.

## Counting the dead

The shootings did not begin a strike; they met one already under way. An unnamed worker wrote on 8 January that "The workers swear that they will come out into the square on Sunday 'with their wives and children'." ([Letter quoted in Lenin, *Revolutionary Days*, chapter 7, published 18/31 January 1905](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1905/rd/7.htm)). Lenin, in exile in Geneva and not an eyewitness, printed that letter alongside reports of closed newspapers, mass meetings and police withdrawal from the streets. He set the government's figure of 96 killed and 330 wounded against a press list: "According to the latest press reports, journalists handed the Minister of the Interior a list of 4,600 killed or wounded, as compiled by reporters." ([Lenin, *Revolutionary Days*, chapter 8, 18/31 January 1905](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1905/rd/8.htm)). He conceded that no exact count was possible. A 1918 editorial introduction to Trotsky's January article speaks of more than 5,000 killed or wounded, a figure belonging to the introduction rather than to Trotsky's 1905 text. These are estimates made under different conditions and for different purposes, not measurements, and they should not be combined into one number.

Trotsky, writing eleven days after the shootings, argued that the form of the event concealed its substance. A priest presenting a loyal petition made the rising look like a plea for reform, when he saw it as independent action by the working class: "The hero, Gapon, has not created the revolutionary energy of the Petersburg workingmen, he only unloosed it." ([Trotsky, "The Events in Petersburg", 20 January 1905](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1918/ourrevo/ch03.htm)). That was a Social Democratic claim about who led the movement, made within days, before later legend-making. Walter Sablinsky's later study takes Gapon and his organisation more seriously than such a dismissal allows.

## From strike to manifesto

In June the crew of the battleship *Potemkin* mutinied. Their manifesto, reproduced by Lenin from a German newspaper report, declared: "We refuse to go on acting as the people's hangman. Our slogan is: freedom for the whole Russian people or death!" ([*Potemkin* crew manifesto, June 1905, quoted by Lenin](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1905/jul/10d.htm)). Sailors used the same political language as the January petition, though the chain from leaflet to printed report was long. In October a dispute among Moscow typesetters over rates for punctuation spread to bakers and then to the railways. Railwaymen demanded an eight-hour day, civil liberties, an amnesty and a Constituent Assembly. Telegraph, post and commerce were paralysed. Trotsky, who wrote an account of the strike while it was happening, described the government's panic and the issue of the October Manifesto on 17 October, drafted in government circles around Sergei Witte. It conceded "no law shall take effect without confirmation by the State Duma" ([October Manifesto, 17/30 October 1905](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/events/1905/october-manifesto.htm)), extended Duma participation to classes previously excluded and spoke of the essential foundations of civil freedom. The same document framed the concessions as an answer to "rioting and disturbances" and threatened measures against disorder. A promise in a manifesto is not evidence that the promised freedoms took effect.

## The Soviet

While the strike was running, workers elected deputies to a soviet, a council of workers' representatives. Trotsky dated its first meeting to the evening of 13 October: "The first meeting of what was to become the Soviet was held on the evening of the thirteenth, in the Technological Institute. Not more than thirty to forty delegates attended." ([Trotsky, *1905*, chapter 8, written 1907–1909](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1907/1905/ch08.htm)). He arrived the next day and described that first gathering from later information, and his claim that the Soviet became the axis of all events is a participant's judgement. The body grew from a strike committee into something like a public authority. One deputy reported ordering a factory to close "in the name of the Soviet"; its office received petitions from domestic servants, soldiers, peasants and a blind veteran, and 302 deputies attended a meeting on 27 November. In the same month it resolved on a financial manifesto, issued on 2 December in eight Petersburg newspapers and signed with the Peasants' Union and three party committees. It called for refusal of land-redemption payments, withdrawal of savings-bank deposits in gold and repudiation of loans raised by a government at war with its own people: "The autocracy has never enjoyed the people's confidence and has never received any authority from the people." ([Financial manifesto of the Soviet and allied parties, 27 November / 2 December 1905](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/government/1905/11/27.htm)). The English text in common use is known to be deficient: George Garvy showed that it omits a crucial paragraph. A programme is not a record of what workers did.

Troops arrested the Soviet on 3 December, after it had resolved to continue preparing for armed insurrection following its chairman's arrest. Trotsky told the court in 1907 that a political general strike was "in its substance, an insurrection", because it paralysed the railways and telegraph on which the state depended: "a political strike, despite the president's doubts, is indeed, in its substance, an insurrection." ([Trotsky's court defence, 4/17 October 1907](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1907/1905/ch31.htm)). He also told the court that the Soviet had never debated armed rising as business, which sits awkwardly with the November resolution and reads as a defence against a capital charge. In his 1930 memoir he recalled fifty-two days of Soviet power and judged its place in retrospect: "In Russian life, the revolution of 1905 was the dress rehearsal for the revolution of 1917." ([Trotsky, *My Life*, chapter 14, 1930](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1930/mylife/ch14.htm)). The same memoir claims that he shaped nearly every decision and that Lenin took no active part. Those claims are self-defence written after political defeat and need corroboration from other participants.

## How historians have read it

- **Abraham Ascher, *The Revolution of 1905* (1988–1992) and *The Revolution of 1905: A Short History* (Stanford University Press, 2004).** Argues that the revolution continued past the Moscow uprising of December 1905 into 1907, with the struggle moving into the Duma; broadens the frame beyond the urban strike narrative. [Work record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL4978119W)
- **Walter Sablinsky, *The Road to Bloody Sunday* (1976).** Reappraises the January events from the available documents, with particular attention to Gapon, rather than treating the priest and his organisation as incidental. [Catalogue record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL162991W)
- **S. A. Smith, *Russia in Revolution* (2017).** Sets 1905 inside a longer crisis of empire rather than a single dramatic year; useful on the dispute over when the revolution ended. [Catalogue record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL20184195W)
- **George Garvy, "The Financial Manifesto of the St Petersburg Soviet, 1905", *International Review of Social History* 20:1 (1975), pp. 16–32.** Prints a fuller translation, identifies Parvus as drafter and shows that the English text in Trotsky's *1905* omits a paragraph. [Article record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/financial-manifesto-of-the-st-petersburg-soviet-1905/FC74EA35FEB777848DE16F1EC66E31B3)
- **Oskar Anweiler, *The Soviets: The Russian Workers, Peasants, and Soldiers Councils, 1905–1921* (1974).** Compares the council form across 1905 and 1917; his treatment of the financial manifesto is brief, a reminder that institutional and documentary historians ask different questions of the same episode. [Catalogue record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL15845250W)
- **Howard D. Mehlinger and John M. Thompson, *Count Witte and the Tsarist Government in the 1905 Revolution* (1972).** Studies the government side of the concession and helps balance accounts written by revolutionaries. [Catalogue record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3011486W)

There is broad agreement on the sequence: the January shootings opened the revolution, the October general strike forced the October Manifesto, and the soviets were new organs of workers' representation. Disputes remain over when the revolution ended, with Ascher against a December 1905 cut-off; over how much Gapon himself mattered; over whether to read the Soviet as Trotsky's embryo of government or through Anweiler's institutional comparison; and over the manifesto's text and authorship, where Garvy corrects the standard English version. Older Soviet-directed accounts and later émigré memoirs disagree over the roles of Lenin and Trotsky, and the participant testimony cannot settle that on its own.

## Limits of the record

The surviving testimony is weighted towards priests, party leaders, diplomats and later memoirists. No worker's diary of the march survives in it, no testimony from the soldiers who fired, no minutes of the Soviet's meetings. Peasants, the Baltic and Polish movements and the Jewish communities facing pogroms appear mainly through the Soviet's statements and Trotsky's chapters rather than through their own words. The account of the 3 December arrest and of the 27 November meeting is reconstructed years later, and spoken exchanges in such passages are not transcripts. Casualty estimates must be attributed and never merged, and dates need constant care because Russian sources use the Julian calendar while some pages mix the two. The Russian petition text exists on a Moscow State University page but could not be read with the encoding available; Gapon's *The Story of My Life* is linked from the Marxists Internet Archive as a large scan that has not been retrieved, so both the petition and the shooting account rest on website transcriptions. Sablinsky's study and the Library of Congress picture catalogue were not accessible, and several eyewitness texts are hosted by teaching sites whose standards are not those of a scholarly edition. All Russian-language material here reaches the reader through translation or transcription. Printed editions such as the 1925 *1905 God v Peterburge* volumes, the Soviet trial record and the *Izvestia* run would be needed to establish what most ordinary participants thought, and archival work to go further.
