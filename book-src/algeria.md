On the night of 1 November 1954 the National Liberation Front, or FLN, made a peace offer at the moment it declared war. Its Secretariat wrote that "our action is directly solely against colonialism, our only blind and obstinate enemy, which has always refused to grant the least freedom by peaceful means" ([proclamation, 1 November 1954](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1954/proclamation.htm), trans. Mitch Abidor). It asked for talks on Algerian sovereignty, the release of political prisoners, an end to emergency measures and recognition of Algerian nationality, and promised to respect French interests and to let Europeans in Algeria choose their nationality. Armed attacks began the same night. Charles-Robert Ageron, who dates the text 31 October, found that most of these terms reappeared in the settlement of March 1962. The distance between the offer and the violence that delivered it runs through the war.

## Who the movement said it was

The FLN's newspaper *El Moudjahid* said in 1956 that "It is about time that an organ of resistance fighters comes to be, to fill a certain void" ([*El Moudjahid*, first issue, 1956](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1956/elmoudjahid-01.htm), trans. Mitch Abidor). It argued that *jihad*, often read as religious war, meant self-defence, and counted the soldier of the National Liberation Army, or ALN, the activist, the liaison agent, the shepherd, the housewife in the Casbah and the striking schoolchild as fighters. The Casbah was the old walled quarter of Algiers.

Some who joined were Europeans born in Algeria. Henri Maillot, a former army cadet who defected and stole arms for the FLN, wrote to the French press in April 1956: "I'm not Muslim, but I am Algerian of European origin. I consider Algeria to be my homeland." ([Maillot, April 1956](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1956/maillot.htm), trans. Mitchell Abidor from the *El Watan* republication of 18 June 2007). Fernand Iveton, a European-born communist and FLN member, wrote days before his execution in February 1957 that "morale is good" and that he had found "a fair amount of understanding and even, from some of them, sympathy" among his guards ([Iveton, 8 February 1957](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1957/iveton-affair.htm), trans. Mitch Abidor). The Algerian Communist Party, allied to the FLN, told settlers in 1957: "The Algerians aren't making war on the French people or the Europeans of Algeria, but on FRENCH COLONIALISTS." ([PCA leaflet, 7 October 1957](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1957/letter-europeans.htm), trans. Mitch Abidor). These were different messages to different audiences in one war.

## The Casbah and the cell

Saadi Yacef commanded the FLN in the Battle of Algiers and recalled the Casbah from inside it. He described roofs linked by hidden passages, a mason's skill turned to escape routes, and FLN "justice" against informers. Entering one family's room, he found the wife and children hidden behind a curtain: "I had never had the occasion to see such terrible poverty laid out before me." ([Yacef, *Souvenirs de la Bataille d'Alger*, 1962](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1956/ali.htm), trans. Mitchell Abidor). The movement bought the family a house, which he said "considerably reinforced the authority of the Front among the little people of the Casbah". The memoir was written a few years later and gives no voice to those the Front sentenced.

Henri Alleg, editor of *Alger Républicain*, was arrested by paratroopers in Algiers in June 1957 and held a month at El-Biar. *The Question*, written four months later, gives a first-person account of interrogation and torture. "But it was necessary for me to tell what I know." That is how he explains his purpose ([Alleg, *The Question*, November 1957](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/alleg/alleg-question.pdf)). The book closes with the words "But they must know what is done IN THEIR NAME." (same edition). The publisher said it was seized after two weeks; Sartre wrote that "torture is a systematised form of hatred that creates its own instruments" ([Sartre, preface to *The Wretched of the Earth*, 1961](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/sartre/1961/preface.htm)).

Omar Hamadi, a captured ALN fighter, wrote to his lawyer in December 1960: "We arrived June 20, 1959 and as is usual at Berrouaghia, as soon as we arrived the beatings began, and without reason." ([Hamadi, 5 December 1960](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1960/hamadi-letter.htm), trans. Mitch Abidor). He named guards and described a rubber pipe filled with steel. This is a complaint written so that abuses might be investigated, not a rounded narrative, and no court record tests it.

## The war in France

A declaration drafted by Maurice Blanchot and signed by 121 French intellectuals, among them Simone de Beauvoir, Sartre, Duras and Resnais, stated in September 1960: "We respect and judge justified the refusal to take up arms against the Algerian people." ([Manifesto of the 121](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/algerian-war/1960/manifesto-121.htm), trans. Mitch Abidor). It called the war "an autonomous action on the part of the army and a caste" and said torture had been restored as an institution in Europe fifteen years after Hitler's fall. Ben Bella later said it "resounded like a thunderclap" and that the signatories, lawyers and "valise carriers" "became more than our friends, they became part of our flesh." ([Ben Bella, On the Manifesto of the 121](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1960/ben-bella-121.htm)). Most signatories were not witnesses. A list of names proves who would be named, not how many agreed.

In May 1958 Francis Jeanson, speaking for the FLN, pressed the French Communist Party to join the anti-fascist struggle to independence for Algeria. The minutes record: "It is only a republican government, relying on the working class, that will make peace in Algeria." ([minutes of the FLN–PCF meeting, 30 May 1958](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1958/minutes-fln-pcf.htm), trans. Mitch Abidor, reproduced in Hamon and Rotman, 1979). The party accepted united action in principle but criticised the FLN's confusion about de Gaulle; material help was ruled out for the present. This is a conversation between leaders.

On 10 October 1961 the FLN's French Federation ordered a three-phase response to a curfew and summary executions: "The measures mentioned above must be energetically fought by an action in three phases" ([directive, 10 October 1961](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1961/oct-10-1961.htm), trans. Mitch Abidor). Algerians were to march with their families on the Champs-Élysées and merchants to close for a day. The directive is evidence of preparation, not of how many came or how they died. A later account gives a government figure of 40 dead and almost 400 from Jean-Luc Einaudi's cemetery records ([account of 17 October 1961](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1961/oct-17-1961.htm)); it is a historian's summary, and the figures remain disputed.

## What independence did not settle

The FLN's French Federation addressed Algerians serving with the French army as "HARKIS! 'BLUE-CAPS'! MEMBERS OF THE 'AUXILIARY POLICE FORCE'". "You weren't born to be the bulldogs of French colonialism." ([leaflet to harkis, 14 July 1960](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1960/harkis.htm), trans. Mitch Abidor). It acknowledged that many had been forced to enlist and predicted that France "WILL ABANDON YOU": "Your place awaits you among your brothers." No harki speaks here; the leaflet records what the FLN promised and threatened.

Frantz Fanon, a psychiatrist and FLN member, warned in *The Wretched of the Earth* that "The national middle class which takes over power at the end of the colonial regime is an under-developed middle class." ([chapter 3, 1961](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/fanon/pitfalls-national.htm), English edition of 1965, translator not named on the page). Without economic power such a class becomes an intermediary for foreign capital and may turn to the single party. "The national bourgeoisie steps into the shoes of the former European settlement: doctors, barristers, traders, commercial travellers, general agents and transport agents." Nationalisation, he wrote, may mean "the transfer into native hands of those unfair advantages which are a legacy of the colonial period". These are arguments by a participant, not measurements.

Djamila Bouhired, a liaison officer in the Battle of Algiers sentenced to death in 1957, wrote to Algeria's president in December 2009: "My retirement benefits and the small war pension I receive do not allow me to live properly" ([Bouhired, 9 December 2009](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/2009/djamila.htm), trans. Mitchell Abidor). She said other veterans were in the same position and that she could not afford three operations. The letters say nothing about 1957; they show how one veteran judged independent Algeria.

## Limits of the record

Leaders and militants speak at length; peasants, conscripts, harkis and pied-noir civilians do not. The harkis appear only in the FLN's threatening leaflet, and no French soldier or settler gives an account of his own motives. That absence is a feature of what survived, not proof that such people had nothing to say. Maillot's letter reached print in *El Watan* in 2007 and the FLN–PCF minutes in a book of 1979. Most documents reached English through Mitch or Mitchell Abidor; where no translator is named, that is stated, and the Alleg text comes from an imperfect scan. The archive preserving much of this material is politically committed; its notes and chronology are commentary, and its own chronology warns that one source is politically conservative and sometimes misrepresents facts. The Évian Accords text is not reproduced here, and no 1962 censorship statute could be identified; the documented seizures concern *The Question* in 1958 and the manifesto in 1960. The death figures for 17 October come from a historian's summary and a contested estimate, none with an explained method. The proclamation is dated 1 November in the document and 31 October by Ageron. French-language archives and oral-history collections on harkis remain to be consulted.

## How historians have read it

- **Charles-Robert Ageron, "Les accords d'Évian (1962)," *Vingtième Siècle. Revue d'histoire*, no. 35 (1992), pp. 3–15.** Accounts for the secret and public negotiations and argues that the accords' two bets had different fates: the hoped-for reconciliation of the communities failed in the second half of 1962, while state-to-state cooperation was "better kept than is generally said". He treats the FLN's 1954 peace terms as the template for 1962. [Source: persee.fr](https://www.persee.fr/doc/xxs_0294-1759_1992_num_35_1_2561); DOI 10.3406/xxs.1992.2561
- **Alistair Horne, *A Savage War of Peace: Algeria 1954–1962* (1977).** The standard English narrative, valued for chronology and its attention to both sides of the Battle of Algiers. It is more descriptive than Ageron on the negotiations and less attentive to Algerian-language sources; useful for sequence rather than the meaning of the accords.
- **Benjamin Stora and Mohamed Harbi, eds., *La Guerre d'Algérie* (Robert Laffont, 2004).** A major French reference work; its volume on the FLN and its documents is the source of the 1962 FLN–ALN appeal to Algerian Jews. [Marxists Internet Archive: israelites](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1962/israelites.htm)
- **Sylvie Thénault, "Les Accords d'Évian n'ont pas été appliqués," in *Algérie : des événements à la guerre* (2012), pp. 155–165.** Argues that the accords' guarantees were not implemented, the clearest direct challenge to Ageron's more favourable judgment on cooperation.
- **Jean-Luc Einaudi, *La bataille de Paris, 17 octobre 1961* (1991).** The study behind the higher estimate of deaths in the Paris massacre, based on cemetery and other records. It is cited in the secondary account above, but its method is not examined in the documents gathered here. [Marxists Internet Archive: oct 17 1961](https://www.marxists.org/history/algeria/1961/oct-17-1961.htm)
- **Phillip Naylor, *France and Algeria: A History of Decolonization and Transformation* (University of Texas Press, 2024), DOI 10.7560/328439.** A recent synthesis, useful as a check on how the field has shifted since Ageron and Horne.

Broad agreement: the war ended in a negotiated settlement in March 1962, torture was used systematically by French forces, and the FLN was the only recognised negotiating partner. Live disagreement: how far the Évian Accords were honoured, how many died in Paris on 17 October 1961, and how to weigh Fanon's claim that political independence without social transformation produces a new elite. Ageron and Thénault disagree directly on the last two years of French–Algerian relations; Einaudi's figures are contested by official ones.
