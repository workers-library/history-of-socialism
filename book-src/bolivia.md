In November 1946, six years before the insurrection, the country's mine workers met at Pulacayo and adopted a programme. The Federación Sindical de Trabajadores Mineros de Bolivia, or FSTMB, the federation of mine unions, was organising around a demand beyond wages:

> "If we wish to avoid a repeat of the massacre of Catavi we must arm the workers."
>
> Source: [FSTMB, *Thesis of Pulacayo*, 8 November 1946, English text published 2019, translator not named](https://workersvoiceus.org/2019/11/11/bolivia-the-historic-thesis-of-pulacayo/)

The Catavi massacre it invoked was an attack on striking miners in 1942. The thesis called for a living wage, a forty-hour week, occupation of mines threatened with closure, and workers' control, meaning management by committees elected in the mines. It rejected the ballot and cooperation with employers. If companies closed their mines:

> "If the capitalists respond to all demands with the threat of closing their mines, there is no option open to the workers other than seizing the mines and organizing production ourselves."
>
> Source: [FSTMB, *Thesis of Pulacayo*, 8 November 1946](https://workersvoiceus.org/2019/11/11/bolivia-the-historic-thesis-of-pulacayo/)

This was a programme, not a record of events, and its authorship was disputed even among Bolivian Trotskyists. Its importance is that it shows what the miners wanted before the revolution, so the later argument over nationalisation can be read as a conflict between two positions.

## The world the miners lived in

The camps were company property. Domitila Barrios de Chungara, who grew up at Pulacayo and Siglo XX and later organised in the Catavi Housewives' Committee, recalled that a miner could wait five or ten years for a house, that a room was four by five or six metres with no water or sanitation, and that a widow had ninety days to leave. These are recollections from the 1970s, not contemporary records, but they describe the world the thesis was written for.

## April 1952

In April 1952 the state's ability to coerce its own people collapsed. The Movimiento Nacionalista Revolucionario, or MNR, the nationalist party that had won the 1951 election, rose on 9 April with the national police and much of organised labour. Dean Acheson, the US Secretary of State, wrote to President Truman six weeks later:

> "the uprising of April 9 constituted to a large extent a spontaneous expression of public sentiment"
>
> Source: [Dean Acheson, memorandum to the President, 22 May 1952, FRUS 1952–1954, The American Republics, volume IV, document 136](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d136)

His memorandum sought a decision on recognition and stressed the junta's lack of control: the army in the La Paz area had been badly damaged, civilians kept the rifles they had seized, and Víctor Paz Estenssoro did not return from six years in exile until 15 April. It has no Bolivian popular voice, but on the failure of the army it is clear.

Domitila's family story puts miners in the fighting away from the capital. She was fifteen in 1952, so this is community memory:

> "It participated actively in the revolution of April 9, 1952, disarming the government forces of Uyuni."
>
> Source: [Domitila Barrios de Chungara, on Pulacayo, oral testimony recorded in the mid-1970s, translated by Victoria Ortiz](https://archive.org/details/let-me-speak)

A correspondent for the American paper Labor Action recorded the new leaders' language. Hernán Siles Zuazo, soon vice-president, promised:

> "our government will not only be one of reform, but it will be a government of political, social and economic transformation"
>
> Source: [Hernán Siles Zuazo, first official declaration, reported late April 1952, rendered in English by *Labor Action*, translator uncredited](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/revhist/supplem/bolivia/robles9.htm)

Juan Lechín, head of the mine workers' union and soon Minister of Mines, promised nationalisation. The correspondent, a partisan anti-Stalinist, drew the division: the workers fought for a social revolution, the MNR for a reform of Bolivian capitalism. That was his interpretation, but the next months show the argument was real.

## Who would control the mines

The Central Obrera Boliviana, or COB, the national federation of labour unions, was founded in 1952. José Villa, a participant writing forty years later, described it in a sentence that is his judgement, not a 1952 document:

> "The COB was born brandishing the Theses of Pulacayo, and with a POR programme and orientation."
>
> Source: [José Villa, retrospective account first published 1992](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/revhist/supplem/bolivia/villa1.htm)

The POR was the Partido Obrero Revolucionario, a small Trotskyist party, Trotskyism being a current of Marxism opposed to the Stalinist model. Lechín headed the COB as well as the mines ministry, and labour ministers reported to the union federation, what one correspondent called a "virtual dual government". Villa records Lechín returning after protesting the number of posts offered to his followers and naming four ministers, creating what he calls co-government.

On 31 October 1952 Paz signed a decree nationalising the three large tin companies, Aramayo, Hochschild and Patiño. It recognised compensation, which the miners opposed. The Labor Action correspondent reported Paz telling the COB:

> "I see now that the nationalization of the mines is indispensable, and I promise to do it"
>
> Source: [Víctor Paz Estenssoro, at a COB meeting, reported October 1952, quoted in English by *Labor Action*, translator uncredited](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/revhist/supplem/bolivia/robles13.htm)

Villa records that when the state mining company, COMIBOL, was set up, only two of its seven directors were nominated by the miners' federation, and those two were not subject to recall. The question the Pulacayo thesis posed was settled in a decree and a boardroom, not in the mine committees.

Peasants occupied land after the army's collapse, chiefly in Cochabamba and around Lake Titicaca, according to Villa. Agrarian reform was enacted in 1953 but unevenly implemented ([James Dunkerley, *Rebellion in the Veins*, 1984](https://archive.org/details/rebellioninveins0000dunk)); Domitila judged that it was carried out and that those put in power worked to undo the revolution's aims.

In July 1953 Washington's Cabinet debated a grant for Bolivian agriculture against continued purchases of low-grade tin. An objection was made that ending purchases would create unemployment and foster communism. It agreed:

> "purchase of Bolivian tin should be continued for one year to the amount of 10,000 tons"
>
> Source: [Minutes of the US Cabinet meeting, 3 July 1953, FRUS 1952–1954, volume IV, document 159](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54v04/d159)

That record shows what American ministers decided, not what their decision did in Bolivia. With the compensation in the nationalisation decree, it shows how far the settlement had moved from the miners' demand.

## What was won and what was lost

Four accounts disagree about what the revolution was. Villa, in 1992, argues the COB held effective power and a workers' government was available. Juan Rey, in 1952, agreed about the dual government but treated the COB leadership as a brake. Domitila, in the 1970s, used neither party's language:

> "The revolution of 1952 was a great event in Bolivia's history. It was really a people's victory. But what happened? The people, the working class, the peasants, we weren't ready to take power."
>
> Source: [Domitila Barrios de Chungara, oral testimony recorded in the mid-1970s, translated by Victoria Ortiz](https://archive.org/details/let-me-speak)

She judged the gains undone by those entrusted with government and listed massacres in 1942, 1949, 1965 and 1967. In 2014 a teacher in La Paz told an interviewer:

> "I don't think it was really like a revolution, because it was unfinished."
>
> Source: [Jaime Ortiz, teacher, interviewed in La Paz in 2014](https://bolivianexpress.org/magazine-sub-item/416)

All four accept that something was won and something lost; they differ over who held the agency. The record is strong on compensation, the COB's existence and the land occupations; the counterfactual claims are interpretations. The death toll is disputed. A contemporary report spoke of about 3,000 dead; Dunkerley gives 490, and other scholarly estimates range from 600 to 5,000, with 600 to 2,000 the most responsible. The figure attributed to Lechín comes through a later biography quoted by Villa, not an archival count, and none of these is a casualty study ([Dunkerley, *Rebellion in the Veins*, 1984, p. 39](https://archive.org/details/rebellioninveins0000dunk); [*Elite Induced Change in the Bolivian National Revolution*, 2019](https://scholarworks.umt.edu/etd/3227/); [*The Bolivian National Revolution*, 2013](https://scholarworks.calstate.edu/downloads/fq978208s)).

The campesinos who did not join Che Guevara's guerrilla band in 1967 were often small farmers with land won after 1952. That is the connection: not a shared programme, but a rural population whose interests had changed.

## How historians have read it

- **James Dunkerley, *Rebellion in the Veins: Political Struggle in Bolivia, 1952–82* (1984).** The standard political history of the period and its aftermath; Villa relies on it repeatedly and accuses the English edition of softening a qualification about POR influence in the COB. [Internet Archive: rebellioninveins0000dunk](https://archive.org/details/rebellioninveins0000dunk)
- **Robert J. Alexander, *The Bolivian National Revolution* (Rutgers University Press, 1958).** An early scholarly account written close to events; Villa quotes its judgement that the POR helped determine the COB's orientation and that for the first six months the COB was "practically in the hands of the Trotskyists". [Internet Archive: bwb p8 cwz 339](https://archive.org/details/bwb_p8-cwz-339)
- **Herbert S. Klein, *A Concise History of Bolivia* (2003).** A broad national history that sets 1952 within long-run social and economic structures rather than party struggle alone; useful as a check on the insider accounts. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL1940885W)
- **Guillermo Lora, *Historia del movimiento obrero boliviano, 1933–1952* (1980).** A participant-historian's labour history, cited by Villa and by the editors of *Let Me Speak!*; indispensable for the miners' movement but explicitly written from the POR tradition. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL3041152W)
- **Mario Murillo, *La bala no mata sino el destino* (2012).** An oral history that the *Bolivian Express* says emphasises "the human voices of those who experienced the revolutionary events" over presidential history; the book itself was not opened. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL32794381W)

Broad agreement exists on the outer facts: the army was defeated, the MNR took office, the mines were nationalised with compensation, adult suffrage was extended, and agrarian reform was enacted but unevenly implemented. Live disagreement concerns the distribution of power in 1952 and 1953, whether a workers' government was a realistic possibility, and whether the revolution should be judged by what it achieved or by what its programme promised. The sources above do not equally support every position: the documentary record for compensation, the COB's existence and the land occupations is strong; the counterfactual claims are interpretations.

## Limits of the record

The strongest verified material is in English translation or in English-language reporting. The original Spanish of the Pulacayo thesis, of the COB's *Ideological Position of the Bolivian Working Class*, of the nationalisation decree of 31 October 1952 and of the agrarian reform decree of 2 August 1953 was not opened; a translation and a Bolivian library are needed for these. The finding aid for Juan Lechín's papers at the Hoover Institution points to his autobiography, interview recordings, photographs and union records, but a visit to the archive is required to use them. No photograph or film from 1952 was verified, and no Quechua- or Aymara-language testimony or direct peasant voice was found; the peasants appear through miners' and journalists' descriptions.

The "Tambaka" counter-insurgency records sometimes listed for this episode could not be identified and may be a code name or a misremembering requiring Spanish-language archival work. The "Villa Imperial" item likewise produced no distinct mining-camp testimony; the phrase commonly names the city of Potosí rather than a single camp. Testimony is uneven by gender and class: the one woman's account is detailed but retrospective and editorially shaped, while the men are partisan organisers, journalists or diplomats. Casualty figures cannot be used without a dedicated study. Finally, the American records are official policy documents: they establish what officials thought and decided, not what their policies did in Bolivia.
