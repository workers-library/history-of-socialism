On 21 December 1844 a group of weavers and artisans—traditionally said to number twenty-eight—opened a shop in Toad Lane, Rochdale, in the north of England ([Rochdale Pioneers Museum (archived)](https://web.archive.org/web/20250119163917/https://rochdalepioneersmuseum.coop/about-us/about-toad-lane/)). Its first stock was flour, butter, sugar and oatmeal. They owned it themselves, having gathered twopence at a time until they held enough to take a lease at £10 a year; the traditional figure for their capital is about £28. A consumer cooperative is a shop owned by the people who use it: they pool money to buy in bulk and receive any surplus in proportion to what they spend, rather than paying a return to outside shareholders. The founders did not mean to stop at a grocery. Their rules set out a much longer journey.

George Jacob Holyoake knew the Rochdale socialists and Chartists and later printed the Society's own minutes. He described how the idea had begun among working people:

> "a few poor weavers out of employ, and nearly out of food and quite out of heart with the social state, met together to discover what they could do to better their industrial condition."
>
> Source: [George Jacob Holyoake, *The History of the Rochdale Pioneers*, first published from 1857](https://archive.org/details/cu31924013720366)

The trigger was a failed strike of flannel weavers in 1843. The arguments continued on Sundays in the Temperance or Chartist Reading Room. Chartists, who wanted political rights for ordinary men, debated there with teetotalers and followers of Robert Owen, who held that character was formed by circumstance and communities could be organised cooperatively. John Kershaw, a coal miner and one of the founders, remembered the conditions:

> "Distress was far and widespread. People were badly fed and badly clothed. The hand-loom weavers seemed to be the worst off of any of the working-class."
>
> Source: [John Kershaw, Rochdale Pioneer, in the *Co-operative News*, 9 April 1893, reproduced in John Samuel, 1894](https://archive.org/details/rochdalepioneers134samu)

Kershaw had begun in a mill at seven and gone into the mine at eight. His memory reaches us through Holyoake and a pamphlet written to persuade American workers, a participant's account at two removes. The Society's 1864 annual report, reproduced in that pamphlet, says it was formed after working men despaired of success in a strike.

The rules show how far the founders meant to go. Alongside the store they listed housing, manufacture to employ members laid off by wage cuts, and land to be cultivated by the unemployed. Then came the clause that gave them their wider name:

> "That, as soon as practicable, this society shall proceed to arrange the powers of production, distribution, education, and government, or in other words, to establish a self-supporting home colony of united interests."
>
> Source: [The Rochdale Society of Equitable Pioneers, rules, 1844](https://panarchy.org/variousauthors/cooperative.html)

Most of the larger objects were never carried out. What survived was a discipline about money: limited interest on shares, meaning a cap on the return paid to anyone's capital, and a surplus divided according to purchases. The shop was to be a business that could not be captured for private gain.

## The rules that outlived the colony

The Society survived the Lancashire cotton famine. Holyoake's history records that during the four years of the crisis members drew out £83,000, the Society gave £750 in relief and spent £1,840 on self-education ([Holyoake, *The History of the Rochdale Pioneers*, 1893, pp. 152–153](https://archive.org/details/historyofrochdal00holyuoft)). The Co-operative Wholesale Society, founded in 1863, was owned by the retail societies it supplied; its customers were its member societies ([Redfern, *The Story of the C.W.S.*, 1913](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.225913/2015.225913.The-Story_djvu.txt)). A cooperative was answerable to its own customers.

By 1966 the International Co-operative Alliance had turned Rochdale practice into six principles:

> "Members of primary societies should enjoy equal rights of voting (one member, one vote) and participation in decisions affecting their societies."
>
> Source: [International Co-operative Alliance, Co-operative Principles, 1966](https://panarchy.org/variousauthors/cooperative.html)

The others limited interest on capital, declared the surplus to belong to members, required member education and urged cooperation between cooperatives. One member, one vote mattered most: however many shares a person held, they could not outvote a neighbour. Land, housing and production for employment had dropped out of the doctrine. In 1995 the Alliance, after a consultation chaired by Ian MacPherson, defined the movement again:

> "A co-operative is an autonomous association of persons united voluntarily to meet their common economic, social, and cultural needs and aspirations through a jointly-owned and democratically-controlled enterprise."
>
> Source: [International Co-operative Alliance, Statement on the Co-operative Identity, 1995](https://panarchy.org/variousauthors/cooperative.html)

It added concern for the community as a seventh principle and did not call cooperatives socialist. A century after Rochdale, the movement described itself in values people of many political views could accept. Both statements say what cooperatives should be, not how many behaved that way.

## The women inside the movement, and the commune

In 1915 the Women's Co-operative Guild published letters from 386 members about childbirth. The editors regularised spelling, removed names and places, and the guild was campaigning for a national maternity scheme, so the collection is not a survey. Its introduction reports 1,396 live children, 83 stillbirths and 218 miscarriages among the respondents, and says the writers were better-off than average working women. One wrote:

> "I do feel I cannot express my feelings enough by letter to say what a great help it would have been to me, for no one but a mother knows the struggle and hardships we working women have to go through."
>
> Source: [A member of the Women's Co-operative Guild, letter 1, in *Maternity: Letters from Working-Women*, 1915](https://archive.org/details/maternityletters00womeuoft)

The preface was by Herbert Samuel, a minister whose argument about population belonged to him, not to the women. The guild campaigned for practical help, and its members used the cooperative as a place from which to speak about lives socialist theory often left out.

The kibbutz made a more radical claim. A kibbutz is a voluntary commune, chiefly in Israel, whose members hold no private property and receive no wages. The movement's own guide, published in 1964, called it:

> "The kibbutz of Israel is a unique type of society in its structure and in its mode of life."
>
> Source: [Ichud Habonim, *A Guide to Kibbutz*, 1964 edition](https://archive.org/details/habo025)

The guide describes a weekly general meeting, committees that rotated, children housed separately from parents, and German restitution money handed to the common fund. It admits debt, difficulty finding office-bearers, and unsettled arguments about artists and about children sleeping at home. Its figures, 85,355 members in 225 settlements, are the movement's own. It depicts the Palestinian villagers whose land surrounded the settlements as a threat and as inferior labour.

Hayuta Busel, a founder of Degania, kept a journal that does not match that confidence. She arrived in 1913 expecting a commune that would free women and found the reality far from her dream. She and her husband arranged that he would care for their daughter while she worked, and after his death she helped build the kibbutz childcare system. In the English text published by the Jewish Women's Archive:

> "The primary condition for struggle is total freedom. We did not want to tie our hands so as to be immobile, but rather to untie the fetters with which others had tied us, so as to be more than parents and able to fight."
>
> Source: [Hayuta Busel's journal, English text published by the Jewish Women's Archive; translator not named on the page](https://jwa.org/encyclopedia/article/busel-hayyuta)

What we have is a historian's selection from a private archive, in an unidentified translation. It is enough to show that the claim the kibbutz had freed women was disputed from inside the experiment.

## "Co-operative Commonwealth" and the question of power

The same phrase meant different things to different movements. In July 1933 the Co-operative Commonwealth Federation, a Canadian party whose programme was written chiefly by Frank Underhill, adopted the Regina Manifesto:

> "The CCF is a federation of organizations whose purpose is the establishment in Canada of a Co-operative Commonwealth in which the principle regulating production, distribution and exchange will be the supplying of human needs and not the making of profits."
>
> Source: [Regina Manifesto, July 1933](https://artsonline.uwaterloo.ca/rneedham/sites/ca.rneedham/files/needhdata/Regina_Manifesto.html)

The manifesto rejected violence and sought power at the ballot box. It called for socialising finance, transport, communications and electric power, a national labour code with works councils, meaning bodies where workers and employers discuss conditions, and state help for agricultural and retail cooperatives. Its closing pledge to eradicate capitalism was made by a party out of power, not recorded as an outcome.

In 1923 Lenin used cooperation for a different purpose. He wrote that Marxists had been wrong to dismiss the old cooperative socialists as fantastic until workers held political power. Given that power and public ownership of the main means of production, the factories, land and machinery that make goods, he argued:

> "given social ownership of the means of production, given the class victory of the proletariat over the bourgeoisie, the system of civilized cooperators is the system of socialism."
>
> Source: [V. I. Lenin, "On Cooperation", 4 and 6 January 1923, translated in *Collected Works*, vol. 33 (1965)](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1923/jan/06.htm)

He wanted cheap credit, bonuses and privileges for peasant cooperators, and said a cultural revolution and universal literacy were prerequisites. Proletariat means the working class. This is a translated, dictated text, a programme for a state cooperative system rather than evidence that voluntary cooperation flourished under one. For the CCF the commonwealth was to be elected; for Lenin it was to be built from state power after a revolution.

The Lucas Aerospace shop stewards in Britain offered a third answer in 1976. Shop stewards are workplace union representatives, and a combine links them across a company's sites. Their committee drew up roughly 150 ideas for socially useful products, twelve of them in areas including medical equipment, alternative energy and transport. The plan rejected worker participation and workers' directors as substitutes for real authority, citing Volkswagen as a warning:

> "There cannot be ‘industrial democracy’ until there is a real shift in power to the workers themselves."
>
> Source: [Lucas Aerospace Combine Shop Stewards Committee, *Alternative Corporate Plan*, 1976](https://archive.org/details/the-lucas-plan)

They also warned that management might take the profitable parts of the plan and discard the socially useful ones. Brian Salisbury, a former combine member, looked back after a fortieth anniversary conference in 2016:

> "An opportunity was lost to make a company receiving public money accountable to the community in which it served."
>
> Source: [Brian Salisbury, former Combine member, "Story of the Lucas Plan", after 2016](https://www.lucasplan.org.uk/story-of-the-lucas-plan/)

Management rejected the plan. It records what skilled workers said they would do with power, not a functioning employee-owned firm. Salisbury writes as a supporter remembering a defeat; his dates and numbers need the 1976 document beside them.

## How historians have read it

- **G. D. H. Cole, *A Century of Co-operation* (1944).** The standard movement history by a guild socialist; treats cooperation as a body of theory and practice with its own institutions rather than as a mere precursor to state socialism. Agrees with Holyoake about the importance of self-help, but places Rochdale inside a longer intellectual history. [Internet Archive: centuryofcoopera0000gdhc](https://archive.org/details/centuryofcoopera0000gdhc)
- **Johnston Birchall, *Building Communities the Co-operative Way* (1988).** A study of British cooperative housing; broadens the subject beyond consumer retail to collective ownership of homes. Useful where the Rochdale housing object finally mattered; differs from Cole in focusing on policy and institutions rather than ideas. [Internet Archive: buildingcommunit0000birc](https://archive.org/details/buildingcommunit0000birc)
- **Melford E. Spiro, *Kibbutz: Venture in Utopia* (1956; edition archived 1964).** Anthropological participant observation of a single kibbutz; argues that communal child-rearing and the abolition of the household economy deliberately remade kinship and gender relations. A necessary critical counterweight to the movement's own 1964 guide. [Internet Archive: kibbutzventurein0000melf](https://archive.org/details/kibbutzventurein0000melf)
- **Hilary Wainwright and Dave Elliott, *The Lucas Plan: A New Trade Unionism in the Making?* (1982; reissued with a 40th-year essay by Wainwright).** A detailed political account written by sympathisers who followed the Combine; agrees with the stewards that management, government and official union structures blocked the plan, and is strongest on the internal politics of the campaign. [Source: spokesmanbooks.com](https://www.spokesmanbooks.com/acatalog/The_Lucas_Plan.html)
- **Ian MacPherson (chair), *Statement on the Co-operative Identity* process, 1995.** Not a book but the consultation record behind the 1995 statement; relevant because it shows the movement deliberately defining itself in universal values language rather than socialist doctrine. [Source: panarchy.org](https://panarchy.org/variousauthors/cooperative.html)

Broad scholarly agreement: Rochdale's 1844 rules became the template for consumer cooperation, and limited interest plus one member one vote are its defining institutional marks. Live disagreement: whether the kibbutz emancipated women or merely collectivised their domestic labour; whether workers' control plans such as Lucas's were a socialist alternative or a defensive reform; and whether cooperation can be equated with socialism at all, which separates the cooperative federalists from Lenin and from the Lucas stewards.

## Limits of the record

No physical archive was visited. The rules, Alliance statements and guild letters were read as transcriptions or scanned text, not page images. Rochdale is richly documented, but mostly by men looking back with pride. The kibbutz is represented by a movement guide and one woman's diary excerpt selected by a historian; no full memoir was opened and the Hebrew sources were not translated. The 1970s material is a workers' control plan, not testimony from an employee-owned firm; Mondragon and the later British worker cooperatives were not verified. The Commonwealth survives as one party programme, not the record of a government. The guild letters and Busel's journal were private writing, and neither is a representative survey. The kibbutz guide depicts the Palestinian villagers displaced by Jewish settlement as a threat and as inferior labour. These sources cannot say how members voted, how many read the rules, or whether a surplus was distributed as promised in a given quarter. They record aspirations, not measured outcomes.
