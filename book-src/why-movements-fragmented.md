At the Hague Congress of September 1872 the International Working Men's Association voted to make the formation of a working class into a political party indispensable. "The conquest of political power has therefore become the great duty of the working class," ran the new Article 7a. It passed by 29 votes to 5 with 8 abstentions; Mikhail Bakunin was expelled by 27 votes to 6. Marx's Inaugural Address of 1864 had already said that "to conquer political power has, therefore, become the great duty of the working classes". Bakunin, who argued for the abolition of the state and of coercive authority, an outlook later called anarchism, answered that "the basic unity of the International has been fractured". He claimed a managed majority; the vote counts cannot confirm or refute that claim. Engels, without naming him, argued that a cotton mill, a railway and a ship at sea all require coordination: "Wanting to abolish authority in large-scale industry is tantamount to wanting to abolish industry itself, to destroy the power loom in order to return to the spinning wheel" ([Inaugural Address](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1864/10/27.htm); [Hague resolutions](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/iwma/documents/1872/hague-conference/resolutions.htm); [Bakunin's reply](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1872/karl-marx.htm); [Engels, *On Authority*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1872/10/authority.htm)).

This was a division over means and organisation, not yet over the ownership of property. Historians read it differently. Stekloff's Marxist history treats the split as a real division with its own later life; Nicolaievsky and Maenchen-Helfen stress Marx's conduct and motives; Joll and Avrich set the conflict within the wider history of anarchism. The documentary case against the Alliance was made by one party, drawn partly from rules supplied by dissidents, and was never tested adversarially. The votes are facts; the weight to give them is interpretation ([First International](https://www.marxists.org/archive/steklov/history-first-international/index.htm); [Joll](https://archive.org/details/anarchists0000jame_y7h3)).

## Reform, revolution and the party

The next division concerned whether capitalism could be voted away. Luxemburg wrote in 1899 that "without the collapse of capitalism the expropriation of the capitalist class is impossible. Bernstein therefore renounces expropriation and chooses a progressive realisation of the 'co-operative principle' as the aim of the labour movement." She was answering Eduard Bernstein, who argued that capitalism could be reformed through parliamentary and trade-union gains; her text was written before any workers' state existed, so it says nothing about how a transition government should be organised. The German revolution of 1918 made that question practical. Workers' and soldiers' councils, elected bodies of workers and soldiers, spread through the cities; on 9 November Scheidemann declared a republic and Liebknecht proclaimed a socialist republic later the same day. Luxemburg wrote that the choice lay between a National Assembly and "all power to the Workers' and Soldiers' Councils", yet at the KPD founding congress thirteen days later she urged entering the assembly elections, and the congress rejected her. She was murdered with Liebknecht in January 1919 ([Luxemburg, *Reform or Revolution*](https://www.marxists.org/archive/luxemburg/1900/reform-revolution/ch09.htm); [German revolution](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/weimar-germany-1918-1933/rosa-luxemburg-on-the-national-assembly-debate-december-1918); [Kollwitz's diary](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/weimar-germany-1918-1933/kaethe-kollwitz-on-the-revolution-1918-19)).

Luxemburg's dispute with Lenin was over the agent as well as the form. From prison in 1918 she wrote that "freedom is always and exclusively freedom for the one who thinks differently" and that socialist democracy had to begin at the seizure of power. Lenin answered from the other direction: "small-scale production engenders capitalism and the bourgeoisie continuously, daily, hourly, spontaneously, and on a mass scale," which grounded "iron discipline" and "absolute centralisation". His *What Is To Be Done?* held that workers develop only trade-union consciousness by their own efforts, so socialist consciousness had to be brought from without. Inside the Russian party the Workers' Opposition wanted unions rather than appointed managers to run production; Kollontai wrote that "bureaucracy is a direct negation of mass self-activity". These texts are arguments with audiences. They establish what leaders said, not what rank-and-file members thought, and each was written under civil war, blockade or exile ([Luxemburg, 1918](https://www.marxists.org/archive/luxemburg/1918/russian-revolution/ch06.htm); [Lenin, 1920](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1920/lwc/ch02.htm); [Lenin, 1902](https://www.marxists.org/archive/lenin/works/1901/witbd/ii.htm); [Kollontai](https://www.marxists.org/archive/kollonta/1921/workers-opposition/ch03.htm)).

## The Sino-Soviet split and its critics

The postwar split was argued in the same formal language. Khrushchev's report to the Twentieth Congress in February 1956 attacked the cult of personality; the Chinese leadership defended Stalin's historic role while objecting to the way a fraternal party was being reassessed. Enver Hoxha rejected "the revisionist theses of the XX Congress" in November 1960. The 1960 Statement of eighty-one parties had declared that "the restoration of capitalism has been made socially and economically impossible" in the socialist countries. By June 1963 the Chinese Central Committee wrote that "a number of major differences of principle now exist in the international communist movement"; the Soviet Open Letter of 14 July answered that the controversy centred on war and peace and called the Chinese position on nuclear war "adventuristic". Albania aligned with China, then broke with it after Mao's death ([Khrushchev's report](https://www.marxists.org/archive/khrushchev/1956/02/24.htm); [Hoxha, 1960](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hoxha/works/nov1960.htm); [1960 Statement](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sino-soviet-split/other/1960statement.htm); [CPC Proposal, 1963](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sino-soviet-split/cpc/proposal.htm); [CPSU Open Letter](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sino-soviet-split/cpsu/openletter.htm)).

Here the sources allow a ranking of explanations. Lorenz Lüthi, using archival and documentary evidence from three continents, argues that disputes over Marxist-Leninist ideology played the primary role, while linking them to Chinese domestic politics and Mao's struggles with internal rivals. Donald Zagoria's earlier study combined ideological with historical, personal, economic and political causes; later scholars have stressed security concerns and national interest. Hoxha's *The Khruschevites* and the Chinese "Comments" are party polemics. As the book's global survey notes, "the party polemics remain evidence of what each leadership said. They are not, by themselves, a settled account of why the split happened." Political disagreement over the meaning of the split does not make a documented vote, text or date uncertain ([Lüthi](https://muse.jhu.edu/book/30313); [Wilson Center discussion](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/event/the-sino-soviet-split-1956-1966-the-cold-war-the-communist-world); [Hoxha, *The Khruschevites*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hoxha/works/1976/khruschevites/index.htm)).

## Eurocommunism and the New Left

Eurocommunism developed from Togliatti's "Italian road". In 1956 he wrote that "parliament, in the past the instrument for the organisation and consolidation of the capitalist regime, can today become an effective instrument in the hands of the parties which aim at a socialist transformation of society", and later argued for polycentrism, meaning that the movement had more than one centre. Hoxha devoted a book to the thesis that "Eurocommunism is Anti-Communism"; the Chinese called it modern revisionism. Berlinguer's "democratic alternative" of 1973, launched after Allende's overthrow, was a related argument about the parliamentary road: a 51 per cent majority would not by itself protect a government ([Togliatti, 1956](https://www.marxists.org/archive/togliatti/1956/togliatti-parliament.htm); [Togliatti Memorandum](https://www.marxists.org/archive/togliatti/1964/memorandum.htm); [Hoxha, 1980](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/hoxha/works/euroco/env2-1.htm); [Berlinguer, 1973](https://www.enricoberlinguer.it/scritti-discorsi-incontri-1972-1984/riflessioni-fatti-del-cile/)).

The New Left broke with the old parties from a different direction. Thompson described his generation as one that "never looked upon the Soviet Union as a weak but heroic Workers' State; but, rather, as the nation of the Great Purges and of Stalingrad"; the British Communist leaders he encountered called the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament a "pathetic illusion". In Paris the March 22 Movement declared "we don't want to be capitalism's watchdogs", while Maoists at Renault denounced the PCF and CGT leaderships for imposing "petty-bourgeois university watchwords". Some students took the opposite road: the Red Army Faction's urban guerrilla concept cited Mao, Lenin, Black Power and the Black Panthers and rejected parliamentary democracy. In the United States, RAM treated the vanguard as the sub-proletariat of exploited races rather than the industrial working class, and the Combahee River Collective wrote that "the major systems of oppression are interlocking". Cabral rejected imported formulas outright: "Because what you have to do in your country you have to create yourself." His claim that "every practice produces a theory" was a direct answer to parties that treated theory as an instruction from abroad ([Thompson, 1959](https://www.marxists.org/archive/thompson-ep/1959/newleft.htm); [March 22 leaflet](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/may-1968/1000-wounded.htm); [Renault leaflet](https://www.marxists.org/history/france/may-1968/red-flag.htm); [RAF, 1971](https://germanguerilla.com/red-army-faction/raf-doc/the-urban-guerilla-concept/); [RAM](http://freedomarchives.org/Documents/Finder/DOC513_scans/RAM/513.RAM.Black.America.Summer.1965.pdf); [Combahee](https://archive.org/stream/Combahee1979/Combahee%201979_djvu.txt); [Cabral, 1972](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/cabral/1973/return-source.pdf)).

## What the record cannot settle

There is broad agreement on the documents, votes, dates and ruptures. There is live disagreement over what chiefly caused them, and the sources here are not equally weighted. The nineteenth-century archive is largely the winners' archive; the First International's minutes come through a Soviet-edited volume, and ordinary members appear mainly when they dissented. The German revolution is well documented for Berlin leaders but has no rank-and-file testimony from the January fighting. The Sino-Soviet polemics are speeches by governments, while Lüthi's archival study and the later security-focused work are scholarly accounts. The New Left record survives in leaflets and manifestos; Ross, Abidor, Cunningham and Umoja study memory, repression and continuity, and disagree with one another. Chabal and Cardoso read Cabral as a practical theorist rather than a systematic one, and scholars dispute whether he was a Marxist in any orthodox sense. These are disagreements about evidence and interpretation, not a reason to treat a party's self-defence as equivalent to an archival finding.
