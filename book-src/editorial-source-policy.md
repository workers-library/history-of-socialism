This book explains socialism and communism through documentary evidence. It draws on the writings of political thinkers, the records of parties and states, the testimony of people who lived through events, contemporary reporting, and the work of historians who have studied the evidence.

## Primary sources

Primary sources are central. They include political writings, manifestos, speeches, letters, party congress records, government documents, constitutions, court proceedings, newspapers and periodicals, memoirs, oral histories and photographs. Each is read as evidence, not as an automatically reliable account. An official record may conceal a failure. A newspaper may reflect the interests of its owner. A memoir written decades later may reshape the past. The book asks who produced a source, when, for whom and for what purpose before using it.

## Range of evidence

The book is not limited to sources produced by movements or governments. Contemporary journalism, police and court records, intelligence material and academic research may all help establish what happened. Where such sources are used, their origins and limits are stated. Historians are engaged where their work helps explain the evidence, and disagreements between them are described with the methods and evidence behind each position.

## Attribution and uncertainty

Claims are attributed to the source that makes them. A government's description of its own policy is presented as what the government said, not as proof that the policy worked. A party's account of a dispute is presented as that party's account. Where evidence is contested, the book states what is known, what is uncertain and what historians disagree about. It does not suppress credible evidence because it complicates a political interpretation, and it does not manufacture balance between well supported findings and unsupported claims.

## Ordinary people

Where evidence permits, the book includes the experiences of workers, peasants, soldiers, women, prisoners, families and critics. Testimony is introduced with the witness's identity, their relationship to the event, when the account was recorded and what it can establish. The book does not invent dialogue, private thoughts, sensory detail or scenes.

## Quotations and citations

The policy requires that quotations reproduce their sources accurately, that omissions are marked, translations identified, and invented quotations, page numbers and archive references excluded. It requires each significant claim to be linked to a supporting source and each chapter to end with a bibliography. These are requirements, not a certification that every chapter currently meets them; known gaps are described in the methodology page.
