On 26 June 1848, the day after the heaviest fighting in Paris, the photographer Charles François Thibault made an image of the barricades in the rue Saint-Maur. Early photographic exposures could not capture movement, so the picture shows the street in daylight after the attack: a substantial barrier thrown up in a working-class district. It cannot show who built it or what happened there. It fixes a fact the written accounts can blur: for four days the eastern districts had fought the army and the National Guard, and the barricades were physical structures in ordinary streets ([Thibault, barricades of the rue Saint-Maur, 26 June 1848; countertype at Musée Carnavalet, original at Musée d'Orsay](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Contretype_d%27un_daguerr%C3%A9otype_de_Thibault_conserv%C3%A9_au_mus%C3%A9e_d%27Orsay._Barricades_de_la_rue_Saint-Maur,_le_26_Juin_1848,_a,_Paris_Mus%C3%A9es_20230513094941.jpg)).

Four months earlier the city had brought down a king and proclaimed a republic. What connected February to June was a demand that fitted into three words: the right to work, meaning the claim that a government must provide employment for those who could not find it. In February workers forced the new government to recognise it. In June they fought over its collapse. The sequence is well recorded because its witnesses disagreed sharply. A conservative deputy, a British diplomat and a revolutionary journalist all described the June fighting as a conflict between classes, then drew opposite conclusions.

## February

Barricades went up in Paris on 22 and 23 February, the king abdicated, and a provisional government was declared at the Hôtel de Ville. Marx, writing two years later, records how workers imposed themselves on it. The worker Raspail gave the Hôtel de Ville a two-hour ultimatum over the proclamation of the republic, and a worker named Marche pressed the claim for work. On 25 February the government decreed the right to work and promised social institutions; on 28 February it created a Commission for Workers with Louis Blanc as president and Albert, a worker, as vice-president. The commission was housed in the Luxembourg Palace, from which it took its name.

## The Luxembourg and the workshops

The Luxembourg Commission began taking evidence on 1 March. Its own published proceedings record 150 to 200 worker delegates sitting in the chamber once occupied by the peers of France. The two demands they pressed hardest were set down in the minutes: "Réduction du nombre des heures de travail ; Abolition du marchandage" ([Luxembourg Commission proceedings, 2 March 1848](https://archive.org/stream/larvolutiondefv00blangoog/larvolutiondefv00blangoog_djvu.txt)), a reduction in working hours and an end to *marchandage*, the practice of letting middlemen subcontract pieces of work to poorly paid artisans. Employers and workers were heard separately, and the commission secured a one-hour reduction in the working day. The record is a managed self-presentation, giving workers' demands as the chair recorded them. Marx judged that the commission had no budget and no executive authority; its authority was real only where the government conceded it.

The right to work took a different institutional form in the national workshops, administered from the rival Hôtel de Ville rather than the Luxembourg. Enrolment grew quickly, and the work offered was relief work, chiefly earthworks, at low pay. Marx called them "English workhouses in the open — that is what these national *ateliers* were." ([Karl Marx, *The Class Struggles in France*, Part I, written 1850](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1850/class-struggles-france/ch01.htm)). Émile Thomas, appointed to direct the workshops, defended them in a 1848 book reprinting the decrees and official papers. He describes his removal from Paris by ministerial order in May, which Lord Normanby, the British minister, independently recorded. Thomas was answering criticism and hostile to Blanc; Normanby's government had not formally recognised the republic. Their agreement is the more useful for being unwilling.

## June

On 22 June workshop workers marched, chanting that they would not be sent away, and Tocqueville records delegates warning the Executive Commission that they would take up arms if the workshops were closed. Lord Normanby, moving about the city during the fighting, wrote in his journal for 23 June: "it has all the appearance of being a general outbreak of Les Proletaires de Paris: not at all confined to those hitherto connected with the Ateliers Nationaux" ([Lord Normanby, journal entry, 23 June 1848, published 1857](https://archive.org/stream/yearofrevolution02normuoft/yearofrevolution02normuoft_djvu.txt)). He noted that no one seemed certain what the insurgents wanted. On the morning of 24 June the Assembly granted executive power to General Cavaignac.

Marx, editing a newspaper in Cologne and working from dispatches, wrote on 29 June, after the 26 June defeat: "The June revolution is the ugly revolution, the nasty revolution, because the phrases have given place to the real thing" ([Karl Marx, "The June Revolution", 29 June 1848](https://marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1848/06/29a.htm)). Of the night of 25 June he wrote of "when the Paris of the bourgeoisie held illuminations while the Paris of the proletariat was burning, bleeding, groaning in the throes of death" ([Karl Marx, "The June Revolution", 29 June 1848](https://marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1848/06/29a.htm)). These are political judgments written in the immediate aftermath, not neutral description. Engels, sifting "facts from either hearsay or lies," praised "the vast magnitude of the uprising, the heroic courage, the rapidly improvised organization and the unanimity of the insurgents" ([Frederick Engels, "The Course of the Paris Uprising", 30 June 1848](https://marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1848/07/01.htm)). His four-column plan for an advance on the Hôtel de Ville introduces its supposed author with "is said to have," so it cannot be treated as established. Tocqueville, who had watched the Constituent Assembly from inside, reached the shared diagnosis from the opposite side: "It was not, strictly speaking, a political struggle, in the sense which until then we had given to the word, but a combat of class against class, a sort of Servile War." ([Alexis de Tocqueville, *Recollections*, written 1850, published 1896](https://archive.org/stream/recollectionsofa00tocquoft/recollectionsofa00tocquoft_djvu.txt)) He explained what he had seen through greed and false theory. He also noted women preparing ammunition and being last to surrender.

## After June

The defeated insurgents were tried by courts-martial and deported without trial; the ten-hour day was repealed. Marx records a petition from the wives and children of insurgents asking for amnesty, and the Assembly's refusal on 19 December. The Assembly also grasped at the allegation that foreign gold had funded the rising. Normanby records Marrast and Flocon blaming neighbouring governments in the official *Moniteur*, while protesting the accusation himself. No surviving document substantiates it, so it belongs among the claims made at the time rather than among findings.

On 10 December the peasant vote decided the presidency. Marx, who had argued that a 45-centime tax on the peasants made them the natural allies of reaction, wrote: "December 10, 1848, was the day of the *peasant insurrection.*" ([Karl Marx, *The Class Struggles in France*, Part II, written 1850](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1850/class-struggles-france/ch02.htm)) Louis-Napoléon won roughly six million votes to Cavaignac's one million. In the *Eighteenth Brumaire*, written three years later, Marx described the return of the old danger: "it appears not with the Phrygian cap of anarchy on its head, but in the uniform of order, in *red breeches*." ([Karl Marx, *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte*, ch. III, written 1851–52](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1852/18th-brumaire/ch03.htm)) Louis Blanc, defending the Luxembourg after his exile, insisted that the battle came only after the republic and universal suffrage were secure: "la bataille de juin ne s'est livrée qu'après le triomphe du suffrage universel" ([Louis Blanc, *Révélations historiques*, 1859](https://archive.org/stream/histoiredelarvo01blangoog/histoiredelarvo01blangoog_djvu.txt)), the battle of June was fought only after the triumph of universal suffrage. Both men had been participants. Blanc's memory and Marx's analysis are evidence of how each understood the defeat, not neutral accounts of it.

## What the record cannot show

No first-person account by a rank-and-file June insurgent survives. Workers speak as delegates in a commission record shaped by its chair, or as figures in other men's prose. The women Tocqueville and Normanby describe carrying ammunition, and the woman shot on a barricade, never speak in their own words. The peasants whose votes decided the presidency are known through Marx's class analysis, not through testimony. Figures for the killed, wounded, imprisoned and deported are not established by these sources; the photograph shows one street after the attack, not the people who defended it. The French texts have no published English version in general use, so the French quotations here carry a working translation, and the scanned editions have OCR defects that need checking against the page images. No court-martial transcript, deportation register or prefectoral archive was consulted. Those gaps fall hardest on the question of who fought and why, which is the one question on which Marx, Tocqueville and Normanby most confidently claimed to know the answer. Mark Traugott's quantitative study of participation ([Traugott, 1985](https://archive.org/details/armiesofpoordete0000trau)) and Donald Cope McKay's monograph on the workshops' administration ([McKay, 1933](https://archive.org/details/nationalworkshop0000mcka)) address two of these questions.
