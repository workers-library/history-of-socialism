Mikhail Bakunin argued that the state, not capital alone, was the central obstacle to working-class emancipation. His writings on authority, federation and the "People's State" gave anarchism a theory of domination and a warning about revolutionary governments that later movements returned to repeatedly. This profile explains those arguments from his own texts, records the organizational disputes they produced, and reports the criticisms made of them without smoothing them over.

## Biography

The published chapter on the [First International](/first-international) already covers the Association's founding, its address and rules, the weekly work of the General Council, the Alliance crisis and the Hague expulsion of 1872; this profile adds the arguments Bakunin himself made, the texts in which he made them and the scholarly disputes about his politics rather than retelling the organisation's history.

Mikhail Aleksandrovich Bakunin was born on 18 May 1814 by the old Russian calendar, or 30 May by the Western calendar, at the family estate of Priamukhino in Tver province, north-west of Moscow, and died in Berne on 1 July 1876. These dates and places come from Marshall Shatz's introduction and chronology to the Cambridge edition of *Statism and Anarchy*, not from a primary document opened for this profile ([Shatz, Scholarly Secondary](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). Shatz describes a well-off gentry family owning about 500 male serfs, with the children educated at home on Rousseauist principles. Bakunin was sent to St Petersburg in 1828 to prepare for the Artillery School, received his officer's commission, but was dismissed for disciplinary reasons in 1834 and sent to a provincial garrison. He then immersed himself in philosophy in Moscow, first in Fichte and then in Hegel, and met Herzen and Ogarev.

Paris was the decisive place in the 1840s. Shatz records that Bakunin met Marx in 1844 and became fast friends with Proudhon, whose combination of the critique of capitalism with hostility to the state he later adapted while rejecting much of Proudhon's programme. The Russian government ordered him home, stripped him of noble status and sentenced him in absentia to hard labour in Siberia after he refused. In 1847 he spoke at a Paris banquet marking the Polish uprising of 1830–31, and in 1848 he returned to Paris, went to Germany, took part in the Prague Slav Congress and its insurrection, and published the *Appeal to the Slavs*. In May 1849 he joined the Dresden insurrection, was arrested, was sentenced to death by Saxon and then Austrian courts, and was extradited to Russia in 1851.

Shatz's chronology gives May 1851 for incarceration in the Peter-Paul Fortress, July–August 1851 for the "Confession" to Nicholas I, March 1854 for transfer to Schlüsselburg Fortress, March 1857 for release and exile to Siberia, October 1858 for his marriage to Antonia Kwiatkowska in Tomsk, July 1861 for his escape via Japan and San Francisco, and December 1861 for his arrival in London. The later chronology is tied to the organizational history: a failed attempt to reach the Polish insurrection of 1863, arrival in Italy in 1864, the International Brotherhood of 1866, joining the International in 1864 and the Geneva Central Section in 1868, the International Alliance of Socialist Democracy in September 1868, its formal dissolution and admission as the Geneva Section in March 1869, the Russian Brotherhood and Slavic Section of Zurich in 1872, expulsion at The Hague in September 1872, publication of *Statism and Anarchy* in 1873, the abortive Bologna insurrection of 1874, and death in Berne in 1876 ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). The MIA archive index prints 1817 in its heading while its own text gives 1814 and attributes the paragraph to a tertiary source; neither the heading nor that paragraph is used here as evidence ([MIA index, directory only](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/index.htm)).

## Intellectual tradition

Bakunin called himself an anarchist and accepted the name. In *Statism and Anarchy* he wrote that "we are the enemies of every government and every state power, and of governmental organization in general", and that "we are therefore called anarchists. We do not protest this name" ([MIA, *Statism and Anarchy*, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1873/statism-anarchy.htm)). Shatz notes that Bakunin used "anarchy" for what is now called anarchism, and that he belongs to the collectivist and social-anarchist current rather than to individualist or market anarchism. Collectivist anarchism here means the current of anarchism that wants land and the means of production held in common and society coordinated by free association and federation, rather than by isolated individuals or market exchange ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy places Bakunin, Kropotkin and Goldman among the "classical anarchists" who developed anarchism in response to Marx and Marxism, and classifies his critique of the state as a central example of political anarchism ([SEP, Scholarly Secondary](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anarchism/)). Kropotkin and Goldman are named here as plain text because no profile of either is assumed to exist.

His affiliations were built around secret and semi-secret bodies as well as published argument. The 1868 program of the International Alliance of Socialist Democracy declared itself atheist, sought the "political, economic, and social equalization of classes and individuals of both sexes", and stated that the Alliance "constitutes a branch of the International Working Men's Association and accepts all its general rules" ([IWA document, Primary Official](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/iwma/documents/1868/iasd-program.htm)). Shatz records that "still other secret organizations may have existed, and the attempt to sort them out has bedeviled historians for a hundred years", and judges that in most cases they were "nothing more than small circles of like-minded intimates, for whom Bakunin delighted in drawing up elaborate statutes and statements of purpose" ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). That gap between anti-authoritarian theory and conspiratorial practice is the main line of criticism from both Marxists and later historians.

## Major contributions

The first contribution was a theory of the state as an institution of domination rather than a neutral instrument. In the *Revolutionary Catechism* he wrote that "absolute rejection of every authority including that which sacrifices freedom for the convenience of the state" was a fundamental principle, and that political and economic organization "must be reorganized to issue from the base to the summit – from the circumference to the center – according to the principles of free association and federation" ([MIA, *Revolutionary Catechism*, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1866/catechism.htm)). In *Statism and Anarchy* he put the claim in its starkest form: "If there is a State, there must be domination of one class by another and, as a result, slavery; the State without slavery is unthinkable – and this is why we are the enemies of the State" ([MIA, *Statism and Anarchy*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1873/statism-anarchy.htm)). The SEP quotes the same sentence with a page reference to the Cambridge edition, 1873 [1990: 178]; that reference is reported from the SEP and was not checked against a physical or paginated copy ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anarchism/)).

The second contribution was the critique of state socialism and of what he called the "People's State". Statism here means the doctrine that socialism needs a state, including a temporary revolutionary state, to reach its goal; it is the view Bakunin opposed with his claim that a state cannot liberate the people it rules. He argued that "the theory of statism as well as that of so-called 'revolutionary dictatorship' is based on the idea that a 'privileged elite,' consisting of those scientists and 'doctrinaire revolutionists' who believe that 'theory is prior to social experience,' should impose their preconceived scheme of social organization on the people" ([MIA, *Statism and Anarchy*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1873/statism-anarchy.htm)). He predicted that a Marxist dictatorship would be a dictatorship over the proletariat, not by it. The Cambridge publisher's description of the 1990 edition states this explicitly: the book predicts that a "dictatorship of the proletariat" will "in fact be a dictatorship over the proletariat, and will produce a new class of socialist rulers" ([CUP, Primary Official publisher record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/bakunin-statism-and-anarchy/666E6E893315DF75B47B41C0AE798EDC)). That is the publisher's description of the book, not an independent finding.

The third contribution was federalism as a positive institutional principle. In *The Paris Commune and the Idea of the State* he wrote that "the future social organization should be carried out from the bottom up, by the free association or federation of workers, starting with the associations, then going on to the communes, the regions, the nations, and, finally, culminating in a great international and universal federation" ([MIA, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1871/paris-commune.htm)). His reply to the [Paris Commune](/paris-commune) is therefore part of an argument about how a liberated society should be built, not only a comment on the rising. Shatz judges that "Bakunin was the first to warn that Marxists in power might simply replace the capitalists they had chased out, leaving the position of the workers essentially unchanged, and after him it became a major component of anarchist thought" ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)).

The fourth contribution was his account of authority and science. In "What is Authority?" he distinguished knowledge from rule: "In the matter of boots, I refer to the authority of the bootmaker; concerning houses, canals, or railroads, I consult that of the architect or the engineer", but no specialist may impose that authority by force ([MIA, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/various/authrty.htm)). In *God and the State* he called for "the revolt of life against science, or rather against the government of science, not to destroy science – that would be high treason to humanity – but to remand it to its place" ([MIA, *God and the State*, ch. III](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/godstate/ch03.htm)). The target was expertise backed by coercion, not expertise itself.

## Analysis of capitalism

Bakunin's account of capitalism starts from the coercion hidden inside a formally free wage contract. In a passage drawn from the unfinished *Knouto-Germanic Empire and the Social Revolution* and reprinted in Maximoff's 1953 selection, he wrote that the capitalist comes to the market driven by "the urge to get rich", while the worker comes driven by "hunger, the necessity of eating today and tomorrow" ([MIA, *The Capitalist System*, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/various/capsys.htm)). His summary of the resulting relation is explicit: "The worker is in the position of a serf because this terrible threat of starvation which daily hangs over his head and over his family, will force him to accept any conditions imposed by the gainful calculations of the capitalist, the industrialist, the employer" ([MIA, *The Capitalist System*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/various/capsys.htm)).

He treated legal equality as a fiction masking economic compulsion. The worker's liberty, he wrote, "is only a theoretical freedom, lacking any means for its possible realization, and consequently it is only a fictitious liberty, an utter falsehood"; after the contract the employer owns "his actions and movements" during working hours ([MIA, *The Capitalist System*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/various/capsys.htm)). He traced the reproduction of poverty to population growth outstripping production, to workers competing against one another, and to competition forcing capitalists to concentrate capital in fewer hands and joint-stock companies. Property and capital, he wrote, are "the power and the right, guaranteed by the State, to live without working" ([MIA, *The Capitalist System*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/various/capsys.htm)).

His economic argument borrows rather than originates. The same text quotes Turgot and J. B. Say on wages being held to the cost of subsistence, quotes Proudhon on competition, and praises Marx's *Capital* as an analysis "so profound, so luminous, so scientific, so decisive" of "the formation of bourgeois capital and the systematic and cruel exploitation that capital continues exercising over the work of the proletariat" ([MIA, *The Capitalist System*, footnotes](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/various/capsys.htm)). Shatz confirms that Bakunin respected Marx's intellect and "adopted many of his criticisms of capitalism", possibly being the first Russian to study Marx closely ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). Bakunin's distinctive addition is the claim that the state, law and religion actively secure this exploitation rather than merely standing above it. The economic critique and the political critique are one argument in his writing, even though the economics was largely inherited.

## Analysis of socialism

Bakunin did not write a detailed blueprint of a completed socialist society; several of his major texts are unfinished fragments, and the *Revolutionary Catechism* explicitly deals with a society in transition, not "the anarchist heaven" ([MIA, *Revolutionary Catechism*, editor's note](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1866/catechism.htm)). What he did provide was a set of institutional principles. The 1868 Alliance program demanded common ownership of "the land, instruments of labor, like all other capital", use of them "only by the workers", the abolition of the right of inheritance, and equal conditions of development for all children ([IWA document, Primary Official](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/iwma/documents/1868/iasd-program.htm)). The *Catechism* adds "the abolition of the right of inheritance", equal political, social and economic rights for women, secular communal education, directly elected judges and officials, and the "completely autonomous commune" as the basic unit ([MIA, *Revolutionary Catechism*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1866/catechism.htm)).

The mechanism he expected was spontaneous federation from below. In *Statism and Anarchy* he wrote that the masses carry "in their instincts (more or less developed by history), in their daily necessities, and in their conscious or unconscious aspirations, all the elements of the future social organization", and that people can be free only when "organized from the bottom up in completely free and independent associations, without governmental paternalism" ([MIA, *Statism and Anarchy*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1873/statism-anarchy.htm)). He rejected schemes imposed from a book or by a learned minority, and insisted that "freedom can be created only by freedom, by a total rebellion of the people, and by a voluntary organization of the people from the bottom up" ([MIA, *Statism and Anarchy*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1873/statism-anarchy.htm)). The absence of a blueprint follows from this premise: a design fixed in advance would repeat the rule of the learned minority he opposed.

His break with the Marxists was over method as much as over the goal. In his unsent letter to *La Liberté* he wrote that "we do not accept, even for the purposes of a revolutionary transition, national conventions, constituent assemblies, provisional governments, or so-called revolutionary dictatorships, because we are convinced that revolution is sincere and permanent only within the masses; that when it is concentrated in the hands of a few ruling individuals, it inevitably and immediately turns into reaction" ([MIA, *Letter to La Liberté*, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1872/la-liberte.htm)). His preferred organization was the International as a federation of autonomous sections whose only binding principle was economic solidarity; political and philosophical opinions were to be discussed freely but never made obligatory ([MIA, *On the International Workingmen's Association and Karl Marx*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1872/karl-marx.htm)). The same text claims that more than a million workers had joined the International; that figure is reported as Bakunin's claim, not as a verified membership count ([MIA, *On the IWMA and Karl Marx*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1872/karl-marx.htm)).

## Recommended reading

These five works are the ones that best carry Bakunin's own argument. Each annotation restates what the work contributes; all five appear in the complete bibliography above.

- *Statism and Anarchy* (1873; Marshall Shatz translation, Cambridge University Press, 1990). His last major work and the clearest statement of why he thought any state, including a workers' state, must rule over the people and how he thought a federal, stateless society could replace it.
- *God and the State* (written 1871, first published 1882). The most widely translated statement of his materialist critique of religion and his claim that science must advise rather than govern.
- *Revolutionary Catechism* (1865–66, with a conflicting date line on MIA). The compact set of principles for the International Fraternity, including federation from the commune upward and the rejection of authority imposed for the sake of the state.
- *The Political Philosophy of Bakunin*, edited by G. P. Maximoff (1953). The broadest English selection, containing *The Capitalist System* and the "Morality of the State" passages that are otherwise hard to find in English.
- *Marxism, Freedom and the State*, translated by K. J. Kenafick (Freedom Press, 1950). Bakunin's own account of the split with Marx, assembled from his 1867–72 writings, and the best single source for his view of the International dispute.

## Selected lectures and interviews

No sound recording, film or televised interview of Bakunin was opened or verified, and none is claimed here. Bakunin died in 1876, before such recording was available. Shatz's chronology records public speaking, including the 1847 Paris banquet speech and speeches in Stockholm during the 1863 Polish crisis, and MIA hosts the texts of these speeches, but a printed speech is not a verified audiovisual source ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy); [MIA index, directory](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/index.htm)). This section therefore records an absence in the material opened for the profile rather than claiming that no such material ever existed.

## Intellectual influences

Hegel was the earliest decisive influence. Shatz records that in Moscow in the 1830s Bakunin "made a very serious study of at least parts of Hegel's doctrines", and that his first original publication was a preface to his translation of two of Hegel's *Gymnasium Lectures* ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). In *Statism and Anarchy* Bakunin treated Hegelian metaphysics and positivism together as "metaphysicians" who would force "future generations into the narrow mold" of theory ([MIA, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1873/statism-anarchy.htm)). *God and the State* names Feuerbach and Comte as the thinkers who completed the demolition of the divine idea, and discusses Mazzini, Michelet, Quinet and John Stuart Mill as his idealist opponents ([MIA, *God and the State*, chs. I and III](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/godstate/ch01.htm)).

Proudhon is the influence Bakunin most clearly absorbed and then modified. Shatz writes that Proudhon "was the first to combine the critique of capitalism with anarchism's hostility to the state", and that Bakunin "assimilated many of Proudhon's basic positions into his anarchist ideology" while later rejecting much of his programme ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). Marx supplied the economic critique: Bakunin's *Capitalist System* praises *Capital* and quotes Turgot, Say and Proudhon in support of its account of wages ([MIA, *The Capitalist System*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/various/capsys.htm)). Herzen and Ogarev were lifelong friends and political interlocutors, and Shatz records that Bakunin joined their circle in London in 1861 ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). Rousseau appears mainly as an object of criticism: *The Immorality of the State* attacks "the great fallacy on the part of Jean Jacques Rousseau" of deriving society from a free contract ([MIA, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1870/immorality-of-the-state.html)).

## Influence and legacy

The Cambridge publisher's description says *Statism and Anarchy* "had an immediate influence on the 'to the people' movement of Russian populism" and that Bakunin's ideas "inspired significant anarchist movements in Spain, Italy, Russia and elsewhere" ([CUP, Primary Official](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/bakunin-statism-and-anarchy/666E6E893315DF75B47B41C0AE798EDC)). These are the publisher's claims about the book's reach, not figures verified here. Shatz adds that the warning about a new ruling class "became a major component of anarchist thought" after Bakunin and that his best-known work, *God and the State*, "has appeared in at least sixteen languages" ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). Shatz's bibliographical note points to studies of Bakunin's influence on Spanish anarchism by George Esenwein and Temma Kaplan, and on Italian socialism by Richard Hostetter and T. R. Ravindranathan; those studies were not opened, so their findings are not repeated here as established facts ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)).

His organizational legacy is contested. Shatz notes that the proliferation of secret groups "has bedeviled historians for a hundred years", and singles out Arthur Lehning's 1974 study of Bakunin's revolutionary organizations as the work that "sorts out the welter of conspiratorial groups" ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy); Lehning's article was identified through this note but not opened). The SEP entry treats Bakunin as a paradigm case in the history of anarchist criticism of the state, while warning that such sweeping generalizations are difficult to support and that historical context matters ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/anarchism/)). Later movements should be described as selecting different parts of his argument, not as inheriting a single settled doctrine; Shatz calls Kropotkin Bakunin's successor as the foremost theorist of Russian anarchism, without settling the terms on which they agreed or disagreed ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)).

## Criticisms and controversies

**The dispute with Marx over the dictatorship of the proletariat.** Bakunin's *Statism and Anarchy* asks: "What does it mean that the proletariat will be elevated to a ruling class? Is it possible for the whole proletariat to stand at the head of the government? There are nearly forty million Germans. Can all forty million be members of the government? In such a case, there will be no government, no state, but, if there is to be a state there will be those who are ruled and those who are slaves" ([MIA, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1873/statism-anarchy.htm)). He concluded that "all dictatorship has no objective other than self-perpetuation, and that slavery is all it can generate and instill in the people who suffer it" ([MIA, *Statism and Anarchy*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1873/statism-anarchy.htm)). Marx answered in his 1874–75 notebook: "It means that so long as the other classes, especially the capitalist class, still exists, so long as the proletariat struggles with it ... it must employ forcible means, hence governmental means", and "With its complete victory its own rule thus also ends, as its class character has disappeared" ([Marx, Conspectus, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1874/04/bakunin-notes.htm)). Marx's central objection was that "the will, and not the economic conditions, is the foundation of his social revolution" ([Marx, Conspectus](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1874/04/bakunin-notes.htm)). The English extract is translated from *MEW* 18, pp. 630–36; that volume was not opened, and Marx's words are quoted from the MIA English page. Shatz judges this "a criticism" with "much to be said" for it, but adds that Marx did not perceive the opposite criticism: his only response to the warning about a new elite was to reiterate that political authority would disappear with class rule ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). This is the theoretical core of the disagreement described more fully in the historical chapter on [Marx and Engels](/marx-and-engels).

**The split in the International.** Marx's marginal notes of 15 December 1868 objected that "The International Association does not admit any 'International branches'" and called the Alliance's proposed central bureau a "New Central Council!" ([Marx's notes, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/iwma/documents/1868/iasd-comment.htm)). The General Council resolved unanimously on 22 December 1868 that the Alliance's rules defining its relation to the IWMA were "null and void" and that it "may not be admitted as a branch" ([IWMA resolution, Primary Official](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/iwma/documents/1868/bakunin-resolution.htm)). At The Hague in September 1872 the Congress adopted a new rule stating that "the conquest of political power has therefore become the great duty of the working class" by 29 votes to 5 with 8 abstentions ([Hague resolutions, Primary Official](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/iwma/documents/1872/hague-conference/resolutions.htm)). The Commission of Inquiry reported that the secret Alliance "has existed, but it has not been sufficiently proved to the commission that it still exists", while finding that Bakunin had attempted to found it and had resorted to "dishonest dealings" in a property matter ([Commission report, Primary Official](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/iwma/documents/1872/hague-commission/report.htm)). Bakunin was expelled. Shatz's assessment is that Marx succeeded "on the grounds, for which no convincing proof was offered", that Bakunin still maintained a secret Alliance, and that the additional charge concerned an alleged fraud and intimidation over his projected translation of *Capital* ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). The formal vote figures in the opened IWA pages disagree: the Commission index reports 24 for, 6 against and 7 abstentions on expulsion, while the resolutions page reports 27 for, 6 against and 7 abstentions and notes that votes of delegates who had left the sitting were counted. The discrepancy is recorded, not resolved. The events themselves are told in the chapter on the [First International](/first-international).

**Criticism of his organizational ideas.** The contradiction between Bakunin's federalism in theory and his secret societies in practice was noted by Shatz, who describes the "small circles of like-minded intimates" and the historian's difficulty in sorting them out ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). Marx's 1868 notes are the contemporary version of the same objection: a self-appointed central bureau and a separate congress under the International's name would, in his words, "compromise us under our own patronage" ([Marx's notes](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/iwma/documents/1868/iasd-comment.htm)). Shatz's guide names Aileen Kelly's 1982 biography as "informative but hostile to its subject", Eugene Pyziur's 1955 study as "a superficial analysis", and Arthur Lehning's 1974 article as the specialist study of the secret societies; none of these was read at source, so only their existence and Shatz's characterization of them are recorded here ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)).

**Antisemitic remarks.** The primary texts confirm the substance of the charge. The Cambridge edition of *Statism and Anarchy* contains the passage: "By origin Marx is a Jew. One might say that he combines all of the positive qualities and all of the shortcomings of that capable race"; it also explains Marx's and Lassalle's statism by "his threefold capacity as an Hegelian, a Jew, and a German", and says Lassalle "was too much of a Jew to feel comfortable among the people" ([Bakunin, *Statism and Anarchy*, Shatz translation, Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). The same work complains that German workers are "confused by their leaders – politicians, literati, and Jews" ([Bakunin, *Statism and Anarchy*](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). Shatz writes that "Equally repellent, though less marked in this work than in some others, is Bakunin's anti-Semitism, which often appeared as a corollary to his anti-Germanism", and that it "long antedated his conflict with Marx" ([Shatz, Scholarly Secondary](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)). He records the counter-argument fairly: "It may be argued that such sentiments, however distasteful, do not negate Bakunin's anarchist principles. It may also be argued that those principles are somehow deficient if even one so passionately committed to them was unable to surmount crude ethnic prejudices." His concluding point is that Bakunin "was hardly unique in this regard" among nineteenth-century socialists and anarchists ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)).

Zoe Baker's essay catalogues further examples, including Bakunin's references to Nicholas Utin as a "little Jew" and to Marx's "truly Jewish vanity", and her judgement that "On the few occasions where he is antisemitic it is abhorrent and should be rejected by everybody" ([Baker, Scholarly Secondary, non-peer-reviewed essay](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/zoe-baker-bakunin-was-a-racist)). Those additional passages were not verified against primary texts opened here and are attributed to Baker rather than asserted independently. René Berthier's reply argues that Baker's claim that Bakunin "was a racist" is incorrect because antisemitic allusions are rare in a body of work devoted to human emancipation, and disputes the propriety of judging a nineteenth-century writer by post-Holocaust criteria ([Berthier, Scholarly Secondary, polemical reply](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/rene-berthier-was-bakunin-a-racist)). Both essays are interventions from within the anarchist tradition, not peer-reviewed studies; the dispute is reported as live, with the primary passages quoted above as the firm ground.

## Related thinkers

**Karl Marx.** The central opponent. Bakunin respected Marx's economics and adopted much of its critique of capital while rejecting his politics ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy); [MIA, *The Capitalist System*](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/various/capsys.htm)). Marx's side of the argument survives in his 1868 notes and the 1874–75 *Conspectus* ([Marx, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1874/04/bakunin-notes.htm)). The chapter on [Marx and Engels](/marx-and-engels) tells the history of that conflict.

**Friedrich Engels.** A participant in the General Council and the Hague proceedings; the IWA archive lists Engels's report on the Alliance and the later polemic *The Bakuninists at Work*, neither of which was opened for this profile ([IWA conflict index, directory](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/iwma/documents/bakunin-conflict.htm)).

**Pierre-Joseph Proudhon.** The first to join the critique of capitalism to hostility to the state, and the largest single influence on Bakunin's anarchism, though Bakunin later rejected much of his programme ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)).

**Alexander Herzen and Nikolai Ogarev.** Lifetime friends and the London circle Bakunin joined in 1861; their journal *The Bell* was the background to his early émigré politics ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)).

**Giuseppe Mazzini.** A target of *The Political Theology of Mazzini and the International* and an object of criticism in *God and the State*; Shatz records that their disagreements over nationalism and the state were personal as well as political ([MIA index, directory](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/index.htm); [MIA, *God and the State*, ch. I](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/godstate/ch01.htm)).

**Ferdinand Lassalle.** Bakunin treated Lassalle's state socialism and his parliamentary strategy as a warning, and described him in explicitly antisemitic terms in *Statism and Anarchy* ([Bakunin, Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)).

**Sergei Nechayev.** The MIA archive lists Bakunin's 1870 "Rebuke of Nechayev on the role of secret revolutionary societies"; the text was not opened, and no claim is made about its contents ([MIA index, directory](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/index.htm)).

**James Guillaume and the Jura Federation.** Guillaume was expelled with Bakunin at The Hague; Bakunin's 1873 letter of resignation to the Jura Federation is opened here ([Hague resolutions, Primary Official](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/iwma/documents/1872/hague-conference/resolutions.htm); [MIA, Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/bakunin/works/1873/letter-jura.htm)).

**Peter Kropotkin.** Shatz calls Kropotkin Bakunin's successor as the foremost theorist of Russian anarchism; no claim is made here about the terms on which they agreed or disagreed ([Shatz](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/mikhail-bakunin-statism-and-anarchy)).
