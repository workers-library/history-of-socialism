On the morning of 18 March 1871 a crowd gathered on Montmartre to take back the cannons that the government had tried to remove ([Prosper-Olivier Lissagaray, *Histoire de la Commune de 1871*](<https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Histoire_de_la_Commune_de_1871_%28Lissagaray%29>)). That government had withdrawn to Versailles after the national defeat and the winter siege of Paris. Louise Michel, a schoolteacher and combatant who was there, recalled that the National Guard, the citizen militia, fraternised with the troops instead of fighting them, and that two generals, Clément Thomas and Lecomte, were killed. She wrote that account fifteen years later, so it is a recollection rather than a transcript. What survives is a set of records made by people who had to explain the Commune, a local governing council, after it was crushed: its own decrees, a court record, memoirs written in exile and an inquiry held a generation later. Together they show a municipal administration, a military defence and a policy of reprisal, and participants disagreed about all three.

## The decree of 5 April

A placard of 25 March summoned the electorate to choose a Commune, and the vote gave the rising an institutional form. Michel remembered women building and holding a barricade at the Place Blanche in the fighting of May: "Les femmes, aux jours de Mai, élevèrent et défendirent la barricade de la place Blanche. Elles tinrent jusqu'à la mort." ([*Mémoires de Louise Michel*, written 1886](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/M%C3%A9moires_de_Louise_Michel/Texte_entier)). The document that fixes policy most sharply is the decree of 2 prairial an 79, or 5 April 1871, printed in the Commune's own *Journal officiel*. Article 4 declared that detainees held under the decree were "the hostages of the people of Paris". Article 5 supplied the consequence: "Toute exécution d'un prisonnier de guerre ou d'un partisan du gouvernement régulier de la Commune de Paris sera, sur-le-champ, suivie de l'exécution d'un nombre triple d'otages retenus en vertu de l'article 4, et qui seront désignés par le sort." ([Article 5 of the hostage decree, 5 April 1871](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/%C2%AB_D%C3%A9cret_des_otages_%C2%BB_du_2_prairial_An_79)). A decree records an intention, not its application. Gaston Da Costa, then twenty and a chief of staff who helped move prisoners from Mazas to La Roquette in requisitioned vans, told the 1897 inquiry that the decree had not been carried out as it was written. The killings at La Roquette and in the rue Haxo followed a different and improvised path.

A second document concerns a bargain that was never made. Benjamin Flotte, a longtime associate of Auguste Blanqui, argued in an 1885 pamphlet that some seventy-four hostages, among them the Archbishop of Paris, could have been exchanged for Blanqui, and he held Adolphe Thiers responsible for refusing. Flotte wrote as an advocate, in openly polemical language. His pamphlet establishes that the exchange was proposed and how he judged its refusal; it does not establish Thiers's motive.

## Voices from twenty-six years later

In 1897 *La Revue blanche* asked participants and contemporaries to explain the rising. The answers disagree. Ernest Daudet, who had lived in Paris during the Commune, called it "Je considère l'insurrection de 1871 comme un crime abominable, — un crime de lèse-patrie — encore que je ne tienne pas pour également criminels tous ceux qui y prirent part." ([Ernest Daudet, inquiry, 15 March 1897](<https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Enqu%C3%AAte_sur_la_Commune_%28La_Revue_blanche%29/1>)). Henri Rochefort, who backed the Commune against Versailles but opposed carrying out the hostage decree, said he had come close to being shot by a council of war. Ranc, a Commune member, described his own attempt at conciliation, and Melliet named the Commune's fault as not knowing and not daring. Da Costa explained how the word hostage had been heard: "Le peuple, la masse, ne comprenait pas bien le sens du mot otage et traduisait naïvement : curé, agent." ([Gaston Da Costa, inquiry, 1 April 1897](<https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Enqu%C3%AAte_sur_la_Commune_%28La_Revue_blanche%29/2>)). The editors themselves conceded that hostile replies were scarce. These are statements by people with reputations to protect, not a balanced sample of opinion.

The social composition of the council rests on firmer evidence. Jacques Rougerie's figures, quoted by Stathis Kouvelakis, put 33 of the 78 council members in the working class and almost 70 per cent of roughly 36,000 people arrested in the same category. Counts of this kind are the basis for describing the Commune as a working-class government. Marx, writing from London in May 1871 while the fighting was still under way, gave that description its best-known form: "the working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready-made state machinery, and wield it for its own purposes." ([Karl Marx, *The Civil War in France*, Third Address, May 1871](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1871/civil-war-france/ch05.htm)). His address was a contemporary interpretation put together from newspaper reports, one voice among many, not a verdict from outside the events.

## The last week

By 21 May troops from Versailles were inside Paris and the struggle moved into the streets. The week that followed is called the *Semaine sanglante*, the Bloody Week. Jules Vallès, a journalist and Commune member, wrote the collapse from inside the twentieth arrondissement as fiction, in a novel published in 1886: "Dimanche 28 mai, 5 h. du matin. Nous sommes à la barricade géante qui est au bas de la rue de Belleville, presque devant la salle Favié." ([Jules Vallès, *L'Insurgé*, 1886](<https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/L%E2%80%99Insurg%C3%A9_%28Vall%C3%A8s%29/Texte_entier>)). He described a young gunner killed beside him and a refusal to receive a parliamentary, a negotiator. The scenes are a participant's reconstruction shaped as literature, not a diary. On 24 May, after the Hôtel de Ville burned, Georges Arnold later recalled that Delescluze, Vaillant and he were delegated to seek an armistice through the German commander at Saint-Denis and to offer unconditional surrender, so that the Communards alone should carry the responsibility. National Guard posts blocked their way and Versailles refused. A government that had lost its headquarters was still trying to negotiate and still issuing orders.

Victorine Brocher was in Belleville during the suppression. She remembered what happened when the firing stopped at the rue Haxo: "Lorsque le feu cessa à la rue Haxo, les officiers de Versailles criaient : « Jetez vos armes, il ne vous sera rien fait. » Quelques-uns confiants, allèrent à la mairie les déposer et ils ne revinrent pas !" ([Victorine Brocher, *Souvenirs d'une morte vivante*, 1909](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Souvenirs_d%E2%80%99une_morte_vivante/28)). She described an elderly householder shot in front of her, an eighteen-year-old killed after his mother pleaded for him, and escaped in boy's clothes. Her book appeared thirty-eight years after the events, and the killings she reports are her observations, not a Versailles record. Céleste Hardouin, held at Versailles and then at the Chantiers prison, described a dusty attic shared by roughly 400 women and 150 children, a tin of preserved meat and a litre of rice or beans divided among sixteen people, and one spoon for six. Those are her counts, not an administrative return. The imprisonment continued after the shooting ended.

Michel was tried by a Council of War, a military court, at Versailles on 18 December 1871. She refused the advocate assigned to her: "Je ne veux pas me défendre, je ne veux pas être défendue ; j'appartiens tout entière à la révolution sociale et je déclare accepter la responsabilité de tous mes actes." ([Statement to the 6th Council of War, 18 December 1871](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/M%C3%A9moires_de_Louise_Michel/Appendice_I)). The prosecution bulletin accused her of inciting the generals' killing and provoking the hostage murders. She admitted that she had welcomed the generals' deaths, and she claimed the burning of Paris as her own act: "Quant à l'incendie de Paris, oui, j'y ai participé. Je voulais opposer une barrière de flammes aux envahisseurs de Versailles. Je n'ai pas de complices pour ce fait, j'ai agi d'après mon propre mouvement." ([Same hearing, 18 December 1871](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/M%C3%A9moires_de_Louise_Michel/Appendice_I)). She denied that murder or arson had ever been discussed in the Commune Council, a denial her statement alone cannot settle. Of the dead she wrote: "Quinze mille environ, pendant la Semaine sanglante, soutinrent le choc d'une armée. On compta à peu près trente-cinq mille fusillés ; mais ceux qu'on ignore ?" ([*Mémoires de Louise Michel*, 1886](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/M%C3%A9moires_de_Louise_Michel/Texte_entier)). The thirty-five thousand is her estimate, published fifteen years later, not a count.

## What the pictures show

The election placard of 25 March is the form in which the central committee addressed the electorate: a large printed sheet. Charles Beauverie drew a barricade in the rue Lepic on 2 April, with a cannon, two insurgents and women and children behind paving stones. It carries that date, but there is no evidence he stood there. Hippolyte Vauvray's print of 1871, sold with a legal deposit stamp, showed soldiers waiting as clergy descended a prison stair, and its title called them "martyrs". The museum catalogue states that it is a photograph of a painting. Each image was made for a purpose: a summons to vote, an artist's rendering of a defence, a devotional story of a killing. None is a neutral record of a moment.

## How historians have read it

- **Robert Tombs, *The Paris Commune 1871*** (Longman, 1999; Routledge reissue, 2014). Treats the rising as the last of the European revolutionary cycle and argues that classic Marxist accounts contain myths, hindsight and polemical distortion, including treating measures that existed only on paper as applied. [Book record](https://books.google.be/books?id/pGTJAwAAQBAJ)
- **Jacques Rougerie, *Paris libre 1871*** (1971). Came to regard Marx as a partial but privileged witness, and disagrees with Tombs over how far the Marxist reading is distortion; his arrest and council figures are quoted by Kouvelakis.
- **Stathis Kouvelakis, "On the Paris Commune: Part 2"** (Verso, 31 March 2021). Reads the Commune as an "expansive political form", sets Rougerie's social data against Tombs and notes that even Tombs concedes the proportion of working-class leaders. [Article](https://www.versobooks.com/blogs/news/5042-on-the-paris-commune-part-2)
- **Édith Thomas, *Les Pétroleuses*** (1963). Studies the women accused of arson and the making of the "pétroleuse" figure; a way of testing Michel's and Brocher's self-presentation against the hostile press image.
- **Gay L. Gullickson, *Unruly Women of Paris: Images of the Commune*** (Cornell University Press, 1996). Examines the gendered imagery of the rising and of its repression, and pairs with Vauvray's print and the women's prison testimony.
- **Prosper-Olivier Lissagaray, *Histoire de la Commune de 1871*** (1876; revised editions 1886 and 1896). A Communard's own history, written close to the events and used as a source by later Marxists; participant historiography rather than a neutral account. [French text](<https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Histoire_de_la_Commune_de_1871_%28Lissagaray%29>)

Historians agree on the outline: the Commune grew out of national defeat and the siege, its council held an unusually high proportion of workers, and the repression after 21 May was severe and its scale contested. They disagree over whether Marx's phrase "working-class government" captures the event or imposes an interpretation on it, over how far the artisanal trades of many council members make a "dictatorship of the proletariat" reading anachronistic, and over whether the hostage policy was a calculated deterrent or a symptom of collapse. These positions are not equally supported. The arrest and council figures are empirical findings; the claim that Thiers deliberately sacrificed the hostages remains an argument resting on a polemical pamphlet.

## Limits of the record

The record is thin in particular places. No foreign correspondent's dispatch survives in it, and no Versailles military order, National Assembly debate or report by Galliffet; the repression is therefore seen chiefly through the people it struck and through interviews conducted decades later. No hostage and no member of Darboy's circle left a first-hand account here; Flotte reports what the Archbishop and Deguerry wrote in letters, but the letters themselves are not part of the record. The court deposition he cites, said to have been given by de Montaud to the 4th council of war, has not been located and cannot support a claim that Versailles provoked the hostage killings. Language is a barrier, since the richest texts are in French and the surviving transcriptions vary in reliability. The trial record and the 1897 inquiry in particular need checking against the scanned page images before their wording is treated as exact. Original copies of the *Journal officiel*, the archives of the Commune's police prefecture, the runs of *Le Cri du peuple* and *Le Père Duchêne*, and the Carnavalet photograph albums would all have to be consulted in person. The surviving voices are uneven: journalists, council members and schoolteachers, overwhelmingly Parisian, and mostly written by those who won the later argument over what the Commune meant. The dead, people in the countryside and ordinary rank-and-file members speak mainly through other people's records. Even the most vivid scene was written between 1879 and 1909, so it is right to say that Michel recalled or Brocher described, not that Paris was.
