In June 1936, with a socialist government in power in France, metalworkers in Paris occupied their factories rather than merely walking out. Simone Weil, a philosopher who had worked in Renault and Alsthom plants in 1934 and 1935, wrote about the strike wave from the workers' side under the pen name S. Galois. She rejected the two conspiracy theories then current, that Communists had provoked the occupations to embarrass the government and that employers had staged them, and said no provocation was needed. What she recorded was a change in the balance of feeling on the shop floor. "Indépendamment des revendications, cette grève est en elle-même une joie. Une joie pure. Une joie sans mélange." (Simone Weil, "La vie et la grève des ouvrières métallos", 10 June 1936; [Source: fr.wikisource.org](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/La_Condition_ouvri%C3%A8re/09)) The strike was a joy in itself, quite apart from the demands. Weil had left wage work by then and wrote as a sympathetic observer of women metalworkers, not as one of them, but her account supplies the grievance that the official settlement does not: the pace of the line, the threat of dismissal, hunger, an unheated changing room, and the sense that with a socialist in power a worker was no longer automatically the weaker party before his employer.

The Popular Front was the coalition that had brought that government to office. It joined socialists, Communists, radicals and liberals against fascism, the far-right movement that had taken power in Italy and Germany and promised to destroy left-wing politics by force. In France the coalition won the election of 1936, and workers answered with one of the largest strike waves in the country's history up to that date.

## The turn and the minimum programme

The new line had been stated publicly a year earlier. On 2 August 1935 Georgi Dimitrov, recently released from Nazi custody after the Leipzig trial and by then a leading official of the Communist International, the body that coordinated communist parties across countries, addressed its Seventh Congress. He defined the enemy in economic terms: "Fascism is the power of finance capital itself. It is the organization of terrorist vengeance against the working class and the revolutionary section of the peasantry and intelligentsia." (Georgi Dimitrov, report to the Seventh Comintern Congress, 2 August 1935, translated from the Sofia Press *Selected Works* edition, 1972; [Marxists Internet Archive: 08 02](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/dimitrov/works/1935/08_02.htm)) He blamed the Social Democrats' willingness to cooperate with capitalist parties for the defeats in Germany, Austria and Spain, and called for a "united front" and an "anti-fascist people's front", including a section headed "A United Front government". His insistence that the road could be blocked was a claim about political will: "Yes, comrades, the road to fascism can be blocked. It is quite possible. It depends on ourselves--on the workers, the peasants and all working people." (same report and translation; [Marxists Internet Archive: 08 02](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/dimitrov/works/1935/08_02.htm)) This was doctrine announced from a congress platform, not a record of private instructions to the French or Spanish parties.

What such a front meant in practice can be read in the Spanish pact of 16 January 1936, signed by republican parties, the Socialist Party with its trade union UGT, the Communist Party, a syndicalist party and the POUM. Its promises were amnesty for political offences since November 1933, reinstatement of dismissed workers, compensation for victims of repression, restored constitutional guarantees, agrarian measures, public works and labour courts. The signatories agreed "sin perjuicio de dejar a salvo los postulados de sus doctrinas, han llegado ha comprometer un plan político común" ("without prejudice to preserving the tenets of their doctrines, they have agreed a common political plan"; the "ha" is in the transcription). (Spanish Popular Front pact, 16 January 1936; [Source: fuenterrebollo.com](http://www.fuenterrebollo.com/Gobiernos/Politica/frente-popular.html)) The republican parties expressly refused the Socialist demands for nationalisation of land with free distribution to peasants, workers' control and nationalisation of the banks. This was a defence coalition with a minimum programme, not an agreed road to socialism.

## The occupations and the Matignon bargain

By early June the French occupations had forced employers and the new government to negotiate. The agreement concluded at Matignon on 7 June 1936 between the CGPF, the employers' federation, and the CGT, the main trade union federation, under the presidency of Léon Blum, conceded immediate collective contracts, recognition of union membership and opinion, wage increases of 7 to 15 per cent within a ceiling on each establishment's wage bill, and workers' delegates in firms employing more than ten people. Its sixth article bound the employers thus: "La délégation patronale s'engage à ce qu'il ne soit pris aucune sanction pour faits de grève." (Matignon Agreements, article 6, 7 June 1936; [Source: institutions-professionnelles.fr](https://institutions-professionnelles.fr/reperes/documents/137-1936-accords-de-matignon-dialogue-social)) The employers undertook to take no sanctions for strike action. In return the CGT delegation promised to ask strikers to return to work once firms accepted the agreement and talks on applying it began. The newspaper *La Croix* printed the full text the next morning, including eligibility rules for delegates and the interior minister's promise of bills on collective contracts, paid leave and the forty-hour week.

The settlement was a bargain between organisations at the top, not a transfer of control over production. Weil noted that workers had enjoyed the occupation itself while leaving the drafting of demands to militants, and she warned that a minimum wage could be offset by faster work and dismissal. The later history of the Front, including the "social pause" and the fall of Blum's government, lies outside the documents considered here; the agreement itself shows what was conceded and what was promised in exchange.

## The trials reach the west

While the fronts were being built, news arrived from Moscow. The trials of leading Soviet communists, beginning in 1936, presented confessions to charges of conspiracy and sabotage, and they divided western leftists who had looked to the Soviet Union for leadership. André Gide, a French writer who had travelled with official guides on a curated tour from June to August 1936, described the closure of Soviet public life from inside the experience: "En U.R.S.S. il est admis d'avance et une fois pour toutes que, sur tout et n'importe quoi, il ne saurait y avoir plus d'une opinion." (André Gide, *Retour de l'U.R.S.S.*, November 1936; [Source: litteraturefrancaise.net](https://litteraturefrancaise.net/fr/oeuvre/andre-gide-retour-de-lurss/)) He left before the August trial and wrote as a former admirer, not as a neutral observer, but his book became a reference point for those who doubted the official account.

Leon Trotsky, a defendant in absentia in the Moscow trials, gave evidence in Mexico to a commission set up by the American Committee for the Defense of Leon Trotsky. His opening statement refused to ask for trust in advance: "I do not demand any a priori confidence in my affirmations. The task of this Commission of investigation is to verify everything from the beginning to the end." (Leon Trotsky, opening statement, 10 April 1937; [Marxists Internet Archive: session01](https://www.marxists.org/archive/trotsky/1937/dewey/session01.htm)) The sub-commission, chaired by the philosopher John Dewey, held thirteen hearings from 10 to 17 April 1937 in Diego Rivera's house at Coyoacán, with about fifty people present, nearly half of them foreign correspondents. Its final findings, signed on 21 September 1937, were blunt: "We therefore find the Moscow trials to be frame-ups. We therefore find Trotsky and Sedov not guilty." (Dewey Commission, *Not Guilty*, 21 September 1937; [Marxists Internet Archive: index](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/document/dewey-report/index.htm)) One commissioner, Carleton Beals, resigned and publicly impugned his colleagues, a dispute the report prints in detail. The commission ruled on the testimony implicating Trotsky and said it could not determine guilt on other charges; it was a politically engaged body working from Soviet-published trial records, not a court.

The same language of accusation crossed into Spain. In *Homage to Catalonia* Orwell described how Communists he called "the official Communist Party" used "Trotskyism", a term for supporters of Trotsky's revolutionary opposition, as a charge of murder and treachery. He set out the strategic disagreement in a single sentence: "The Communists hold that Fascism can be beaten by alliance with sections of the capitalist class (the Popular Front); their opponents hold that this manoeuvre simply gives Fascism new breeding-grounds." (George Orwell, *Homage to Catalonia*, 1938; [Source: orwell.ru](https://www.orwell.ru/library/novels/Homage_to_Catalonia/english/e_htc)) That was a participant's inference about Soviet pressure, which he noted was denied at the time, but the accusatory machinery he described was real and reached the POUM, the party he had fought alongside.

## How historians have read it

- **Julian Jackson, *The Popular Front in France* (first published 1988).** The standard political history of the 1934–38 coalition; reads the Front as a defensive anti-fascist alliance rather than a revolutionary government, a view consistent with the Matignon bargain and the minimum programme. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL4970752W)
- **Stéphane Sirot, "'« Pain, paix, liberté »: tout le programme du Front populaire", *L'Humanité*, 30 June 2024.** Argues from the programme's wording that it reflected earlier trade-union demands rather than being only a deal between party headquarters, qualifying a purely top-down reading of 1936. [Source: humanite.fr](https://www.humanite.fr/histoire/front-populaire/pain-paix-liberte-tout-le-programme-du-front-populaire)
- **Robert Conquest, *The Great Terror* (first published 1968).** The classic account of the purges as centrally directed repression resting on fabricated confessions; broadly agrees with the Dewey Commission on the falsity of the trial testimony while writing in a different Cold War framework. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL29408W)
- **J. Arch Getty, *Origins of the Great Purges* (1985).** A revisionist account that puts more weight on institutional conditions and less on a single personal design, the main live interpretive disagreement with Conquest's intentionalist framing. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL18280112W)
- **Paul Preston, *The Spanish Civil War: Reaction, Revolution and Revenge* (2006).** A broad narrative that frames Orwell's testimony without treating the memoir as the whole of Spanish events, and bridges this chapter to the Spanish Civil War chapter. [Source: openlibrary.org](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL258233W)

Broad agreement holds that the Popular Front was a defensive coalition against fascism with a limited social programme, and that the Moscow confessions implicating Trotsky were false. Live disagreement concerns how much of the purges was personally directed by Stalin and how far the French and Spanish fronts took orders from Moscow.

## What the record cannot show

There is no first-hand voice here of a rank-and-file French striker other than Weil, a former factory worker writing as a sympathetic visitor, and no opened Comintern correspondence, French or Spanish police file, or full Soviet trial transcript. The trial record appears only as quoted by the commission and by later writers. Nothing in these sources measures how many western leftists changed their minds; that would need sales figures and a separate, properly sourced treatment. The full French programme text and Gide's fuller books were not available, so his words are confined here to one verified excerpt, and the French and Spanish documents are quoted in the original rather than in a verified English translation. The material is also unevenly drawn from prominent figures. Dimitrov, Trotsky, Gide, Dewey's commissioners and Orwell all wrote from positions inside the conflicts they describe; the commission was initiated by a defence committee, and the pro-Stalin case is the thinnest part of the record. Ordinary party members, strikers outside Weil's account, and the families of those accused in Moscow are silent. The figure of roughly 12,000 strikes, half in the Paris region, comes from an institutional history of the French National Assembly rather than from a contemporary strike census. The documents support influence and a shared vocabulary between Moscow and the western fronts; they do not establish a single chain of command.
