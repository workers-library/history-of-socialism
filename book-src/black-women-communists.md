Claudia Jones, Angela Davis and Assata Shakur wrote in different decades and from different organizations. Jones published her central essay on Black women in the Communist Party's theoretical journal in 1949 ([*Political Affairs*, June 1949](https://www.marxists.org/history/usa/pubs/political-affairs/1949-06v28n6-political-affairs.pdf)), then wrote about peace, imprisonment and the Caribbean diaspora until her death in 1964 ([MIA Claudia Jones archive](https://www.marxists.org/archive/jones-claudia/index.htm)). Davis wrote an autobiography in 1974 ([*Angela Davis: An Autobiography*](https://archive.org/details/angeladavisanaut0000davi_c3e7)), edited a collection on political prisoners in 1971 ([*If They Come in the Morning*](https://attachments.are.na/7908851/2bf9ee771eec90d3c7e99335a9e8eeb2.pdf?1594068994=)) and developed an analysis of housework and imprisonment over the following three decades ([*Women, Race and Class*](https://www.marxists.org/subject/women/authors/davis-angela/housework.htm); [*Are Prisons Obsolete?*](https://archive.org/download/ArePrisonsObsolete/Are%20Prisons%20Obsolete%20by%20Angela%20Y.%20Davis_djvu.txt)). Shakur published an autobiography in 1987 ([*Assata*](https://archive.org/download/Assata/assata%20shakur%20an%20biography_djvu.txt)) and a series of letters from exile in the 1990s and 2010s ([1998 letter](https://notmytribe.com/solidarity/our-resources/assata-shakur-letter-to-the-pope); [2014 letter](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/socialist-viewpoint-us/janfeb_15/janfeb_15_36.html)). Reading them together means comparing arguments made in distinct settings, not smoothing those settings away.

Each woman is a witness to her own life, and each account has a purpose. Jones's 1953 court statement is a declaration of principle addressed to a judge who had already sentenced her; her 1956 prison account was written for a party newspaper after her deportation. Davis's autobiography is a retrospective book, her prison interviews are political statements made while she was in custody, and her 1998 television interview looks back over twenty-five years. Shakur's account of a 1973 traffic stop was written more than a decade later and is a work of political autobiography, not a police report. Where a court record or a contemporary report exists, it is set beside that account. Donald Hines, who worked with Jones in London, is the one participant writing about someone else's life, and his recollections were published in 2008, more than forty years after the events he describes.

## Claudia Jones, 1915 to 1964

### A journalist in the Communist Party

Claudia Jones was born in Port of Spain, Trinidad, on 21 February 1915 and joined her parents in Harlem in 1924. She entered the Communist Party in 1936, partly in response to its defence of the Scottsboro defendants, and by 1937 was on the editorial staff of the *Daily Worker*. These details come from a [participant account by Donald Hines](https://www.marxists.org/history/erol/uk.secondwave/jones-gazette.pdf), first published in *Race & Class* in 2008. Hines worked with Jones at the *West Indian Gazette* and writes as a colleague and admirer. His chronology is the fullest available, but his purpose was to recover a neglected figure, not to provide a detached assessment.

The [MIA Claudia Jones archive](https://www.marxists.org/archive/jones-claudia/index.htm) preserves her bylines and makes the shape of her early work visible. She wrote on NAACP youth delegates in July 1937, on "Negro Youth Pamphlet" in June 1938, and on the party's youth leadership in a piece titled "Young Communist Leader Lauds Workers School Value as Educator" in January 1941. Her 1942 title was "Lift Every Voice - For Victory!" In 1940 she published the pamphlet "Jim-Crow in Uniform." The bylines show her working on two fronts from the start: defending Black youth against discrimination and building the organizational life of the party and its youth league.

By the mid-1940s she was writing on what the party called the Negro Question. A discussion article on the draft resolution appeared in *Political Affairs* in August 1945, and in January 1946 she published "On the Right to Self-Determination for the Negro People in the Black Belt" in the same journal. Her 1946 *Daily Worker* articles covered the filibuster against fair employment legislation, a CIO packing-house campaign against Jim Crow, and mass action against lynching. The theoretical argument was attached to specific campaigns from the beginning.

The turn to Black women as a distinct subject came in 1948 and 1949. "For New Approaches to Our Work among Women" appeared in *Political Affairs* in August 1948. The landmark "An End to the Neglect of the Problems of the Negro Woman!" appeared in the same journal in June 1949, volume 28, number 6, on pages 51 to 67. "We Seek Full Equality for Women" followed in the *Daily Worker* on 4 September 1949, and a notice titled "A Correction" on 18 September dealt with political errors in the editing of that article. Jones was a working journalist whose texts were contested even inside her own paper.

### The 1949 argument

"An End to the Neglect" opens by describing "the gross neglect of the special problems of Negro women" in the labour movement, among left-wing progressives and inside the Communist Party itself. Jones's claim is not that Black women's militancy derives from someone else's. She writes that "once Negro women undertake action, the militancy of the whole Negro people, and thus of the anti-imperialist coalition, is greatly enhanced." The argument turns the party's assumptions around: the women it had treated as a supplementary constituency were, in her account, the force that could transform the whole movement.

The theoretical core is an account in which race, class and gender combine rather than sit side by side. Jones writes of "the special oppression she faces as Negro, as woman, and as worker," and states the structural position plainly: "Negro women, as workers, as Negroes, and as women, are the most oppressed stratum of the whole population." Considered as a worker, a Black woman was confined to the lowest-paid jobs, with domestic service as the great reservoir. Considered as Black, she faced the "white chauvinist stereotype as to where her place should be," upheld by film, radio and the press. Considered as a woman, she was measured against the "traditional 'mammy'" who puts the care of other people's families above her own. Jones insisted that these could not be ranked in a hierarchy, because each reinforced the others.

Her term for the mechanism was "super-exploitation." By this she meant that Black women were paid less and worked under worse conditions than other workers because they were Black and because they were women. "Not equality, but degradation and super-exploitation," she wrote, "this is the actual lot of Negro women!" The demonstration was empirical. Drawing on Women's Bureau bulletins, she reported that "in 1940, two out of every five Negro women, in contrast to two out of every eight white women, worked for a living," and that "the majority of Negro women get less than half the pay of white women." She connected wages to family health and to the death rates of Black mothers and children. The three forms of oppression were not a matter of identity alone; they arose from the way work was organized and from the ideas used to justify that arrangement.

Two scholarly readings of this essay show how historians disagree about its place. Denise Lynn, a historian of women in the American Communist Party and author of *Claudia Jones: Visions of a Socialist America* (Polity Press, 2024), notes in an [essay for the African American Intellectual History Society](https://www.aaihs.org/claudia-jones-and-ending-the-neglect-of-black-women) that Erik S. McDuffie credits the communist Louise Thompson with coining the phrase "triple oppression." Carole Boyce Davies, in *Left of Karl Marx: The Political Life of Black Communist Claudia Jones* (Duke University Press, 2008), argues instead that Jones popularized the idea and synthesized arguments she had been making for years. Davies's book, which won the 2008 Letitia Woods Brown Memorial Book Award, places Jones within Caribbean intellectual history, Black American feminism and the history of communism, and contrasts Jones's own narration of her life with the account in her FBI file. Lynn's reading emphasizes that Jones's framework was an emancipatory politics for everyone: if the most oppressed could be freed, the claim followed, then everyone could be.

Jones also reversed the order in which the party discussed the woman question and the national question. "the Negro question in the United States is prior to, and not equal to, the woman question," she wrote, because only by fighting chauvinism and winning full equality for Black people could women as a whole advance. A broad women's movement that left racial inequality untouched would not do. That claim was controversial inside the party, and Lynn treats it as the essay's most contested passage and the one on which its argument turns. The essay closes by naming socialism as "the historic mission" of the working class and "the achievement of a Socialist America, the final and full guarantee of woman's emancipation" (p. 64).

Jones then turned the analysis into a demand addressed to her own party. "Negro Communist women must everywhere now take their rightful place in Party leadership on all levels," she wrote (p. 66), criticizing the practice of treating women who were already leaders in their churches and communities as recruits who needed to get their feet wet. Theory in her practice was a weapon for reorganizing the institution that claimed to represent the constituency.

### Self-determination, peace and repression

Jones's analysis of Black women's oppression was never separate from her analysis of the national question. The 1946 self-determination article and the 1949 essay belong together: liberation for Black women could not be won inside a women's movement that left the national question intact, nor inside a Black movement that left the gender question intact. Her 1950 and 1951 articles on women and peace added a third term. "International Women's Day and the Struggle for Peace" appeared in *Political Affairs* in March 1950, followed by "For the Unity of Women in the Cause of Peace!" in February 1951 and "The Struggle for Peace in the United States" in February 1952. Her regular *Sunday Worker* column, "Half the World," ran from March 1950 through August 1951. The title was itself an argument: a peace movement that ignored women could not claim to be a mass movement.

Her 1953 "Statement in Court," reprinted in *13 Communists Speak to the Court*, shows how she joined the 1949 essay to the prosecution. She told the judge that she spoke "not with any idea that what I say will influence your sentence of me," because to be a communist and an officer of the party was, in her account, the only act to which she pleaded guilty and was "no crime" under the court's own prior rulings. She described the verdict as a "framed-up verdict on a framed-up indictment and trial," and returned to the treatment of her own journalism: "You dare not, gentlemen of the prosecution, assert that Negro women can think and speak and write!" The connection between the essay and the prosecution is made by Jones herself; it is her account of why the article mattered, not an independent finding about the judge's motives.

Her February 1956 *Daily Worker* account describes imprisonment after deportation. She writes from "a London flat one month after my forcible ejection by political deportation from the United States," and recalls travelling under guard from Grand Central with Elizabeth Gurley Flynn and Betty Gannett. She describes the loss of a surname into a number, the "orientation" cottage where prisoners were isolated and set to scrubbing already shining floors, and the racial order of the institution: "'White' Cottage and 'Colored' cottages were the norm at Alderson." The account is detailed and specific about the regime she experienced. Its limits are those of prison testimony written for a political purpose after the event; it records what she saw and how she understood it, and it is not a complete description of the prison.

### London and the carnival

Deported in 1955, Jones arrived in London in December and founded the *West Indian Gazette* in 1958. The first Caribbean-style carnival in Britain was held at St Pancras Town Hall on 31 January 1959, with five more annual carnivals up to 1964. These facts come from Hines, who worked with her on the paper. He describes the paper's function after the 1958 Notting Hill and Nottingham racial attacks: "Into this world was the West Indian Gazette born, like the goddess of mythology, fully armed ready for battle." On the origin of the carnival he recalls: "It was December of 1958. Everybody laughed, and then Claudia called us to order. 'Why not?' she asked. 'Could it not be held in a hall, somewhere?' Yes it could, and it was held in St Pancras town hall in January 1959. The BBC televised it."

Hines is careful on attribution. He rejects the simple claim that Jones started the Notting Hill Carnival, while insisting on the precedent: "But it seems incredible that those who took carnival on to the streets of Notting Hill in the late 1960s were unaware of the six carnivals between 1959 and 1964." The surprise is Hines's, and it is directed at later people's ignorance. The precise claim is narrower than a founding myth. Jones created a Caribbean carnival tradition in London out of which the later street festival grew. Her late writing kept the theoretical thread intact. "American Imperialism and the British West Indies" appeared in *Political Affairs* in April 1958, and "The Caribbean Community in Britain" in *Freedomways* in Summer 1964, pages 341 to 357. The first treated the Caribbean as an object of United States power; the second treated the diaspora forming on British soil as a new political subject. She died in London in December 1964.

The term "intersectionality" was coined by the legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex," published in the *University of Chicago Legal Forum* in 1989. Jones's 1949 essay came four decades earlier. It is reasonable to say that she anticipated a vocabulary later used for the combination of race, class and gender, as the [libcom edition of the essay](https://libcom.org/article/end-neglect-problems-negro-woman) does. It is not accurate to describe her as an intersectional theorist in the contemporary sense, and the comparison is most useful when it highlights what her Marxist account added: a material explanation of why the three forms of oppression combine, rooted in wages, domestic work and the stereotypes that legitimate them.

## Angela Y. Davis, 1944 to the present

### Witnesses and chronology

Davis's life is documented in her own *Autobiography* (1974) and in a campaign biography prepared by the National United Committee to Free Angela Davis in November 1970 and reprinted in *If They Come in the Morning* (1971). The second source is a political document produced by the organization Davis helped name; it describes a childhood in Birmingham under "George Wallace and Bull Connor, the burning cross of the old South, the electric cattle prods of the new," and records that the family lived on "Dynamite Hill." Its purpose was to build a defence campaign, and it selects details accordingly. The autobiography is retrospective and shaped as a political formation narrative. Where the two sources overlap, they broadly agree; where they differ, the autobiography is the fuller account.

Davis was born in Birmingham in 1944. At fifteen she left for a high school in New York on a Quaker scholarship, then won a scholarship to Brandeis, where she first studied French literature. Her own account records two distinct periods in Paris. In the summer of 1962 she attended the Eighth World Youth Festival in Helsinki and then travelled to Paris, where she encountered the racial politics of the Algerian war: "To be an Algerian living in Paris in 1962 was to be a hunted human being." She describes racist graffiti, bombings in cafés frequented by North Africans and a police assault on a demonstration near the Sorbonne. Later, during her junior year, she returned to Paris with her Brandeis group and studied French literature at the Sorbonne from November. The two periods explain two statements that can otherwise look contradictory: her reference to "Paris in 1962" in a prison interview and the account of her junior year in the autobiography. In her final year at Brandeis she began philosophical study with Herbert Marcuse. She went to Frankfurt for graduate work, returned to the United States to complete her doctorate under Marcuse at the University of California, San Diego, and joined the Communist Party and the Che-Lumumba Club, an all-Black party collective in Los Angeles. In autumn 1969 she was appointed to a philosophy professorship at UCLA.

### How she understood her own radicalization

Davis gives an unusually precise account of the 16th Street Baptist Church bombing of September 1963. She hears of it at Brandeis while buying a newspaper between classes: "At first I was only vaguely aware of the words. Then it hit me! It came crashing down all around me. Birmingham. 16th Street Baptist Church. The names." She names two of the four girls, Carole and Cynthia, as friends, and describes her anger at the response around her: the faces of other students "were closed," and they tried to console her "as if friends had just been killed in a plane crash." The campaign biography adds that people in Birmingham knew who the bombers were and that no arrests followed. These passages establish how she experienced the event. They do not by themselves establish the legal or investigative history of the bombing.

The earlier Birmingham of the 1949 house bombing is described with equal precision. She was washing her shoelaces for Sunday School when "an explosion a hundred times louder than the loudest, most frightening thunderclap I had ever heard shook our house." The point of these memories is not anecdotal. In her telling, the bombing campaign made her parents more determined that she understand racial violence as a historical system rather than "the nature of things."

Her fullest explanation of why she joined the Communist Party comes from the prison interviews. "My decision to join the Communist Party emanated from my belief that the only true path of liberation for Black people is the one that leads toward a complete and total overthrow of the capitalist class in this country and all its manifold institutional appendages," she said. She joined the Che-Lumumba Club because it was "committed to the task of rendering Marxism-Leninism relevant to Black people." On how she linked the struggle at home to struggles abroad, she said that "one can't be a true revolutionary without being cognizant of the need to link up with forces all over the world battling with imperialism," and that her party membership was "predicated in part on the ties the party has established with revolutionary movements throughout the world." In the same interview she rejected the assumption that white workers were permanently conservative: "Black leadership in working-class struggles is needed to radicalize necessary sectors of the working class."

The prison interviews also record what she had learned in Cuba, which she visited in 1969. Comparing Cuban and United States racism, she said that "Learning from the Cuban variety of prerevolutionary racism... it became obvious that we would have to wage a relentless battle against racism at all times and on all levels." Her autobiography describes that 1969 trip as one "which had proved to me what socialism can do to eradicate racism." The claim is a political interpretation drawn from a visit, not a comparative study.

### Arrest, trial and the campaign

Davis went underground in 1970 after guns bought in her name were used in an attempt to free George Jackson from a California courtroom. She was arrested in New York on 13 October and held in California on charges of murder, kidnapping and conspiracy. Her account of the first night in the Women's House of Detention describes hearing supporters outside: "Sure enough, muffled rhythms were penetrating these massive walls... they were chanting, 'Free Angela Davis.'" She describes the cell in 4b as "no more than four and a half feet wide," with an iron cot bolted to the floor and a seatless toilet. She understood the stakes: "A conviction on any one of these charges could mean death in the gas chamber." In the prison interviews she argued that the court was "increasingly becoming a powerful instrument of repression," and that "an exclusively legalistic approach to my defense would be fatal." Her "Statement to the Court" made the political character of the charge explicit: "According to the indictment, one of the overt acts in the conspiracy with which I am charged consists in my advocacy of the liberation of the Soledad Brothers." Her advocacy was treated as motive for a crime.

The international campaign that formed around her case has been studied by historians who were not participants. Daniel Rosenberg's articles "The Free Angela Movement in the United States, 1969–1991" (*American Communist History*, 2020) and "The Free Angela Movement in Global Context, 1970–1972" (same journal, 2021) trace the campaign's organization across borders. Maxim Matusevich's study "'You Are Not Alone': Angela Davis and the Soviet Dreams of Freedom" (*International Review of Social History*, 2024) shows that the official Soviet campaign did not exhaust what the case meant to Soviet citizens; dissidents and ordinary readers used her image for their own purposes. Dan Berger's essay "Thinking Black against the Carceral State" (2017), written for the African American Intellectual History Society, argues that prisoner defence campaigns of this kind are a foundational part of broader freedom struggles and notes that the demand was always "Free Angela Davis and All Political Prisoners," not simply free her.

Davis made the same point herself. She received "from 100 to 400 letters a day, at least half of them originating from abroad," including many from schoolchildren in the German Democratic Republic. But, she said, "it is all the more important that this campaign be extended, that its limits be extended to become a fight to release all political prisoners within this country." In a 1998 interview she recalled insisting that the committee be named the National United Committee to Free Angela Davis and All Political Prisoners, and that after the trial it became the National Alliance against Racist and Political Repression. The change widened a campaign for one defendant into a permanent organization against political repression. Asked whether she felt guilty about becoming a cause célèbre, she answered: "So rather than feeling guilty is what I have done is to continue the work." She was acquitted on all charges in June 1972 ([*New York Times*, 5 June 1972](https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/books/98/03/08/home/davis-acquit.html)).

### From the prison collection to abolition

*If They Come in the Morning: Voices of Resistance* was published in 1971 by The Third Press, edited by Davis and Bettina Aptheker with other members of the National United Committee. Its preface opens: "Political repression in the United States has reached monstrous proportions." The volume collected writing by Davis, Aptheker and imprisoned Black and Brown activists, was translated into several languages and served the campaign then under way. Alyosha Goldstein, writing in *American Quarterly* in 2023, places it among the books that galvanized the modern abolition movement, alongside the 1976 *Instead of Prisons* handbook. Julian Bond's foreword linked the book to an older tradition of political defence, including Du Bois's 1951 trial. The book's importance lies in that documented use; it is not necessary to rank it against other works.

In *Women, Race and Class* (1981), Davis returned to the question Jones had raised in 1949, working through a different object. The chapter on housework observes that it is "virtually invisible" and argues that "the socialisation of housework, including meal preparation and child care, presupposes an end to the profit-motive's reign over the economy." She grounds the argument in the history of Black women's paid labour: "In 1910, when over half of all Black females were working outside their homes, one-third of them were employed as paid domestic workers. By 1920 over one-half were domestic servants, and in 1930 the proportion had risen to three out of five." Like Jones, she refused to treat gender apart from race and class, though she worked through paid and unpaid domestic work rather than the party's organizational neglect.

Her later prison abolitionism developed the same method. In *Are Prisons Obsolete?* (2003) she describes the "prison industrial complex," so called because "prison building and operation began to attract vast amounts of capital... in a way that recalled the emergence of the military industrial complex." She argues that the prison "functions ideologically as an abstract site into which undesirables are deposited, relieving us of the responsibility of thinking about the real issues afflicting those communities from which prisoners are drawn in such disproportionate numbers." The claim about what the prison does to public thinking connects her later work to the 1949 analysis of racial ideology. Historians of abolition place this argument in a longer movement. Ruth Wilson Gilmore's *Golden Gulag* (University of California Press, 2007) explains the growth of California's prison system through surpluses of capital, labour and land, and Gilmore was a co-founder with Davis of the organization Critical Resistance in the 1990s. Davis's own contribution was to join that analysis to a critique of race and gender.

In the 1998 interview she used a term her earlier work had not used. "in the latter 1990s we have to recognize the intersectionality, the interconnectedness of all of these institutions and attitudes," she said, in a passage about the failure of Black communities to take progressive positions on gender and sexuality. The word is hers in that statement; there is no need to claim it as a term she later adopted. The same interview returns to the relation between political campaigns and daily life. On the Black Panther Party, she describes the debate as one about "whether they should become active in campaigns against police violence, for example, or whether they should focus their energy on wearing African clothes and changing their name and developing rituals," the latter being the cultural nationalism she distinguished from revolutionary internationalism, meaning a politics organized around police campaigns and international alliances rather than dress, names and ritual. On coalition she said: "We need to bring black communities, Chicano communities, Puerto Rican communities, Asian American communities together."

## Assata Shakur, 1947 to the present

### Reading her own account

Shakur's published writing is the starting point for her political ideas and for much of the narrative of her life. It is also a body of self-representation produced by a person who understood herself to be a political prisoner in exile. Her 2014 open letter describes a life of activism from the 1960s: "I participated in various struggles: the Black liberation movement, the student rights movement, and the movement to end the war in Vietnam. I joined the Black Panther Party." The 1998 letter to Pope John Paul II gives the same sequence in different words. These are retrospective summaries, consistent with each other and with the broad chronology of the period, but they are not independent confirmation of each other. For the institutional history of the party she joined, Joshua Bloom and Waldo E. Martin's *Black against Empire* (University of California Press, 2013) remains the standard scholarly account, tracing both the party's rapid growth under police repression and the federal counterintelligence campaign against it.

Her account of the Black Liberation Army is similarly deliberate. "The Black Liberation Army is not an organization: it goes beyond that. It is a concept, a people's movement, an idea," she wrote in her 1977 court statement, reproduced in *Assata*. That statement also gives the social explanation: "The idea of a Black Liberation Army emerged from conditions in Black communities: conditions of poverty, indecent housing, massive unemployment, poor medical care, and inferior education." In the same passage she wrote that "ninety percent of the men and women in this country's prisons are Black and Third World." The figure was part of an argument about why resistance arose; she supplied no source for it, and it should not be repeated as a finding. Her best-known claim about political formation is that "Black revolutionaries do not drop from the moon. We are created by our conditions. Shaped by our oppression." Images of Attica, San Quentin, Bedford Hills, Leavenworth and Sing Sing follow, naming the prisons that made the idea concrete for her.

Scholars have treated this writing as a political text with its own conventions. Joy James's essay "Framing the Panther: Assata Shakur and Black Female Agency" (2009) argues that Shakur's memoir and interviews link her to the underground Black Liberation Army more than to the Panther Party as it became a cultural commodity, and that she is easily romanticized or demonized. Anna Hinton's peer-reviewed article "A War of Minds Waged Against Bodies: The Political Activist as Prisoner and Patient" (*Anthurium*, 2019) reads the same memoir as a Black disability studies text, arguing that Shakur uses her injured body to bear witness to state violence. Philip V. McHarris's "Assata Shakur and the Counterinsurgency of Law" (*Columbia Journal of Race and Law*, 2026) treats the courtroom and pretrial detention as parts of a legal architecture of control and reads her flight to Cuba as a form of marronage, or flight from enslavement. Sam C. Tenorio's "Assata's escape as disincarceral practice" (*Cultural Dynamics*, 2020) makes a related argument about movement and confinement. These studies disagree about emphasis, but all treat the autobiography as an argument rather than a neutral record.

### The 1973 stop and what the sources establish

The opening of *Assata* is a first-person account of the traffic stop on the New Jersey Turnpike on 2 May 1973. Shakur, Zayd Malik Shakur and Sundiata Acoli were stopped, and the encounter ended with Zayd Shakur and State Trooper Werner Foerster dead, Acoli and Shakur wounded, and Trooper James Harper injured. These bare facts are documented outside her memoir. A [2023 review of the case in *Gothamist*](https://gothamist.com/news/50-years-after-shootout-killed-nj-cop-black-power-folk-hero-assata-shakur-has-a-complex-legacy) notes that how the stop began is not in dispute: the car was pulled over for a broken taillight and shots were fired. The federal appellate opinion in *New Jersey v. Chesimard*, 555 F.2d 63 (3d Cir. 1977), records that she had been indicted on two counts of murder, one of armed robbery, one of illegal weapons possession and four counts of assault on police officers, and that her pretrial motions included a claim to observe the Muslim Sabbath on Fridays. A [University of Virginia law library account](https://archives.law.virginia.edu/dengrove/writeup/black-liberation-army-member-assata-shakur-tried-murder-and-assault-charges) records that a jury convicted her on 25 March 1977 and that she received the maximum sentence.

What rests on Shakur's account alone is a different set of claims. She describes being dragged from the car and kicked, and a trooper threatening to shoot her: "'Bitch, you'd better open your goddamn mouth or I'll blow your goddamn head off!'" (the transcription is damaged at "stom-ach," where the word is plainly "stomach"). In her 2014 letter she gives her version of the shooting: she says she was shot with her hands in the air, and that Trooper Harper admitted killing Zayd Malik Shakur. She describes the trial as one in which "Sundiata Acoli nor I ever received a fair trial," says the news media had convicted them beforehand, and calls the 1977 proceeding "a legal lynching." She reports more than two years in solitary confinement, threats against her lawyers and an all-white jury.

These claims are not equivalent in status. The stop, the deaths, the charges, the 1977 conviction and the sentence are documented in court records and contemporary reporting. Whether she fired first, and whether the prosecution was deliberately murderous, remain contested. Her account is a witness statement with a clear political purpose and a known interest in the outcome; the fact that FBI counterintelligence programs targeted Black organizations, established in David Cunningham's *There's Something Happening Here* (University of California Press, 2004) and other studies, makes her suspicion of the state intelligible without settling the facts of the turnpike encounter. "Legal lynching" is her characterization of the trial, not a finding of fact. The analysis matters because treating her testimony as self-evident would repeat the error of dismissing it outright.

### The court statement, the poems and exile

The closing movement of her 1977 court statement is at once a political program and a poem, and it is the source of the text later circulated as "Assata": "It is our duty to fight for our freedom. / It is our duty to win. / We must love each other and support each other. / We have nothing to lose but our chains." In the autobiography these lines belong to the court statement rather than appearing under a separate poem heading. Immediately before them she addresses Black people who might find themselves on the turnpike, which she calls "a checkpoint where Black people are stopped, searched, harassed, and assaulted." The poem "Affirmation," printed near the opening of the book, moves from ordinary images of sunshine and tricycles to the claim that "a wall is just a wall / and nothing more at all. / It can be broken down." Others are shorter. "LOVE" runs to three lines: "Love is contraband in Hell, / cause love is an acid / that eats away bars." "THE TRADITION" repeats a refrain about carrying on.

In 1979 she escaped from prison and eventually reached Cuba, where she received asylum. Her letters give the political meaning she attached to the move. "I advocate self-determination for my people and for all oppressed inside the United States. I advocate an end to capitalist exploitation, the abolition of racist policies, the eradication of sexism, and the elimination of political repression," she wrote to Pope John Paul II in 1998. "If that is a crime, then I am totally guilty." The 2014 letter closes with greetings from Cuba, described as "One of the Largest, Most Resistant and Most Courageous Palenques (Maroon Camps) that has ever existed on the face of this planet." The word links her exile to the history of communities founded by people who fled slavery, which is also the term used by McHarris for her political practice. Her own account of the arrival in Havana joins the personal to that history: "Our fight had started on a slave ship years before we were born. Venceremos, my favorite word in Spanish, crossed my mind."

## What the three texts share

Jones, Davis and Shakur wrote from different positions, and the differences should not be flattened. Jones worked inside a Communist Party and used its journal to argue for a reorganization of its own leadership. Davis wrote as a professor and party member whose case became the focus of an international campaign, and she used that platform to argue for the release of all political prisoners and later for the abolition of prisons. Shakur wrote from underground and then from exile, and her central claim was that oppressive conditions produce revolutionaries. All three connected race, class and gender in a single analysis rather than treating them as separate campaigns to be coordinated afterward. Jones's "as workers, as Negroes, and as women," Davis's insistence in the 1998 interview on "the intersectionality, the interconnectedness of all of these institutions and attitudes," and Shakur's program of an end to capitalist exploitation, racist policies, sexism and political repression are three formulations of that refusal.

All three also used the courtroom and the public platform to state a program rather than to plead for themselves. Jones told the judge that she pleaded guilty to holding communist ideas. Davis argued that her advocacy of the Soledad Brothers was treated as the motive for a crime and moved from criticism of "an exclusively legalistic approach" to self-representation. Shakur turned a sentencing statement into a definition of the Black Liberation Army and a poem. The form of the defence was itself part of the politics.

The international dimension was central rather than an extra for each of them. Jones moved from the "Half the World" column to essays on the British West Indies and the Caribbean community in Britain, and she built a carnival out of the diaspora she wrote about. Davis described letters from the German Democratic Republic, Cuba, Italy, Germany and France, and located her party membership in a network of revolutionary movements. Shakur ended her letters with greetings from Cuba and with the claim that the struggle on a slave ship preceded her by generations. None of them treated the Black struggle in the United States as a national question alone.
