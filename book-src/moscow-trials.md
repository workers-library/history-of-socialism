## The transcripts

On 19 August 1936 sixteen men were brought before the Military Collegium of the Supreme Court in Moscow, the court that heard political cases, in the first of three public proceedings later grouped together as the Moscow Trials. The state published each proceeding as a book, so the voices of the accused could be read far beyond the courtroom ([1936 report](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/government/law/1936/moscow-trials/index.htm)). The English text is an abridgement from the People's Commissariat of Justice with no translator named. It shows the architecture of the proceeding and the way confessions were produced: Andrei Vyshinsky, the state attorney, repeatedly put words into a witness's mouth and asked him to confirm them. Questioned on 20 August, Grigori Zinoviev, a former leader of the Communist International, the body that directed communist parties worldwide, accepted that he and Lev Kamenev had practised "double-dealing" since the 1920s and that Stalin, Kirov and Voroshilov had been marked for assassination. Asked whether he was telling the whole truth, he replied, "Now I am telling the whole truth to the end." ([Zinoviev testimony, 20 August 1936](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/government/law/1936/moscow-trials/20/zinoviev.htm)). The record preserves the words, not the conditions in which they were spoken.

The last pleas of 23 August read as a contest in self-accusation. Kamenev told the court: "For ten years, if not more, I waged a struggle against the Party, against the government of the land of Soviets, and against Stalin personally." ([last pleas, 23 August 1936](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/government/law/1936/moscow-trials/23/last-pleas.htm)). Zinoviev called Trotskyism "a variety of fascism." Smirnov still denied responsibility for acts after his arrest, and Holtzman's entire printed plea was one sentence denouncing his co-defendants. That divergence matters because the editing was severe. Friedrich Adler, a socialist writing from the published report rather than the courtroom, noted that Vyshinsky's speech filled forty-nine pages while the last pleas, said to last fourteen hours, were compressed into ten ([Adler, *The Witchcraft Trial in Moscow*, 1936](https://www.marxists.org/archive/adler-friedrich/1936/trials.htm)). What was cut cannot be recovered.

## Bukharin in the dock

The last trial, in March 1938, put Nikolai Bukharin, a former Party theorist and one of the Old Bolsheviks, members of the party before 1917, before the same court on charges that a "bloc of Rights and Trotskyites" had acted for foreign intelligence services ([1938 trial record](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bukharin/works/1938/trial/index.htm)). His testimony is the hardest document to read, because confession and rebuttal sit in the same text. He accepted political responsibility for the bloc yet denied the particular acts. Asked on 7 March whether he had sent Slepkov to organise an insurrection, he answered "Yes"; asked whether the plan was his, he said, "I have the right to tell the Court not what you want, but what really happened." ([Bukharin interrogation, 7 March 1938](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bukharin/works/1938/trial/2.htm)). Asked to accept Rykov's or Sharangovich's word as proof, he replied, "If Rykov says it is, I have no grounds for not believing him" ([Bukharin interrogation, March 1938](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bukharin/works/1938/trial/2.htm)). The case was built out of prisoners' statements about one another.

His last plea of 12 March combined a sweeping confession with detailed denials. He denied complicity in the deaths of Kirov, Menzhinsky, Kuibyshev, Gorky and Peshkov, denied the 1918 plot to kill Lenin, and called the prosecutor's reasoning tautology, an argument that assumes what it sets out to prove. Explaining his own timing: "For three months I refused to say anything. Then I began to testify. Why? Because while in prison I made a revaluation of my entire past." ([last plea, 12 March 1938](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bukharin/works/1938/trial/3.htm)). He attacked the court's principle: "The confession of the accused is not essential. The confession of the accused is a medieval principle of jurisprudence." ([same](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bukharin/works/1938/trial/3.htm)). And: "I categorically deny that I was connected with foreign intelligence services, that they were my masters and that I acted in accordance with their wishes." ([same](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bukharin/works/1938/trial/3.htm)). Whether this was strategy, damaged memory or the effect of interrogation cannot be settled from the transcript. The plea was also a performance addressed, as Bukharin said, to "West European and American intellectuals," and his widow's later repudiation belongs to another moment ([1938 trial index](https://www.marxists.org/archive/bukharin/works/1938/trial/index.htm)).

## Details that could be checked

The January 1937 trial of Piatakov and Radek left claims that could be tested outside the courtroom. A pamphlet by the "Que Faire?" group around André Ferrat, a dissident communist, recorded that Piatakov described flying from Berlin to Oslo in December 1935 to meet Trotsky, and that an official Oslo check found no foreign plane had arrived that month ([*Why Did They "Confess"?*, 1937](https://www.marxists.org/history/ussr/government/law/1937/moscow-trials/radek-piatakov-trial.htm)). It placed Holtzman's supposed Copenhagen meeting at a Hotel Bristol demolished in 1917 and noted that case-file numbers ran to thirty-six for seventeen defendants. The pamphlet was a polemic relying on press reports, but these were details that either happened or did not.

The two observers who sat in a courtroom reached opposite conclusions. D. N. Pritt, a British King's Counsel and Labour member of parliament at the 1936 trial, judged the confessions genuine: "It seems plain to me, on a number of different grounds, that anything in the nature of forced confessions is intrinsically impossible." ([Pritt, *The Zinoviev Trial*, 1936](http://www.revolutionarydemocracy.org/archive/prittzinov.htm)). He had seen only the public hearing, not the preliminary investigation, a point he conceded later. Adler, working from the printed report, saw the omissions and the false hotel detail and concluded the opposite: "The 16 defendants are dead. The manner in which their 'confessions' were secured is covered with the cloak of secrecy." ([Adler, 1936](https://www.marxists.org/archive/adler-friedrich/1936/trials.htm)). Both were honest witnesses with strong commitments, and nothing in the record reconciles them.

Trotsky, named in the charges but living in exile in Mexico, was defended by a commission chaired by the philosopher John Dewey. A frame-up is a false charge manufactured by the accuser, and this was its word: "We therefore find the Moscow trials to be frame-ups." ([*Not Guilty*, findings of 21 September 1937](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/document/dewey-report/index.htm)). It found the Oslo flight had not happened and heard testimony about night interrogations and torture, while saying plainly that it did not know whether the defendants were guilty of other charges and had no power to compel documents from governments. Independently, the US ambassador Joseph Davies telegraphed from Moscow on 2 March 1938 that all but three of the twenty-one defendants waived counsel and that Krestinsky repudiated his confession in open court: "Krestinski's withdrawal of plea of guilty, his claim of not guilty, and his repudiation of confession made as late as yesterday was the sensation of the opening session." ([Davies telegram, 2 March 1938](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1933-39/d453)). Davies was a diplomat, not an investigator, filing an impression that evening.

## How the confessions were obtained

On method the sources converge, though none shows a confession being made. A Central Committee directive attributed to Stalin, dated 10 January 1939, states that "the use of physical pressure in the work of the NKVD has been permitted since 1937 in accordance with a resolution of the VKP CC" ([directive attributed to Stalin, 1939](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/stalin/works/1939/01/10.htm)). The NKVD was the Soviet secret police, the VKP the Communist Party. Pressure was to be used exceptionally against prisoners who refused to name accomplices, and the document admits abuse by named officials. Its translation is disputed and its authenticity contested, and it belongs after the trials. The Dewey commission had already printed testimony from Serge, Ciliga and Tarov about night interrogations and torture, and Radek said an examining official told him fifteen people had testified against him and that he spent "two and a half months" learning what others had confessed ([Dewey report](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/document/dewey-report/index.htm)). In 1956 Khrushchev told the Twentieth Congress: "Often, no longer able to bear barbaric tortures, they charged themselves (at the order of the investigative judges/falsifiers) with all kinds of grave and unlikely crimes." ([Khrushchev report, 24 February 1956](https://www.marxists.org/archive/khrushchev/1956/02/24.htm)). He was defending a party that had authorised the repressions, but on method his account agrees with the directive and with the defendants' own timelines.

## Limits of the record

These sources leave large gaps. The English reports are abridged Soviet translations with no named translators, and no Russian stenographic original underlies the quotations above. The full 1937 court report is not available, so Piatakov's and Radek's testimony comes through the Dewey commission, the contemporary pamphlet and surviving indictment material, all partial or second-hand. Bukharin's "Letter to the Central Committee" has survived in no reliable English full text, and quotations attributed to it cannot be used without the original. The 1939 directive attributed to Stalin has a disputed translation and contested authenticity, and proves nothing about any one trial. Answering the question of method fully would need the Moscow archives and a translator. The voices that survive are senior men, foreign lawyers and diplomats; wives, children, guards and ordinary prisoners appear only when a commission quoted them. The case for the trials rests chiefly on one sympathetic visitor, Pritt, and other contemporary Soviet defences have not been set beside him. The directive, Khrushchev's account and the defendants' timelines make duress highly likely, yet no confession has been shown to be fabricated word for word. The records establish the architecture of the proceeding and converging testimony about pressure, not the private guilt or innocence of every man in the dock.

## How historians have read it

- Stephen Kotkin, *Magnetic Mountain: Stalinism as a Civilization* (1997). Argues that Stalinism offered ordinary people a language and a project they partly chose; its archival and interview method sets the trials in a wider society, explaining popular participation rather than centring the terror. [Source: ucpress.edu](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/magnetic-mountain/paper)
- Nicolas Werth, "Mass Crimes under Stalin (1930–1953)" (2008). Distinguishes the public show trials, which accounted for 40,000–50,000 of roughly 800,000 executions, from the secret "mass operations" decided by Stalin and carried out by the NKVD. [Source: sciencespo.fr](https://www.sciencespo.fr/mass-violence-war-massacre-resistance/en/news/mass-crimes-under-stalin-1930-1953/)
- Roy Medvedev, "Bukharin's Last Years," *New Left Review* I/109 (1978). A Soviet dissident's reconstruction of Bukharin's final years from materials available before the open archives; it treats the confessions as a political tragedy rather than a simple lie. [Source: newleftreview.org](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i109/articles/roy-medvedev-bukharin-s-last-years.pdf)
- Robert Conquest, *The Great Terror* (1968; revised 1990). The classic totalitarian account, which read the trials as a planned elimination of the Old Bolsheviks.
- J. Arch Getty, *The Origins of the Great Purges* (1985) and Getty, Rittersporn and Zemskov, "Victims of the Soviet Penal System in the Pre-War Years" (1993). Revisionist archival work stressing the quota system and local officials over a single master plan.
- Stephen Cohen, *Bukharin and the Bolshevik Dream* (1974). The standard biography of Bukharin as a revolutionary whose policies were defeated and whose confession must be read against his earlier political record.

There is broad agreement that the confessions were not reliable evidence, that physical coercion was used, and that the public trials were a small part of a much larger repression. Historians still disagree about how much of the defendants' testimony was true, how much agency they kept, and how directly Stalin planned each stage. The finding that the trials were frame-ups is well supported for the charges against Trotsky and Sedov; it is not proof about every defendant's private conduct.
