On 14 June 1839 a large roll of paper was carried into the House of Commons. Thomas Attwood, the MP for Birmingham, had helped draft the petition and now presented it; members laughed at the roll. A sympathetic ally rather than a man of the movement, he spent his speech on the defensive: "I never, in the whole course of my life, recommended any means, or inculcated any doctrine except peace, law, order, loyalty, and union" ([Thomas Attwood MP, House of Commons, 14 June 1839](https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/1839-06-14/debates/17c1dabe-b179-4b6c-b9df-72579cf5605c/NationalPetition%E2%80%94TheChartists)). He said it carried 1,280,000 signatures from 214 towns and villages and some 500 public meetings. The Speaker then ruled that a member presenting a petition could not speak at length. That roll was the most visible part of Chartism, the first mass working-class political movement in Britain.

The petition pointed to a document published a year earlier. The People's Charter of May 1838 was not a rallying cry but a complete draft of an Act of Parliament, thirty-six pages long, with thirteen sections, rules for registration, a mechanism for secret voting, annual parliaments and £500 a year for members. Its authors were the London Working Men's Association committee, with William Lovett and Francis Place taking the leading hand. The six points were a programme that could be printed as a bill and read clause by clause: a vote for every man, a secret ballot, no property qualification for MPs, equal electoral districts, annual parliaments and payment of members. "Universal suffrage" then meant every adult man; women were outside it.

## A draft Act of Parliament

The Charter's preface explained the authors' distrust of earlier reformers: "We have seen one zealous Reformer after another desert us as his party was triumphant, or his interests served." ([London Working Men's Association, address prefixed to *The People's Charter*, 8 May 1838, third edition September 1838](https://www.chartistancestors.co.uk/the-peoples-charter-a-radical-pamphlet)) That calculation also decided who was left out. They did not claim women were unfit to vote; they feared that including women's suffrage would rouse prejudice and "retard the measure" for men. On this point a movement built on the claim that working people performed the duties of citizens gave way to the calculations of a parliamentary campaign.

The Female Political Union of Newcastle rejected the doctrine that politics belonged to men: "We have been told that the province of women is her home, and that the field of politics should be left to men; this we deny." ([Female Political Union of Newcastle, *Northern Star*, 2 February 1839](https://spartacus-educational.com/ExamIR14.htm)) They linked overwork and under-education to political exclusion and built organisations with rules and funds. The Charter's exclusion of women therefore did not mark their absence from the movement. It marked a limit in what its male authors would ask Parliament for.

## Freemen and missionaries

The first national petition put the claim in the movement's own language: distress followed from political exclusion, "the few have governed for the interest of the few." Its most quoted line claimed citizenship rather than charity: "We perform the duties of freemen; we must have the privileges of freemen." ([National Petition, 1839, reprinted in William Lovett, *Life and Struggles*, 1876](https://www.chartistancestors.co.uk/full-text-of-the-petition-for-the-charter-1839)) The text closed with a formal prayer to the Commons. It omitted equal electoral districts, a point Lovett and others disputed with Attwood.

The first Chartist Convention met in London on 4 February 1839 to decide what should happen if Parliament refused. Its fifty-three delegates, by Lovett's count, included John Frost and Feargus O'Connor. They argued over moral force and physical force, meaning persuasion and lawful pressure on one side, the threat of armed resistance on the other. Missionaries went out with strict instructions "to refrain from all violent and unconstitutional language, and not to infringe the law in any manner by word or deed" ([instructions to Convention missionaries, 1839, recorded by William Lovett in 1876](https://www.chartistancestors.co.uk/first-chartist-convention-1839)). The Convention also asked local meetings whether they would withdraw savings, abstain from taxed goods, observe a "sacred month" without work, arm themselves and deal only with Chartists. That manifesto was consultative, not proof that anyone did these things, and both Lovett and O'Connor later denied responsibility for measures each had backed. The dispute over force was less a fixed split between programmes than an argument participants later reshaped in their own defence.

## The Westgate Hotel

In the early hours of 4 November 1839 the argument reached the Westgate Hotel in Newport. John Frost led a march from the mining valleys; the windows were smashed, the Riot Act was read, and soldiers fired. Henry Mullock, recalling the scene in 1897, remembered the crowd's movement rather than its politics: "We watched until they turned the corner of the Westgate and began smashing the windows." ([Henry Mullock, recalled in the *South Wales Argus*, 1897](https://www.southwalesargus.co.uk/news/18010469.newport-uprising-words-people-saw-happen)) More than three thousand court documents survive from the treason trials, including depositions, an indictment and a workhouse register listing men with gunshot wounds. Who fired first is still unclear, and witnesses gave evidence to magistrates after the event under a prosecution seeking treason convictions. Frost and two others were transported for life.

With no central party, the newspaper that held the movement together mattered as much as its conventions. The *Northern Star*, founded in 1837 by O'Connor and Joshua Hobson, sold around ten thousand copies in 1838 and as many as fifty thousand for one issue in 1839. A French illustrated paper described a worker reading it aloud in a workshop to men it called "fervid Chartists," his voice "borrow[ing] its notes from the voice of a locomotive." That is reportage rather than audited circulation, but it describes a paper read in common and used to call meetings. The same network carried the strike of 1842. As wages were cut, strikers met on Mottram Moor on 7 August and resolved "that all labour should cease until the People's Charter became the law of the land" ([resolution at Mottram Moor, 7 August 1842, recalled by Thomas Cooper in 1872](https://www.marxists.org/history/england/chartists/plug-plot.htm)). Cooper recalled the Convention meeting in Manchester on 17 August while every chimney stood cold. Whether this was a Chartist general strike or a wage strike that Chartists took over remains disputed.

The distress behind the strike entered the House of Commons. Thomas Duncombe told MPs in May 1842 that the second petition carried nearly three and a half million signatures and read letters from Sheffield, Burnley and the Vale of Leven into the record. A Burnley correspondent wrote: "The working classes are in an awful state of destitution; there are hundreds out of employment, and those that are employed, or partially so, cannot by their earnings procure a sufficiency of food." ([unnamed Burnley correspondent, letter read to the House of Commons by Thomas Duncombe, 3 May 1842](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1842/may/03/the-national-petition-the-charter)) A Convention delegate from the Vale of Leven added: "I know scores of families who had never tasted animal food in that time; oatmeal boiled in water, sweetened with a little sugar, is their principal diet." ([William Thomason, letter read to the Commons, 3 May 1842](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1842/may/03/the-national-petition-the-charter)) The letters were selected by an advocate, not a survey, but they put places and amounts on record. MPs refused to hear petitioners at the bar and defeated the motion by 49 votes to 287.

## Five points out of six

The third petition, presented by O'Connor in April 1848, was too heavy to carry through the Commons in one piece and was rolled across the floor. O'Connor claimed more than five million signatures; Commons clerks counted 1,975,496, of which 8.2 per cent were women. The demand was unchanged, but its spokesmen now treated the Charter as a route to wider social change. Engels, reporting a London meeting in January 1848, heard Ernest Jones argue that political freedom had to come first: "Under feudalism, the people were fat slaves; under your rule, Sir defender of the middle class, they are lean slaves." ([Ernest Jones, London meeting, January 1848, reported in the *Northern Star*, translated into French by Engels and back into English in MECW](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1848/01/19.htm)) George Julian Harney ended with "No vote, no musket!" Jones's words reach us through a newspaper report, a French translation and an English retranslation.

A modern register built from contemporary newspapers locates around ninety-three female groups between 1838 and 1851, with estimates of up to two hundred; the Birmingham Women's Political Union claimed three thousand members by September 1838. They kept rules, raised funds, boycotted shops and spoke in public, and in Sheffield later petitioned for women's suffrage. Five of the Charter's six points eventually became law. Equal electoral districts were achieved in 1885; the exception was annual parliaments, the demand for annual elections. Equal districts were the point that had divided Lovett from Attwood in 1839 and that the petition of that year left out. Malcolm Chase has argued that petitioning itself constructed Chartism and settled who counted as the people ([Chase, 2019](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/128375/)); Dorothy Thompson's study shifted attention to local, everyday organisation and to women's part in a movement for manhood suffrage ([review of Thompson, 1984](https://web.archive.org/web/20260108222221/https://www.historytoday.com/archive/chartism)).

## What the record cannot show

The original signature sheets do not survive, so no headline total can be checked against the names. The Newport court papers are rich, but the depositions were taken by magistrates after the event from people who had often not given evidence voluntarily, and witnesses minimized their own part. No comparable testimony survives for the strikers of 1842 or for female Chartists, whose voices appear only where a reporter recorded them, often without first names. Most testimony is hostile, as with magistrates and loyalist papers, or written decades later, as with Lovett, Cooper and the newspaper witnesses. Bronterre O'Brien's own writing, Jones's own speeches and Welsh-language material remain outside this account, and the apparent decline of women's groups after 1842 may be a silence in the press. The personal collection behind Chartist Ancestors is not an archival edition, so its transcriptions need checking against copies in the British Library or National Archives. What cannot be recovered is the movement's inner life: how a strike spread house by house, or what most marchers at Newport believed they were doing.
