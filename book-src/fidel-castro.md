Fidel Castro was born on 13 August 1926 at Birán, in the eastern province then called Oriente, and died in Havana on 25 November 2016. Between those dates he became the central figure of a revolution that overthrew a dictatorship, changed the ownership of land and industry, aligned Cuba with the Soviet bloc, and outlived the collapse of that bloc by decades. He also left an enormous body of speeches, declarations, interviews and reflections. Those texts are the starting point for any study of the Revolution, because they show what its leaders wanted the world to believe about it. They need to be read with care. A courtroom plea written by a defendant is an argument, not a neutral record. A party newspaper is an institution with an interest. A speech delivered twenty years after an event is evidence of how a leader later understood that event, not necessarily of what happened at the time. Historians have spent decades checking the movement's founding stories against court records, interviews with participants and the testimony of people who lived through the changes. This chapter follows the life and the arguments together, and marks where the official account and the independent evidence part company.

## Student politics and the road to Moncada

Castro entered the University of Havana in 1945 and became a student leader there. In 1947 he joined the founding of the Orthodox Party (*Partido Ortodoxo*), the party built around Eduardo Chibás, whose radio broadcasts attacked corruption and whose death in 1951 made him a martyr for a generation of students. The biographical note published by Pathfinder Press, a United States movement publisher that issues collected editions of Castro's speeches, records the sequence: "A student leader at the University of Havana from 1945. Founding member of the Orthodox Party in 1947 and central organizer of its revolutionary-minded youth" ([Pathfinder Press](https://www.pathfinderpress.com/collections/fidel-castro)). In *History Will Absolve Me* Castro quoted Chibás as an authority on the abuses of the regime: "How right Eduardo Chibás was in his last radio speech, when he said that Batista was encouraging the return of the colonels, castor oil and the law of the fugitive!" ([*History Will Absolve Me*, 16 October 1953](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1953/10/16.htm)).

The official Cuban constitutional history presents the same period as a constitutionalist one. When General Fulgencio Batista seized power on 10 March 1952, "the first [person] who went to a court to denounce the coup d'état of Batista and the repeal of the Constitution of 1940 was Fidel Castro Ruz. The second was Armando Hart," and the University Students' Federation, the FEU, "carried out the burial of the Constitution" ([Cuban National Assembly, *Historia Constitucional Cubana*](https://www.parlamentocubano.gob.cu/index.php/historia-constitucional-cubana)). Castro's own retrospective account, given in 1961, stresses method rather than doctrine. His generation "went about recruiting young people, picking from the youthful elements ... those who were more serious, more willing, more involved and had a more sincere revolutionary inclination," and when rival student groups arrived "with a machine gun," his own organisation "didn't have machine guns" and won cadres by political explanation instead ([Speech on Marxism-Leninism, 2 December 1961](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/12/02.htm)). The decision to organise a revolutionary movement came only "until we became convinced that the people were really being deceived and that it was all madness, all that madness" (Ibid.). This is a retrospective self-description, valuable for what the mature Castro wanted to say about his own formation and not a contemporary record of the 1940s.

He described that formation in deliberately unflattering terms. In the 1961 address he recalled that when he left university "I had already been greatly influenced — I wouldn't say that I was a Marxist-Leninist, far from it. It is possible that I had two million petit bourgeois prejudices and a string of ideas that I'm glad not to have anymore" ([1961 Marxism-Leninism speech](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/12/02.htm)). He added a detail that is itself a piece of autobiographical evidence: "it is a great satisfaction to have been illiterate at the age of eighteen and to feel revolutionary as I do now at thirty odd years — I think the 'odd years' run to thirty-six" (Ibid.). The intellectual lineage he claimed was Cuban before it was international. In the 1953 defence he invoked "the Master," José Martí, from memory, because prison censorship kept Martí's books from him: "it makes no difference! I carry the teachings of the Master in my heart, and in my mind the noble ideas of all men who have defended people's freedom everywhere!" ([*History Will Absolve Me*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1953/10/16.htm)). Two decades later he still posed the question of lineage in Martí's terms: "Was Marti a Marxist-Leninist? No, Marti was not a Marxist-Leninist. Marti said of Marx that since he placed himself on the side 01 the poor, he had all his sympathies" ([1961 Marxism-Leninism speech](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/12/02.htm); the error "01" is in the archive transcription).

*History Will Absolve Me* also set out the movement's view of the coup. Castro wrote that "the March 10th coup took place at the moment when the civil government's prestige had dwindled to its lowest ebb, a circumstance of which Batista and his clique took advantage," and asked why the generals had not waited until after the elections, "Simply because, had they waited for the majority of the nation to express its will at the polls, the troops would not have responded to the conspiracy!" ([*History Will Absolve Me*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1953/10/16.htm)). He described his own legal response: as a private citizen he "presented a writ denouncing the crimes and asking that Fulgencio Batista and his seventeen accomplices be sentenced to 108 years in prison as decreed by the Social Defense Code," but "Days and months passed. What a disappointment! The accused remained unmolested: he strode up and down the country like a great lord and was called Honorable Sir and General: he removed and replaced judges at will" (Ibid.). The lesson he drew became the movement's founding premise: "I heartily believe revolution to be the source of legal right; but the nocturnal armed assault of March 10th could never be considered a revolution" (Ibid.). The Revolution thus presented itself in its foundational text not as an assault on law but as the restoration of a violated legal order.

## Moncada, 26 July 1953, and the defence speech

At dawn on 26 July 1953 a group of young revolutionaries attacked the Moncada Barracks in Santiago de Cuba and the garrison at Bayamo. The operation's proclaimed watchword survives in the short address Castro delivered before leaving:

> "This movement will triumph anyway. If you are victorious tomorrow, Martí's aspirations will became true sooner. If not, the gesture will serve to set an example for the Cuban people to take up the flag and keep going forward. The people will support us in the Eastern region and in the entire island. We the Youth of the Centennial of the Apostle, just as in 1868 and in 1895, here in the East we cry out for the first time: FREEDOM OR DEATH!"

> Source: [Fidel Castro, *Speech before Leaving for the Moncada Barracks*, 26 July 1953](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1953/07/26-july-1953.htm)

The same address records the declared rule of restraint, "The slogan is not to kill, only as a last resort" (Ibid.), a rule to which the defence speech returns: "Everyone had instructions, first of all, to be humane in the struggle. Never was a group of armed men more generous to the adversary" ([*History Will Absolve Me*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1953/10/16.htm)). The 26th of July Movement took its name from the date.

The attack failed within minutes. The rebels were driven from the barracks, and many were captured. After the fighting, Batista's soldiers summarily executed more than fifty rebel prisoners. That much is not in serious dispute. What is disputed is the account Castro gave at his trial. Antonio Rafael de la Cova, a historian of Cuba at Indiana University, spent three decades interviewing more than a hundred participants, including surviving rebels, soldiers, government personnel and politicians. His study of the attack concludes that Moncada was a humiliating military defeat that Castro recast as a political victory, and it sets out to separate the facts from what de la Cova calls the persistent falsehoods in *History Will Absolve Me* about atrocities committed by both sides ([Antonio Rafael de la Cova, *The Moncada Attack: Birth of the Cuban Revolution*, 2007](https://www.latinamericanstudies.org/academic/Moncada-order.pdf)). Hugh Thomas's *Cuba: The Pursuit of Freedom*, a comprehensive academic history first published in 1971, treats Moncada as the opening act of a longer crisis of Cuban constitutional government rather than as an inevitable turning point ([Hugh Thomas, *Cuba: The Pursuit of Freedom*](https://archive.org/details/cubapursuitoffre0000unse)). The broad consequence is clear even where the details are contested: the defeat and the executions gave the movement the political material it needed, and the memory of Moncada became the founding legend of the Revolution.

Castro was captured on 1 August 1953 and tried in October. The text he delivered in his own defence was printed in Havana by Editorial de Ciencias Sociales in 1975, translated by Pedro Álvarez Tabío and Andrew Paul Booth, and rechecked against the Spanish *La historia me absolverá* in 1981 ([MIA headnote](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1953/10/16.htm)). It is both a plea against the dictatorship and a complete programme of government. Its opening establishes the ground on which Castro wanted to stand: "Never has a lawyer had to practice his profession under such difficult conditions; never has such a number of overwhelming irregularities been committed against an accused man. In this case, counsel and defendant are one and the same" (Ibid.). Its social core is a definition of "the people" that became central to the movement's language:

> "When we speak of struggle and we mention the people we mean the vast unredeemed masses, those to whom everyone makes promises and who are deceived by all; we mean the people who yearn for a better, more dignified and more just nation; who are moved by ancestral aspirations to justice, for they have suffered injustice and mockery generation after generation."

> Source: [*History Will Absolve Me*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1953/10/16.htm)

Castro enumerated those masses with exact figures: "the six hundred thousand Cubans without work," "the five hundred thousand farm laborers who live in miserable shacks, who work four months of the year and starve the rest," "the four hundred thousand industrial workers and laborers whose retirement funds have been embezzled," "the one hundred thousand small farmers who live and die working land that is not theirs," "the thirty thousand teachers and professors," "the twenty thousand small business men," and "the ten thousand young professional people" (Ibid.). These were the Revolution's self-declared constituency, and the census of hardship became a standard part of Castro's oratory. In his 1960 address to the United Nations he repeated the inventory in slightly different form: "600,000 able Cubans were unemployed," "Three million out of a population of somewhat over 6,000,000 did not have electric lights," and "Thirty-seven and one half percent of our population were illiterate" ([*To the U.N. General Assembly*, 26 September 1960](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1960/09/26.htm)).

The programme itself was set out as five laws that "would have been proclaimed immediately after the capture of the Moncada Barracks and would have been broadcast to the nation by radio": (1) the return of power to the people and the proclamation of the 1940 Constitution as the supreme law until the people decided otherwise; (2) non-mortgageable, non-transferable ownership of the land to tenant farmers, sharecroppers and squatters holding five caballerías (a traditional measure of land area) or less, with the state indemnifying former owners on the basis of the rent the land would have earned over ten years; (3) the right of workers and employees to share 30 percent of the profits of large industrial, commercial and mining enterprises, including the sugar mills; (4) the right of sugar planters to 55 percent of sugar production and a minimum quota of forty thousand arrobas for small tenant farmers established for three years or more; and (5) the confiscation of holdings and gains acquired by fraud under earlier regimes, including those of the heirs of the guilty ([*History Will Absolve Me*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1953/10/16.htm)). Castro promised further measures: "the Agrarian Reform, the Integral Educational Reform, nationalization of the electric power trust and the telephone trust, refund to the people of the illegal and repressive rates these companies have charged, and payment to the treasury of all taxes brazenly evaded in the past" (Ibid.). The six-point summary has been widely quoted because it names the areas the Revolution later claimed as its own: "The problem of the land, the problem of industrialization, the problem of housing, the problem of unemployment, the problem of education and the problem of the people's health: these are the six problems we would take immediate steps to solve, along with restoration of civil liberties and political democracy" (Ibid.).

The speech's economic diagnosis is an early form of the class analysis the Revolution later made explicit: "The nation's future, the solutions to its problems, cannot continue to depend on the selfish interests of a dozen big businessmen nor on the cold calculations of profits that ten or twelve magnates draw up in their air-conditioned offices" (Ibid.). Its confidence is stated in words Castro borrowed from Martí: "A true man does not seek the path where advantage lies, but rather the path where duty lies, and this is the only practical man, whose dream of today will be the law of tomorrow" (Ibid.). It ends with the line that gave the text its title:

> "I know that imprisonment will be harder for me than it has ever been for anyone, filled with cowardly threats and hideous cruelty. But I do not fear prison, as I do not fear the fury of the miserable tyrant who took the lives of 70 of my comrades. Condemn me. It does not matter. History will absolve me."

> Source: [*History Will Absolve Me*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1953/10/16.htm)

Two further themes of the later Revolution appear here. The first is a refusal of vengeance: "For my dead comrades, I claim no vengeance. Since their lives were priceless, the murderers could not pay for them even with their own lives. It is not by blood that we may redeem the lives of those who died for their country. The happiness of their people is the only tribute worthy of them" (Ibid.). The second is the claim that revolutions cannot be imported or exported. Castro rejected the dictator's assertion that the attack had been financed from abroad: "the regime has emphatically repeated that our Movement did not have popular support. I have never heard an assertion so naive, and at the same time so full of bad faith" (Ibid.). His statement that the right of insurrection against tyranny "is always binding within a democratic society" supplied the movement's legal doctrine, and he supported it with a catalogue of thinkers from Aquinas and Mariana to Locke, Rousseau and Paine, proving that "the right of rebellion against tyranny, Honorable Judges, has been recognized from the most ancient times to the present day by men of all creeds, ideas and doctrines" (Ibid.). The closing appeal addresses the judges as future defendants: "Remember that today you are judging an accused man, but that you yourselves will be judged not once, but many times, as often as these days are submitted to scrutiny in the future" (Ibid.).

Castro was sentenced to fifteen years. During his imprisonment *La historia me absolverá* was written, duplicated clandestinely and circulated. The 1975 edition was translated and then "rechecked the translation with the Spanish La historia me absolverá, same publisher, in 1981" ([MIA headnote](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1953/10/16.htm)), which presupposes the earlier Spanish printings. Pathfinder's biographical note records the release: "Released in May 1955 after a mass amnesty campaign" ([Pathfinder](https://www.pathfinderpress.com/collections/fidel-castro)), a formulation that makes the official claim explicit, namely that the amnesty was won by public mobilisation rather than granted by the dictator's grace.

## Exile, the Granma expedition and the Sierra Maestra

In Mexico, Castro organised the expedition that carried the 26th of July Movement back to Cuba. The movement publisher's summary reads: "From Mexico, Castro prepared the Granma expedition, which returned to Cuba in December 1956. He commanded the Rebel Army during the 1956–58 revolutionary war and from May 1958 headed the July 26 Movement" ([Pathfinder](https://www.pathfinderpress.com/collections/fidel-castro)). Castro's own memorial address of 18 October 1967 supplies a participant's detail: Che Guevara joined the group in Mexico in July or August 1955, "and in one night — as he tells in his accounts — he became a future expeditionary of the 'Granma.' But at that time that expedition had no ship, no weapons, no troops" ([*In Tribute to Che Guevara*, 18 October 1967](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1967/10/oct-18-1967.htm)). The same address recalls that Guevara "spent the whole voyage suffering a serious asthma attack without a single chance of relief, but also without a single complaint" (Ibid.).

The landing of 2 December 1956 and its aftermath were described by Castro in his 1961 retrospective on the origins of the party. He did not embellish it: "When we reached the Sierra Maestra, however, it was evident that we had not organized certain aspects of the struggle we were undertaking. For example, we hadn't even made a geographical survey of the Sierra Maestra. We hadn't even set up a preliminary organization in the Sierra Maestra; in short, we could not have started the struggle under worse conditions. ... we did not know a single peasant in the Sierra Maestra" ([1961 Marxism-Leninism speech](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/12/02.htm)). The first armed action after the landing is dated by Castro himself: "the day on which we took the first district, that of La Plata on January 17, we launched a surprise attack at dawn on a mixed patrol of soldiers and marines consisting of twelve men — we were about sixteen. We took them by surprise and overcame them. We took all their arms. Our forces came out of the operation that day with twenty-nine men" (Ibid.). The Sierra strategy was not imagined as a small-band adventure: "We knew that you can win power only with the support of the people, by mobilizing the masses. We never thought we could win power with ten, twelve or a hundred men. We intended to lay the groundwork for revolutionary struggle through a guerilla action and to develop the struggle until it becomes a mass struggle" (Ibid.).

The relationship between the Rebel Army and the rural poor is described in the same retrospective in concrete terms:

> "Wherever we went, we found peasants who were faced with lawsuits. All the peasants were faced with the problem of eviction. ... There was not one school there; not one teacher. If these peasants had realized much sooner what they could have done, it is possible that with only half a dozen rifles they could at least have made themselves independent in the mountains! For the conditions were very favorable. It was a better fate for the peasant to grab a rifle and rise up than to be thrown off his land and endure the hardship and misery he suffered."

> Source: [1961 Marxism-Leninism speech](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/12/02.htm)

The theory of guerrilla war that emerged from this experience is stated in the same address with unusual clarity. "We were like a match in a haystack. I won't say in a cane field because a match in a cane field is serious business. A match in a haystack! That was the guerrilla movement, given the conditions that existed in our country. Little by little, the struggle became a struggle of all the people" (Ibid.). Castro linked the image to a distinction that runs through his later thought. By **objective conditions** he meant the material circumstances of the country: its poverty, its landholding, its institutions and its international position. By **subjective conditions** he meant the awareness, organisation and leadership of the people involved. "Revolutions do not spring from the minds of men. People can interpret an historical law, a certain moment of historical development. To make a correct interpretation is to propel the revolutionary movement. In Cuba, our role has been that of propellers of that movement, through evaluating a series of objective conditions" (Ibid.). The same distinction appears in the Second Declaration of Havana of 1962: "The subjective conditions of each country, that is to say the factor of consciousness, organisation and leadership, can speed up or hold back the revolution according to how far they have developed; but sooner or later, in each historical epoch, when the objective conditions ripen, consciousness is acquired, organisation is achieved, leadership arises and the revolution occurs" ([*Segunda Declaración de La Habana*, 4 February 1962](http://www.cuba.cu/gobierno/discursos/1962/esp/f040262e.html), translation mine). This is a **materialist** account of revolution, meaning one that explains political change by material circumstances rather than by ideas or great individuals alone.

The turning point of 1958 was the failed general strike of April and the subsequent Rebel Army offensive. Castro's own assessment is a corrective to romantic accounts: "I remember when we gave the word to strike, prematurely, when the revolutionary movement made what you could call an error in evaluating the objective conditions, already trying to seize power in April, 1958. At that time, we still had very few men. If I am not mistaken, the total of our guerrilla forces numbered about 180 combatants" ([1961 Marxism-Leninism speech](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/12/02.htm)). The strike failed "because conditions were not completely ripe, because of the tactics employed; but mostly cause, fundamentally, conditions were not ripe; the military force of the Revolution amounted to less than 200 men" (Ibid.). The final strike of 1958 succeeded because the armed struggle had advanced far enough to support it: "What made the maneuvers of the American embassy and of reaction disappear like candy in a school yard? Simply the general strike. It was not necessary to fire one more shot. That was the right moment to give the signal for a general strike" (Ibid.). In official Cuban terms the general strike was the crown of the insurrection, not a substitute for it.

## Victory, 1 January 1959

On 1 January 1959 Castro addressed the people at Céspedes Park in Santiago de Cuba. The speech gives the Revolution's own periodisation of the transition:

> "We have finally reached Santiago. (SHOUTING AND APPLAUSE) The road has been long and tough, but we have arrived."

> "The Revolution begins here and now. The Revolution won't be an easy task. The Revolution will be a tough undertaking and full of dangers, especially during this initial period."

> Source: [Fidel Castro, *Address at Céspedes Park*, 1 January 1959](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1959/01/01-jan-1959.htm)

The shorter formulation "The Revolution begins now" is a separate text of 3 January ([*The Revolution Begins Now*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1959/01/03.htm)). The longer address deals at length with the attempted palace coup by General Cantillo, which "could have been done right from the start, with the complete support of the people and with triumph assured right from the start, but what it did instead was to somersault into a vacuum" ([Céspedes Park address](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1959/01/01-jan-1959.htm)). It insists that "there are no vanquished persons here, because only the people have been the victors," and promises that "we will be generous with everyone ... Only the war criminals will be punished because that is the inevitable duty we have with justice ... Since there will be justice, there will be no vengeance. There will be no hatred" (Ibid.). Castro announced that Dr. Manuel Urrutia Lleó would take office as president, that Che Guevara and Camilo Cienfuegos had been ordered to advance on Havana, and that a school-city "that can hold 20,000 children" would be built. He quoted Martí, "All the glory of the world fits into a single kernel of corn," and ended: "the dead will continue to command" (Ibid.). The speech reaffirmed the Moncada pledge toward the families of the fallen soldiers: "we will be making sure that the means of support, the assistance, the education of the children of the soldiers fallen fighting against us will not be lacking, because they are not to blame for the horrors of the war" (Ibid.).

The institutional skeleton was set out by Pathfinder: "Castro was prime minister from February 1959 to 1976, when he became president of the Council of State and Council of Ministers. He was commander in chief of the armed forces from 1959 to 2008" ([Pathfinder](https://www.pathfinderpress.com/collections/fidel-castro)). The Rebel Army became the core of the revolutionary armed forces, while the 26th of July Movement, the Popular Socialist Party and other organisations were gradually merged into a single party structure.

## Agrarian reform and the literacy campaign

The first major law of the new government was the Agrarian Reform of 17 May 1959, preserved in Castro's speech on its promulgation ([*On the promulgation of the Agrarian Law*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1959/05/17.htm)). Its declarations were conciliatory in tone: "We do not make laws by hate, as we do not hate anybody. We understand perfectly that we are a consequence of the past, we have the obligation to correct past mistakes" and "It is criminal that there be uncultivated land in a country where people is hungry" [sic] (Ibid.). Castro also forbade spontaneous seizures: "The Agrarian Law does not mean of course that a man has a right to grab the piece of land he likes, that anybody has the right to distribute land by his own initiative. It does not mean disorder" (Ibid.). He told the United Nations in 1960: "Without an agrarian reform, our country would have been unable to take that step; we made an agrarian reform. Was it a radical agrarian reform? We think not. It was a reform adjusted to the needs of our development" ([1960 UN address](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1960/09/26.htm)).

The Revolution presented the 1959 law as the fulfilment of the Moncada programme, but the two were not identical, and the differences matter. The second Moncada law proposed indemnity calculated on the rent the land would have earned over ten years; the 1959 law paid compensation in twenty-year bonds bearing 4.5 percent interest. The fifth Moncada point concerned the confiscation of assets gained by fraud under earlier regimes, not land redistribution. The claim of direct fulfilment belongs to the Revolution's own presentation of its history, not to a comparison of the texts. What the law did do was break the **latifundio**, the system of large landed estates, and reduce foreign ownership of large areas of farmland. Independent historians have argued over how far the reform was shaped by the political need to respond to United States pressure. Farber, writing from a democratic socialist position, treats the agrarian question as part of a wider argument about who controlled the revolution and whether the new state ever created genuine worker and peasant power ([Samuel Farber, *Cuba Since the Revolution of 1959: A Critical Assessment*, 2011](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/reviews/capsule-review/2012-01-01/cuba-revolution-1959-critical-assessment)).

The nationalisations followed a similar official logic of response rather than abstract design. In his 1960 UN address Castro listed a fifty-percent rent reduction, the cancellation of the Telephone Company's concessions, lower electricity rates, the Agrarian Reform Law and a mining law imposing a 25 percent tax on mineral exports ([1960 UN address](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1960/09/26.htm)). "We were not 100 per cent communist yet (LAUGHS) We were just becoming slightly pink. We did not confiscate land; we simply proposed to pay for it in twenty years" (Ibid.). At the mass meeting of 2 September 1960 he announced the nationalisation of "36 centrales azucareros, la compañía eléctrica, la compañía de teléfonos y las compañías petroleras" and warned: "Si continúan las agresiones económicas contra nuestro país, continuaremos nacionalizando las empresas norteamericanas" ([*Declaración de La Habana*, 2 September 1960](http://www.cuba.cu/gobierno/discursos/1960/esp/f020960e.html)).

The literacy campaign of 1961 is a well documented case of a revolutionary reform reaching ordinary people. Castro's May Day speech describes "the volunteer teachers paraded and also representatives of the 100,000 young people on their way to the interior to wipe out illiteracy" ([*May Day Celebration*, 1 May 1961](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/05/01.htm)). The baseline he gave at the UN was stark: "Thirty-seven and one half percent of our population were illiterate; 70 per cent of the rural children had no teachers" ([1960 UN address](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1960/09/26.htm)). Independent studies broadly accept that the campaign sharply reduced illiteracy, while stressing that it was also a political exercise. Arlo Kempf records that in 1961 more than 120,000 volunteer teachers worked alongside roughly 148,000 trained teachers, reducing illiteracy from almost 24 percent to 3.9 percent, and that about 30 percent of the volunteers were Black or mixed race ([Arlo Kempf, "The Cuban Literacy Campaign at 50," *Critical Education* 5(4), 2014](https://ices.library.ubc.ca/index.php/criticaled/article/download/183269/184389)). Jonathan Kozol's study in the *Harvard Educational Review*, based on interviews conducted in Cuba and on publications then unavailable in the United States, documented the logistics and the frankly political intent of the teaching methods, and closed with personal accounts from two young volunteers ([Jonathan Kozol, "A New Look at the Literacy Campaign in Cuba," *Harvard Educational Review* 48(3), 1978](https://www.harvardeducationalreview.org/content/48/3/341)). Rachel Herman has shown that women volunteers, many of them teenagers sent from the cities to live with peasant families, used the campaign to challenge traditional gender norms even though the male-led revolution did not create space for an independent feminist movement ([Rachel Herman, "An Army of Educators: Gender, Revolution and the Cuban Literacy Campaign of 1961," *Gender & History* 24(1), 2012](https://web.archive.org/web/20230221022303/https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1468-0424.2011.01670.x)).

The voices of the volunteers themselves survive in oral histories. Joanne Elvy interviewed women who had served as *brigadistas* and recorded one explanation of why the campaign mattered: "This Campaign was the first major task of the Revolution, one that invited the people to become involved, en masse, to resolve challenges faced by the country. And for this reason, this Campaign held great value: social value, economic value, political value ... Indeed, the formation of our consciousness" ([Joanne Elvy, "Women in the Cuban Literacy Campaign," *Literacies* 7, 2007](http://literacies.ca/literacies/7-2007/pdf/elvy.pdf)). Rosa Hernández Acosta, who taught in rural Cuba without electricity or running water, recalled that many of the volunteers were young women from Havana and other cities, and that the experience changed them: "All of them were no doubt motivated by the experience they had during the Campaign. It was a tremendous experience for everyone involved" ([interview with Rosa Hernández Acosta, JSTOR Daily, 2021](https://daily.jstor.org/rosa-hernandez-acosta-on-the-cuban-literacy-campaign)). These testimonies establish what the official statistics cannot: that the campaign was lived by young people, often women, who went to places they had never seen and came back changed. They also carry the limits of retrospective memory and of interviews conducted decades later.

## Socialism declared

The official chronology of the socialist character of the Revolution has two anchor dates. On 16 April 1961, at the funeral farewell for those killed in the bombing of Cuban air bases, Castro proclaimed the socialist character of the Revolution. Raúl Castro's 2021 Central Report opens with that exact statement, calling it "The 60th anniversary of the proclamation by Comandante en jefe Fidel Castro Ruz of the socialist character of the Revolution, on April 16, 1961" ([Raúl Castro, *Central Report to the Eighth Congress of the Communist Party of Cuba*, 16 April 2021](https://en.granma.cu/cuba/2021-04-22/central-report-to-the-eighth-congress-of-the-communist-party-of-cuba)). On 1 May 1961 Castro declared the new constitutional project: "That constitution has been left behind by this revolution, which, as we have said, is a socialist revolution. We must talk of a new constitution, yes, a new constitution, but not a bourgeois constitution ... The new social system is called socialism, and this constitution will therefore be a socialist constitution" ([*May Day Celebration*, 1 May 1961](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/05/01.htm)). On 2 December 1961 he made his personal ideological declaration:

> "What is the socialism we have to apply here? Utopian socialism? We simply have to apply scientific socialism. That is why I began by saying with complete frankness that we believe in Marxism, that we believe it is the most correct, the most scientific theory, the only truly revolutionary theory. I say that here with complete satisfaction and with complete confidence: I am a Marxist-Leninist, and I shall be a Marxist-Leninist to the end of my life."

> Source: [*Speech on Marxism-Leninism*, 2 December 1961](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/12/02.htm)

"Scientific socialism" was Castro's term for the Marxist claim that socialism follows from the analysis of material conditions rather than from moral wishful thinking. In the same address he gave the theoretical reason for a single revolutionary party: "What 26th of July program are we going to carry out unless it is a Marxist-Leninist program? Why should we carry out two Marxist-Leninist programs?" (Ibid.). The party programme would be "a Marxist-Leninist program conforming to the specific objective conditions of our country" (Ibid.).

The **First Declaration of Havana** was promulgated by the National General Assembly of the People at the Plaza de la República on 2 September 1960. The complete text is preserved by the Cuban government ([*Declaración de La Habana*](http://www.cuba.cu/gobierno/discursos/1960/esp/f020960e.html)). Castro framed the assembly as an act of popular sovereignty: "el pueblo es soberano, es decir que la soberanía radica en el pueblo y que de él dimanan todos los poderes" and "¡aquí se ha reunido hoy el pueblo!" (Ibid.). Its nine points condemned the Declaration of San José de Costa Rica, denounced a century of United States intervention, rejected the Monroe Doctrine, accepted Soviet assistance in case of invasion, denied Soviet or Chinese responsibility for the Cuban Revolution, defined democracy in anti-oligarchic and anti-racist terms and proclaimed a catalogue of social rights. Two passages define the Revolution's self-image:

> "La Asamblea General Nacional del Pueblo de Cuba condena, en fin, la explotación del hombre por el hombre, y la explotación de los países subdesarrollados por el capital financiero imperialista."

> "El derecho de los campesinos a la tierra; el derecho del obrero al fruto de su trabajo; el derecho de los niños a la educación; el derecho de los enfermos a la asistencia médica y hospitalaria; el derecho de los jóvenes al trabajo; el derecho de los estudiantes a la enseñanza libre, experimental y científica; el derecho de los negros y los indios a la 'dignidad plena del hombre'; el derecho de la mujer a la igualdad civil, social y política; el derecho del anciano a una vejez segura; ... el derecho de los Estados a la nacionalización de los monopolios imperialistas ...; el derecho de las naciones a su plena soberanía."

> Source: [*Declaración de La Habana*](http://www.cuba.cu/gobierno/discursos/1960/esp/f020960e.html) (translation mine)

The document closes with the phrase that became the Revolution's signature: "Aquí está hoy Cuba para ratificar, ante América Latina y ante el mundo, como un compromiso histórico, su dilema irrenunciable: Patria o Muerte" (Ibid.; "Here is Cuba today to ratify, before Latin America and the world, as a historic commitment, its irrevocable dilemma: Homeland or Death").

The **Second Declaration of Havana** was approved on 4 February 1962, in the immediate wake of the Punta del Este conference ([*Segunda Declaración de La Habana*](http://www.cuba.cu/gobierno/discursos/1962/esp/f040262e.html)). It links Marxian political economy, the Cuban experience of guerrilla war and the claim that revolution becomes possible when material conditions have matured. Its central thesis is known worldwide:

> "En muchos países de América Latina la revolución es hoy inevitable. Ese hecho no lo determina la voluntad de nadie; está determinado por las espantosas condiciones de explotación en que vive el hombre americano, el desarrollo de la conciencia revolucionaria de las masas, la crisis mundial del imperialismo y el movimiento universal de lucha de los pueblos subyugados."

> Source: [*Segunda Declaración de La Habana*](http://www.cuba.cu/gobierno/discursos/1962/esp/f040262e.html) (translation mine: "In many countries of Latin America revolution is inevitable today. That fact is determined by no one's will; it is determined by the frightful conditions of exploitation in which the American human being lives, the development of the revolutionary consciousness of the masses, the world crisis of imperialism and the universal movement of struggle of the subjugated peoples.")

The document explains hostility to Cuba as fear of a continental revolution: "Lo explica el miedo. No el miedo a la Revolución Cubana; el miedo a la revolución latinoamericana" (Ibid.). It presents revolution as a midwife: "La revolución es en la historia como el médico que asiste el nacimiento de una nueva vida. No usa sin necesidad los aparatos de fuerza, pero los usa sin vacilaciones cada vez que sea necesario para ayudar al parto" (Ibid., translation mine). It ends: "Porque esta gran humanidad ha dicho ¡Basta! y ha echado a andar. Y su marcha de gigantes ya no se detendrá hasta conquistar la verdadera independencia ... ¡Patria o Muerte! ¡Venceremos!" (Ibid.). The "duty of every revolutionary" formula appears in Castro's elaboration at the Tricontinental Conference in 1966: "as the Havana Declaration says, the duty of every revolutionary is to effect the revolution, and effect it in deed not in word" ([*At the Closing Session of the Tricontinental Conference*, 15 January 1966](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1966/01/15.htm)).

## Playa Girón and the October Crisis

**Playa Girón** is the Cuban name for the Bay of Pigs invasion of April 1961, named for the beach where the main fighting occurred. Castro's own extended account is the address of 23 April 1961, delivered four days after the victory ([*We must defend our country*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/04/23.htm)). "On Monday morning at 3:15 I was informed that fighting was going on at Playa Giron and Playa Larga" (Ibid.). His command assessment joins the military and the political: "Our duty as a soldier in the trenches is to defend our country. All our spirit, all our thoughts, all our energy should be concentrated on this history-making period" (Ibid.). The casualty figures he gave are part of the official record: "On 19 April there were losses ... There were 87 dead on our side and 250 wounded ... It is possible that the dead on our side will amount to 100" (Ibid.). Raúl Castro's 2021 report gives the official retrospective: "the 60th anniversary of our victory over the mercenary expedition, achieved in less than 72 hours by Rebel Army, police and militia forces who, under the personal leadership of compañero Fidel, did not give the invaders a moment's rest" ([Central Report](https://en.granma.cu/cuba/2021-04-22/central-report-to-the-eighth-congress-of-the-communist-party-of-cuba)). The May Day address develops the political meaning: "A revolution expressing the will of the people is an election everyday, not every four years; it is a constant meeting with the people, like this meeting" ([*May Day Celebration*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/05/01.htm)). On the invaders' class composition: "About 800 came from well-to-do families. They had a total of 27,556 caballerias of land, 9,666 houses, 70 industries, 10 sugar centrals, 2 banks, and 5 mines" (Ibid.).

The **October Crisis**, known outside Cuba as the Cuban Missile Crisis, is the subject of Castro's televised interview of 2 November 1962, in which he read the shorthand record of his two meetings with UN Secretary-General U Thant ([*U.N. Demands of Cuba*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1962/11/02.htm)). The document sets out the five demands: an end to the economic blockade and pressure, cessation of subversive activities and mercenary invasions, an end to pirate air attacks, an end to violations of Cuban airspace and territorial waters, and withdrawal of the United States naval base at Guantánamo. It refuses on-site inspection as a violation of sovereignty: "I believe that this question of inspection is one more attempt to humiliate our country, therefore we do not accept it. ... The path of peace is not the path of sacrificing the rights of peoples, of violating the rights of peoples; that is precisely the path that leads to war" (Ibid.). Castro stated the property question in strictly sovereign terms: "when the Soviet Government decided to withdraw those weapons, which belonged to it, we respected that decision" (Ibid.).

This is Cuba's case as Castro presented it. It is not the whole story of the crisis. Graham Allison's *Essence of Decision*, first published in 1971 and revised with Philip Zelikow in 1999, made the episode a central case study in how governments decide, using three models of decision making: the rational actor model, which treats the state as a single calculating agent; the organisational process model, which explains outcomes by the routines of bureaucracies; and the governmental politics model, which explains them by bargaining among officials ([Graham T. Allison and Philip Zelikow, *Essence of Decision*](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/27993/chapter/211707775); [Barton J. Bernstein's review essay](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/447720/summary)). Allison's work does not settle the Cuban claims, but it shows why a speech by one head of government, however detailed, cannot by itself establish how the crisis was decided in Washington or Moscow. Hugh Thomas's narrative places the confrontation within the longer history of United States relations with Cuba ([Thomas](https://archive.org/details/cubapursuitoffre0000unse)). The official Cuban account remains essential for understanding what the Revolution thought it was defending and what it demanded.

## Institutionalising the Revolution

The 1961 Marxism-Leninism speech explains the necessity of a merger: "you cannot make a revolution, and above all, you cannot carry a revolution forward without a strong and disciplined organization" ([1961 speech](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1961/12/02.htm)). The sequence was the Integrated Revolutionary Organizations, or ORI, then the United Party of the Socialist Revolution, or PURS, then the Communist Party of Cuba, or PCC. In the speech of 3 October 1965 presenting the first Central Committee, Castro narrated the last step:

> "First we were the ORI (Spanish language acronym for Integrated Revolutionary Organizations), during the first stages in the unification of the Revolutionary forces ... Then we were the United Party of the Socialist Revolution, which represented extraordinary progress, an extraordinary advance in the creation of our political apparatus ... what, in your opinion, is the name our party should have? ... The Cuban Communist Party! ... our first Central Committee adopted yesterday."

> Source: [*Speech during the presentation of the Central Committee of the Cuban Communist Party*, 3 October 1965](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1965/10/03-oct-1965.htm)

The same speech announced the founding of *Granma*, "a new daily newspaper which will bear the name of GRANMA, the symbol of our Revolutionary concept and of our path" (Ibid.). Castro's 1965 characterisation of the party is a primary statement of what the constitution would later formalise: "the government of a party, the party of the workers, made up of the best workers, formed with the full participation of the masses, so it can justly and rightly be said that it is the vanguard of the workers" (Ibid.). Here **vanguard** means the politically conscious minority that claims to lead the wider working class. The party's own statutes, as cited in Raúl Castro's 2021 report, define the PCC as "the continuation of the Cuban Revolutionary Party, created by Martí to lead the struggle for independence, of the First Communist Party founded by Carlos Baliño and Julio Antonio Mella, and the party that emerged from the voluntary integration of the three revolutionary organizations that led the struggle against the Batista dictatorship" ([Central Report](https://en.granma.cu/cuba/2021-04-22/central-report-to-the-eighth-congress-of-the-communist-party-of-cuba)).

The 1966 Tricontinental closing speech is a central statement of Cuban internationalism: "Our country is a small one; our territory could even be partially occupied by the enemy; but that would never mean a cessation of our resistance. ... for the Cuban revolutionaries the field of battle against imperialism takes in the whole world" ([*Tricontinental Conference closing speech*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1966/01/15.htm)). Castro's tribute to Che on 18 October 1967 made the same point through the figure of Guevara: "No man like him in these times has taken the proletarian internationalist spirit to its highest level!" ([*In Tribute to Che Guevara*](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1967/10/oct-18-1967.htm)). He defended the relevance of Guevara's ideas twenty years later: "many of Che's ideas are absolutely relevant today" ([*Che's ideas are absolutely relevant today*, 8 October 1987](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1987/10/08.htm)).

The 1976 Constitution gave this settlement legal form. The National Assembly's official history states that "A inicios de 1959 se hace la Ley Fundamental. A partir de 1976 se produce el periodo de institucionalización del país. La Constitución de 1976 es una Carta Magna socialista, define el carácter socialista de la Revolución cubana," and that the draft was discussed by "más de seis millones de personas" before a referendum of 15 February 1976 in which, according to the official figures, 98 percent of electors voted and 97.7 percent voted in favour ([National Assembly](https://www.parlamentocubano.gob.cu/index.php/historia-constitucional-cubana)). These are official figures reported by the state's own legislative body, not independently audited results. The same source quotes the historian Eduardo Torres Cuevas describing the text as "martiana y por su ejecución y concepción es fidelista. El marxismo-leninismo está dentro de su propia proyección" (Ibid.; "Martí-inspired, and in its execution and conception it is Fidelista. Marxism-Leninism is within its own projection"). Raúl Castro's 2021 report specifies that "the complete wording of Article 5 ... was the personal work of Comandante en jefe Fidel Castro Ruz," establishing the Communist Party of Cuba as the superior leading force of society and the state ([Central Report](https://en.granma.cu/cuba/2021-04-22/central-report-to-the-eighth-congress-of-the-communist-party-of-cuba)).

The 2002 reform inscribed "el carácter irrevocable del socialismo y del sistema político y social revolucionario" ([National Assembly](https://www.parlamentocubano.gob.cu/index.php/historia-constitucional-cubana)). Samuel Farber's critical assessment, written by a historian sympathetic to democratic socialism, argues that the centralisation confirmed by these arrangements was not a temporary response to siege but a durable feature of the Cuban state, and that the regime's record on political dissidents, Black Cubans and gay people cannot be set aside because of its achievements in health and education ([Farber, *Cuba Since the Revolution of 1959*](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/reviews/capsule-review/2012-01-01/cuba-revolution-1959-critical-assessment); [New Politics review](https://newpol.org/review/cuba-different/)). That judgement is contested by other left-wing writers, who argue that Farber underestimates the effect of the United States embargo and the popular content of the reforms ([Links International Journal of Socialist Renewal](https://links.org.au/system-or-siege-samuel-farber-misses-main-cause-cubas-problems)). The disagreement is a genuine one about evidence and emphasis, not a matter of quoting one side against the other.

## The Special Period and the later years

The **Special Period** was the name given to the economic emergency declared after the collapse of the Soviet Union cut off subsidies, oil and trade. Castro's 2005 University of Havana address, revisited by *Granma* in 2026, gives the official account:

> "Nearly twenty-five years had passed since the collapse of the USSR and the most arduous years of the 'Special Period.' Cuba—defying all predictions from both friends and adversaries—remained standing, in the same geographical location, still facing existential threats and powerful enemies."

> Source: [Granma, *Fidel and the Oral Essay on Life*, 17 November 2026](https://en.granma.cu/cuba/2026-11-17/fidel-and-the-oral-essay-on-life), recalling Castro's 17 November 2005 speech

The last reflection listed on the first page of the *Granma* archive is dated October 2016, and the party paper continues to maintain the archive under the heading "Articles and Reflections by Fidel" ([Granma, *Reflections of Fidel*](https://en.granma.cu/reflections-of-fidel)). In the 2005 speech Castro posed the question that preoccupied the late Revolution: "Are revolutions destined to collapse, or can human beings cause them to collapse? Can people, can society, prevent revolutions from collapsing, or not?" ([Granma](https://en.granma.cu/cuba/2026-11-17/fidel-and-the-oral-essay-on-life)).

The human cost of those years is well documented and does not appear in Castro's speech. Between 1989 and 1993 Cuba's economy contracted by a third or more, imports of fuel, food and spare parts collapsed, and transport and electricity supplies failed for long periods ([Jorge Pérez-López, "The Cuban Economic Crisis of the 1990s and the External Sector," ASCE, 1998](https://ascecubadatabase.org/asce_proceedings/the-cuban-economic-crisis-of-the-1990s-and-the-external-sector)). Carmelo Mesa-Lago's long-term study in the *CEPAL Review* found that almost all social indicators deteriorated in the first half of the 1990s, that rationing in Havana covered only about half of recommended calorie intake, and that by 2003 some indicators had still not regained their 1989 levels ([Carmelo Mesa-Lago, "Social and Economic Problems in Cuba during the Crisis and Subsequent Recovery," *CEPAL Review* 86, 2005](https://repositorio.cepal.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/3cd9b743-ba48-4d96-b9a9-b92185af21e2/content)). Efrén Córdova documented the effects on workers, including unemployment, idled factories and reduced wages ([Efrén Córdova, "The Situation of Cuban Workers during the Special Period in Peacetime," ASCE, 1996](https://ascecubadatabase.org/asce_proceedings/the-situation-of-cuban-workers-during-the-special-period-in-peacetime/)). In the summer of 1994 more than 30,000 people left on homemade rafts, and many died at sea ([FIU Law Review, 2023](https://ecollections.law.fiu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1569&context=lawreview)). A scholarly study based on interviews in Havana records how ordinary people remembered the period: "People's memories of the early 1990s are invariably colored by hunger and fear. Buses stopped running, and there were rotating blackouts that left people without power for up to 16 hours at a time." One resident drew a distinction that the official statistics could not express: "There is hunger in Cuba, but not hunger hunger" ([Wilson dissertation, University of California, 2011](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4zn2f4d0/qt4zn2f4d0.pdf)). That testimony establishes what survival meant at household level. It does not prove that the government's policies were the sole cause, since the loss of Soviet trade was external, but it does show that the official language of dignity and standing had a cost that others paid.

The **Update** was the name given to the economic reforms begun under Raúl Castro after 2011, and the **Conceptualization of the Cuban Economic and Social Model** was the document that stated their theoretical justification. Raúl Castro's 2021 Central Report records that the Sixth Congress of 2011 initiated the *Lineamientos*, or Guidelines, and that the Seventh Congress of 2016 revised them, "maintain[ing] 17 Guidelines, modify[ing] 165, delet[ing] 92 and add[ing] 18, for a total of 200." The guiding formula was "an appropriate combination of centralized planning and decentralization, with the necessary autonomy at intermediate and basic levels of the enterprise system and local governments." The stated limit was that "the ownership by the entire people of the fundamental means of production constitutes the foundation of workers' real power," and that "there are limits that we cannot exceed because the consequences would be irreversible and lead to strategic errors, the very destruction of socialism and therefore, our national sovereignty and independence" ([Central Report](https://en.granma.cu/cuba/2021-04-22/central-report-to-the-eighth-congress-of-the-communist-party-of-cuba)). The report also describes the *Tarea Ordenamiento*, or Reordering Task, begun on 1 January 2021, which unified currencies and exchange rates and reformed salaries and subsidies. It insists that "in Cuba, the implementation of shock therapies that hurt the most humble strata of the population will never be allowed; no one will be left unprotected" (Ibid.). Farber's 2011 book, reviewed in 2012 and therefore predating these 2021 measures, reads the reforms of that period as a turn toward a Sino-Vietnamese model of state capitalism joined to political authoritarianism, with military and party officials as the likely beneficiaries ([Foreign Affairs review of Farber](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/reviews/capsule-review/2012-01-01/cuba-revolution-1959-critical-assessment)).

The **rectification process**, the official name for the 1980s course correction away from some market mechanisms and back toward political and moral mobilisation, was announced by Castro in 1986 and shaped the decade's speeches ([Castro Internet Archive index](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/index.htm)). The 2019 Constitution was approved by referendum on 24 February 2019 and proclaimed on 10 April, according to the official figures with 90.15 percent participation and 78.30 percent of all eligible citizens voting in favour ([National Assembly](https://www.parlamentocubano.gob.cu/index.php/historia-constitucional-cubana)). The Eighth Congress was held from 16 to 19 April 2021 with 300 delegates representing more than 700,000 party members ([Central Report](https://en.granma.cu/cuba/2021-04-22/central-report-to-the-eighth-congress-of-the-communist-party-of-cuba)). Castro's last years also included a recurring complaint about his own public image. In the interviews with Ignacio Ramonet he said: "in this country there's not a single school, factory, hospital or building that carries my name. Nor are there statues, there practically aren't even pictures of me. Here we don't do official portraits" ([excerpt from *My Life, a Spoken Autobiography*](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/socialist-viewpoint-us/marapr_08/marapr_08_23.html)).

## Coda

The primary record and the scholarly record answer different questions. Castro's texts show how the Revolution explained itself: as the restoration of violated sovereignty, as a social programme of land, education and health, as a socialist project that was always a process rather than a finished state, and as a claim to continental and international reach. In the 1962 university speech he drew the distinction himself: "The revolution is socialist. We are socialists but we are building socialism. The society itself is not socialist although the majority of the people may be so" ([*The Revolution Must Be a School of Unfettered Thought*, 13 March 1962](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/newspape/socialist-viewpoint-us/june_02/June_02_8.html)). Historians such as Thomas, de la Cova, Farber, Kozol, Kempf, Herman, Mesa-Lago and Pérez-López ask other questions: what happened at Moncada, who controlled the revolution, what the literacy campaign actually taught, and what the crisis of the 1990s cost. Their answers disagree with each other and often with the official narrative. Reading the two bodies of evidence together gives a more reliable account than either alone. The documents establish what the Revolution claimed; the independent scholarship tests those claims against the experience of the people who lived through them.
