Howard Zinn wrote history from the standpoint of people usually absent from history books: workers, women, Black and Indigenous Americans, immigrants and soldiers. The phrase "history from below" is a description used by Bish Sanyal, the MIT professor who introduced Zinn at a lecture in 2005. It means telling the past through the lives of ordinary people rather than through rulers and governments. Zinn himself did not use that phrase; he and his publishers said "from the bottom up." His best-known book, *A People's History of the United States: 1492–Present*, made that approach familiar to a large audience. His refusal to present himself as a neutral historian became part of the argument about his work. This profile sets out what he argued, what the sources show and where historians objected ([what history establishes](/what-history-establishes)).

## Biography

Howard Zinn was born in Brooklyn on 24 August 1922 and died in Santa Monica on 27 January 2010. His own website describes a working-class immigrant household; the Tamiment Library finding aid for his papers adds that he was one of four sons of Edward and Jennie (Rabinowitz) Zinn. He worked from the age of fourteen, graduated from Thomas Jefferson High School and served an apprenticeship as a ship fitter at the Brooklyn Navy Yard. He joined the Army Air Corps in early 1943, trained as a bombardier and flew combat and humanitarian missions in Europe. He married Roslyn Shechter in 1944. Under the GI Bill he took a bachelor's degree at New York University in 1951, then an MA at Columbia in 1952 and a PhD in 1958; the Tamiment finding aid records a post-doctoral year in East Asian Studies at Harvard in 1960–61 ([biography](https://www.howardzinn.org/about/biography/); [Tamiment finding aid](http://dlib.nyu.edu/findingaids/html/tamwag/tam_542/)).

He taught part time at Upsala College in New Jersey from 1953 to 1956, then moved to Spelman College in Atlanta in 1956 as chair of history and social sciences. Spelman was a historically Black college for women. There Zinn became involved in the civil rights movement, advised the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), wrote for *The Nation* and *Harper's*, and mentored students including Marian Wright Edelman, Alice Walker and Bernice Johnson Reagon. Spelman's president dismissed him in 1963. Zinn recalled the reason in his own words: "I was fired for insubordination. Which happened to be true." He took a post at Boston University, where he taught until retiring in 1988 and became a leading critic of the Vietnam War. In 2005 Spelman invited him to deliver the commencement address and awarded him an honorary doctorate. His awards included the Lannan Foundation Literary Award for Nonfiction, the Eugene V. Debs Award and the Ridenhour Courage Prize ([biography](https://www.howardzinn.org/about/biography/); [Tamiment finding aid](http://dlib.nyu.edu/findingaids/html/tamwag/tam_542/)).

One date is unsettled. Zinn's own bibliography dates *You Can't Be Neutral on a Moving Train* to Beacon Press, 1994, with later editions in 2002 and 2018, and Beacon currently lists a 2002 paperback and a 2025 cloth edition. The Tamiment finding aid calls it a "1995 autobiography." That discrepancy is not resolved here ([bibliography](https://howardzinn.org/bibliography); [Beacon paperback](https://beacon.org/You-Cant-Be-Neutral-on-a-Moving-Train-P418.aspx); [Beacon cloth](https://www.beacon.org/You-Cant-Be-Neutral-on-a-Moving-Train-P2498.aspx); [Tamiment finding aid](http://dlib.nyu.edu/findingaids/html/tamwag/tam_542/)).

## Intellectual tradition

Zinn belonged to the [New Left](/new-left) and to the tradition of history written from the side of ordinary people, but he stood apart from the professional institutions of radical history. In a 2013 essay the Rutgers historian David Greenberg noted that Zinn never wrote for *Studies on the Left* or *Radical History Review* and, according to Martin Duberman's biography, did not join the Radical Historians Caucus of the American Historical Association. Zinn's own writing explains why. In a 1999 book-festival talk he said he "didn't want to be a historian who went into the past and got lost," but wanted "to see what I could find and come out and apply it to what was going on in the world" ([Greenberg](https://newrepublic.com/article/112574/howard-zinns-influential-mutilations-american-history); [Book TV transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/book-tv-peoples-history-san-francisco/)).

He rejected the label of neutral scholar. In the foreword he wrote for the 2018 edition of his memoir, quoted on his own website, he said he did not "pretend to an objectivity that was neither possible nor desirable" and told students: "You can't be neutral on a moving train. . . . Events are already moving in certain deadly directions, and to be neutral means to accept that." In the 1999 C-SPAN interview he put it more bluntly: "I was not going to be a neutral teacher. I was not going to be a neutral historian," because "to be neutral in such a world is to collaborate with what is going on." In *A Power Governments Cannot Suppress* he wrote that "writing about history, for me, is never a neutral act," and that to write only of failures would be "to make historians collaborators in an endless cycle of defeat." These are his stated commitments, not a finding that neutrality is impossible ([memoir page and Taylor foreword](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/you-cant-be-neutral-autobiography); [Book TV transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/book-tv-peoples-history-san-francisco/); ["If History Is to Be Creative"](https://howardzinn.org/collection/if-history-is-to-be-creative)).

His relation to Marxism and anarchism was selective. Greenberg, quoting Duberman, reports that Zinn found "the Marxian idea of redistributing society's wealth according to need" congenial while feeling "considerable attraction to the anarchists' anti-authoritarian stance," and that Duberman concluded Zinn "wasn't much interested in political theory." His own published work supports the breadth but not a strict system: he wrote the play *Marx in Soho*, about Marx, Jenny Marx and Bakunin, and *Emma*, about Emma Goldman, and the bibliography of *A People's History* points readers to *Monthly Review*, *Science and Society*, *Radical America* and *Socialist Revolution* alongside mainstream journals. In a 1963 debate on whether the House Un-American Activities Committee should be abolished, he carefully separated opposition to political repression from opposition to socialism: Americans did not want a communist state, he argued, "not so much that we object to a planned economy" or "to socialized medicine," but because they did not want "any government agency inspecting what we say, what we think." The wider record of repression and organising is covered in [another chapter](/labour-repression-and-organising) ([Greenberg](https://newrepublic.com/article/112574/howard-zinns-influential-mutilations-american-history); [Marx in Soho](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/marx-in-soho/); [Emma](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/emma/); [bibliography of *A People's History*](https://howardzinn.org/collection/peoples-history); [1963 HUAC debate transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/huac-debate-emory-1963/)).

## Major contributions

Zinn's central contribution was a usable one-volume narrative of United States history written from the standpoint of people usually absent from such books. His publisher's description says the book "chronicles U.S. history from the bottom up" and tells it "from the point of view of — and in the words of — America's women, factory workers, African-Americans, Native Americans, the working poor, and immigrant laborers." The chapter list shows the method in practice: "Columbus, the Indians, and Human Progress," "Drawing the Color Line," "Robber Barons and Rebels," "The Socialist Challenge," "War Is the Health of the State," "Or Does It Explode?" and "The Unreported Resistance." In the book's introduction to its bibliography, Zinn explained the documentary base. It drew on "twenty years of teaching and research in American history, and as many years of involvement in social movements," on generations of scholars of Black, Indigenous, women's and working-class history, and on non-professionals who collected material about ordinary people. He defended his sparse referencing openly: full footnotes "would have meant a book impossibly cluttered with footnotes," so he named authors and titles in the text and marked especially useful works with asterisks in a chapter-by-chapter bibliography ([book page](https://howardzinn.org/collection/peoples-history); [HarperCollins product page](https://www.harpercollins.com/products/a-peoples-history-of-the-united-states-howard-zinn)).

His second contribution was to treat movement participation as a source of historical knowledge. The SNCC Digital Gateway, a project of the SNCC Legacy Project and Duke University Libraries, records that Zinn attended SNCC's founding conference at Shaw University, became one of the "middle-aged radicals" around its young organisers, and turned down a Beacon Press commission to write about the NAACP because, as he put it, "the real story in the South today is SNCC." He collected oral histories and wrote *SNCC: The New Abolitionists*, described by the Gateway as one of the first books to discuss the organisation in its members' own words. At a 1963 SNCC meeting he pushed organisers to define their aims with questions such as "What is our economic and political goal? What kind of revolution do we want?" ([SNCC Digital Gateway](https://snccdigital.org/people/howard-zinn)).

Third, he developed an argument about how power actually works. In "If History Is to Be Creative" he wrote that "to omit these acts of resistance is to support the official view that power only rests with those who have the guns and possess the wealth," and that "people, when organised, have enormous power, more than any government." His account of political change puts movements, not institutions, at the centre: "The government of the United States did not initiate any of the things that were necessary to remedy those grievances. People had to go out and create movements in order to bring that about." In 1999 he summarised his own standard: "If history is to be creative, to anticipate a possible future without denying the past, it should, I believe, emphasize new possibilities by disclosing those hidden episodes of the past when, even if in brief flashes, people showed their ability to resist, to join together, and occasionally to win" (["If History Is to Be Creative"](https://howardzinn.org/collection/if-history-is-to-be-creative); [Book TV transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/book-tv-peoples-history-san-francisco/)).

## Analysis of capitalism

Zinn treated capitalism as a system of class power rather than as a neutral mechanism of growth. In the 1999 talk he said "you might say, I grew up class conscious" in a shipyard, where he helped organise young shipyard workers excluded from the skilled unions, alongside Black people and women whom the unions excluded. He rejected the idea that Americans were "one big happy family" with common interests: "we are society of many different interests, of people who are rich, and people who are poor, and a lot of people in between who are nervous." He applied that frame to the founding period, saying the Constitution was "written by 55 rich, white men in Philadelphia" to "perpetuate the wealth, to perpetuate slavery, to perpetuate things as they were, to replace the British ruling class with a local ruling class." He remembered being taught industrial history as a story of steel output and railroad miles, with "nothing . . . said . . . about who worked on those railroads, about the Irish immigrants and the Chinese immigrants who worked on those railroads, and died in great numbers," or about girls who entered New England textile mills at twelve ([Book TV transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/book-tv-peoples-history-san-francisco/)).

The mechanisms he emphasised were control over wealth, control over the state and control over what the public is allowed to see. "Government has mostly been on the side of the rich and powerful," he said in 1999, while adding that pressure from movements had at times forced it to act otherwise, as with Social Security in the 1930s. In "If History Is to Be Creative" he wrote that "History can disabuse us of the idea that the government's interests and the people's interests are the same," and listed the means by which elites sustain that illusion: "money, political power, the major media." His chapter titles encode the same analysis: "Robber Barons and Rebels," "The Empire and the People" and "Carter-Reagan-Bush: The Bipartisan Consensus." City Lights' description of *A Power Governments Cannot Suppress* says the book draws on "untold histories" to comment on "government dishonesty, terrorism, the wars on Iraq and Afghanistan, the loss of our liberties, immigration and the responsibility of the citizen to confront power for the common good" ([Book TV transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/book-tv-peoples-history-san-francisco/); ["If History Is to Be Creative"](https://howardzinn.org/collection/if-history-is-to-be-creative); [contents of *A People's History*](https://howardzinn.org/collection/peoples-history); [City Lights book page](https://citylights.com/open-media-series/power-governments-cannot-suppress-tos)).

He also linked militarism to economic interests. In the 2005 MIT lecture he argued that the justification for American expansion shifted from anti-communism to terrorism after 9/11 but that "there are motives deeper than the idea of fighting terrorism." In *A People's History* he asked whether the atomic bomb was dropped because "too much money and effort" had been invested in it or to impress the Soviet Union; the question is quoted in Sam Wineburg's critique. The 1999 talk shows the same suspicion of official accounts: "Never look at the surface of things," he said, describing how quiet Spelman students had been before the sit-ins ([MIT lecture transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/myth-american-exceptionalism-mit/); [Wineburg](https://historynewsnetwork.org/article/where-howard-zinns-a-peoples-history-falls-short); [Book TV transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/book-tv-peoples-history-san-francisco/)).

## Analysis of socialism

Zinn wrote no blueprint for a socialist society, and none was found in the sources opened for this profile. His positive commitments are visible in fragments. Greenberg, drawing on Duberman's biography, reports that Zinn found Marx's principle of distribution according to need attractive and was drawn to anarchist anti-authoritarianism, but concluded that Zinn had an "undeveloped political philosophy." Zinn's own *Marx in Soho* is described by its publisher as an introduction to "Marx's life, his analysis of society, and his passion for radical change," featuring Marx's wife Jenny and the anarchist Bakunin; *Emma* presents Emma Goldman, "the anarchist, feminist, and free-spirited thinker who was exiled from the United States because of her outspoken views, including her opposition to World War I." The chapter "The Socialist Challenge" in *A People's History* treats the Socialist Party, the IWW, Populists and figures such as Debs and Goldman as part of a popular tradition rather than as a state model ([Greenberg](https://newrepublic.com/article/112574/howard-zinns-influential-mutilations-american-history); [Marx in Soho](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/marx-in-soho/); [Emma](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/emma/); [contents and bibliography of *A People's History*](https://howardzinn.org/collection/peoples-history)).

His institutional preference was for direct popular action over state power. In the 2005 MIT lecture he answered a question about which institutions should uphold moral norms by saying that "institutions are all malleable, subject to interpretation," that "all these institutions depend on who has the power," and that "it takes citizen action." In 1999 he defined democracy not as "three branches of government" but as "people giving you something to drink when you're thirsty," and said that "the very name Founding Fathers" concealed the fact that the framers were slave owners. These are commitments to popular, participatory democracy and to equality, not a detailed account of ownership or planning. His 1963 HUAC statement that he objected to a communist state because of political supervision, not because of "a planned economy" or "socialized medicine," is the clearest primary statement of what he did and did not reject ([MIT lecture transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/myth-american-exceptionalism-mit/); [Book TV transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/book-tv-peoples-history-san-francisco/); [1963 HUAC debate](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/huac-debate-emory-1963/)).

## Recommended reading

These selections are starting points into Zinn's work, not the full range of his writing.

- *A People's History of the United States: 1492–Present* (1980). It tells American history from the standpoint of women, workers, Black and Indigenous Americans, the working poor and immigrants, and is the best place to see his method in practice.
- *SNCC: The New Abolitionists* (1964). It presents SNCC in its members' own words and shows why he treated movement participation as a source of historical knowledge, making it the clearest example of that method.
- *Marx in Soho: A Play on History* (1999). It introduces Marx's life and analysis of society through a play featuring Jenny Marx and Bakunin, making it the most direct guide to how he presented Marx and the anarchist tradition.
- *Emma* (2002). It presents Emma Goldman, the anarchist and feminist exiled for opposing the First World War, and is the best short guide to the anti-authoritarian current he drew on.

## Intellectual influences

Zinn's own account gives a clear chain. He described reading George Seldes' *Sawdust Caesar* and *The Brown Book of Nazi Terror* as a teenager, discussing politics with young Communists in Brooklyn, and being knocked unconscious by mounted police at a peaceful Times Square rally. "From that moment on," he recalled, "I was no longer a liberal, a believer in the self-correcting character of American democracy." He then read *The Communist Manifesto*, *The Jungle* and *The Grapes of Wrath*. His parents, themselves factory workers, had sent away for a set of Dickens; he read all twenty volumes ([biography](https://www.howardzinn.org/about/biography/)).

His shipyard years gave him what he called "a kind of class consciousness," and his move south put him under the influence of the students and organisers of the Atlanta student movement and SNCC. In the 1999 interview he said he "learned more from my years at Spelman than my students learned from me," and that he "began to read the Black historians who were not on my reading list in Columbia." He named Ella Baker as an adult adviser alongside him to SNCC and worked alongside Staughton Lynd. His publications show the pull of Emma Goldman and Karl Marx, and in "If History Is to Be Creative" he invokes examples of popular uprisings from the Philippines and Iran to Poland and South Africa ([biography](https://www.howardzinn.org/about/biography/); [Book TV transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/book-tv-peoples-history-san-francisco/); [SNCC Digital Gateway](https://snccdigital.org/people/howard-zinn); ["If History Is to Be Creative"](https://howardzinn.org/collection/if-history-is-to-be-creative)).

## Influence and legacy

HarperCollins' description says *A People's History* had been in print since 1980; Zinn's own page states that as of 2025 more than four million copies had sold in English and the book had been translated into more than a dozen languages. The Tamiment finding aid records the scale of the wider enterprise: HarperCollins marked one million copies with a New York performance in 2002; *Voices of a People's History* appeared in 2004; public readings culminated in the film *The People Speak* in 2009; a reference to the book in *Good Will Hunting* (1997) widened its readership; and the Zinn Education Project was founded in 2008 to supply teaching materials to secondary schools. The same finding aid documents the institutional record of his legacy: 86 linear feet of papers, TAM.542, acquired in 2010 with later gifts, including correspondence, drafts, lecture notes, audio and video, and archived websites ([book page](https://howardzinn.org/collection/peoples-history); [Tamiment finding aid](http://dlib.nyu.edu/findingaids/html/tamwag/tam_542/)).

The book's reach also drew organised opposition. Seven Stories Press reported in 2017 that an Arkansas state representative had proposed a bill to ban books by or about Zinn from public schools and charter schools, and linked the bill text. That is a publisher's report that the bill was proposed and opposed by the publisher; the bill itself was not opened, and the distinction between a report and a verified legislative record matters here ([Seven Stories author page](https://sevenstories.com/authors/172-howard-zinn)).

## Criticisms and controversies

**Professional historians on selection and method.** Sam Wineburg, Margaret Jacks Professor of Education and a professor of history by courtesy at Stanford, published "Undue Certainty: Where Howard Zinn's *A People's History* Falls Short" in *American Educator*, Winter 2012–2013; the reprint at History News Network, dated 18 December 2012, was opened. Wineburg accepts Zinn's right to choose an interpretation but argues that the book "relies almost entirely on secondary sources, with no archival research," is "naked of footnotes," and uses primary documents to "prop up the main text" rather than to open a new view. His detailed case study is chapter 16, on the Second World War: he says Zinn generalised about Black attitudes from a few anecdotes drawn from a single secondary source, Lawrence Wittner's *Rebels Against War*, while omitting Wittner's figures showing only about 400 Black conscientious objectors; he calls this an example of historian Aileen Kraditor's "yes-type" question. Wineburg also attacks Zinn's unqualified counterfactual that Japan "would have agreed to stop the war" had the Emperor been retained, notes that the original twenty chapters were largely unrevised between 1980 and 2003, and argues that the book's certainty models bad historical thinking for students ([Wineburg](https://historynewsnetwork.org/article/where-howard-zinns-a-peoples-history-falls-short)).

Zinn's defenders reply on the evidence. David Detmer, a professor of philosophy at Purdue University Northwest, wrote a point-by-point response hosted by HowardZinn.org. He argues that Wineburg's claim that Zinn "extinguishes" qualifiers is wrong: *perhaps* appears over a hundred times in the text and *seem/seems/seemed* far more; that *A People's History* does have a twenty-page chapter-by-chapter bibliography and short author-title citations in the text; and that Wineburg omitted the United States Strategic Bombing Survey finding, quoted by Zinn, that Japan would probably have surrendered before 1 November 1945 even without the bombs or Soviet entry. Detmer also disputes Wineburg's account of the Black-war attitudes evidence, saying Zinn gave five items on the cited page, not three, and used the qualifier "seemed to be." Both essays should be read together. Neither side of this exchange was written by a specialist in the wartime Japan decision, and Detmer's page is a rebuttal published by the subject's own website ([Detmer](https://howardzinn.org/history-distorted-sam-wineburgs-critique)).

David Greenberg, a professor of journalism and media studies and of history at Rutgers, reviewed Martin Duberman's biography of Zinn in *The New Republic* on 19 March 2013 under the title "Agit-Prof." Greenberg accepts that Zinn's book gave readers "the American experience as seen by the losers and the victims" and that the new left transformed the profession, but argues that the "orthodox version" Zinn attacked was already being dismantled by professional historians in the 1970s. He quotes Zinn's declaration that because "selection, simplification, \[and\] emphasis" are inevitable, the historian's choices are "tools for contending social classes, races, nations," and calls this a rejection of empirical responsibility. He quotes Duberman's judgements that the book at times "lacks nuance," with the world "divided into oppressors and oppressed, villains or heroes," and that Zinn was a "lazy, conventional theorist" with an "undeveloped political philosophy." Greenberg also reports that reviewers Michael Kammen and Oscar Handlin panned the original book while Eric Foner was more favourable; those reviews are known only through Greenberg's and Wineburg's citations, not directly ([Greenberg](https://newrepublic.com/article/112574/howard-zinns-influential-mutilations-american-history)).

Other named critics were not opened. Michael Kazin's "Howard Zinn's History Lessons," *Dissent* 51, no. 2 (Spring 2004), pp. 81–85, is cited by both Wineburg and Greenberg. Wineburg reports that Kazin called the book "unworthy of such fame and influence" and explained its success as consolation for an American left that had fared badly since 1980; Greenberg reports the phrase "a certain consolation." The full *Dissent* article returned a 403 error when it was tried, so this is second-hand. Wineburg's endnotes also name Kammen's "How the Other Half Lived" (*Washington Post Book World*, 23 March 1980), Handlin's "Arawaks" (*American Scholar* 49, no. 4, Autumn 1980, 546–550) and Foner's "Majority Report" (*New York Times*, 2 March 1980); they were not opened. An attempt to read "The Second Worst History Book in Print?: Rethinking *A People's History of the United States*" at Project MUSE met a verification challenge, so no claim is made about its contents beyond the title and the abstract snippet returned in search results. Under the [editorial source policy](/editorial-source-policy), none of these is treated as a directly examined source.

There are disputes within Zinn's own work. He conceded gaps in the 1999 C-SPAN interview: "when the first edition of the book came out, I really didn't do justice to the struggles of Chicano people in this country," or to gay and lesbian people or disabled people. He promised to note the Deaf President Now episode for a later printing. Wineburg treats the near-identical text of the original twenty chapters across editions as a failure to engage new scholarship; the two points are related but not the same ([Book TV transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/book-tv-peoples-history-san-francisco/); [Wineburg](https://historynewsnetwork.org/article/where-howard-zinns-a-peoples-history-falls-short)).

Some biographical points remain disputed or are reported at second hand. The Spelman dismissal is documented in Zinn's own account, his biographer's account as summarised by Greenberg, and correspondence held at Tamiment and the Wisconsin Historical Society; the details of the incident Greenberg mentions were not independently verified. Zinn's bombing of Royan and his later opposition to war are described on his own biography page and in the 1999 interview. His response to Morton Sobell's 2008 admission is reported by Wineburg from a *New York Times* podcast; the underlying article was not opened. Seven Stories Press' account of the proposed Arkansas ban is a publisher's report, not a legislative record examined here ([biography](https://www.howardzinn.org/about/biography/); [Greenberg](https://newrepublic.com/article/112574/howard-zinns-influential-mutilations-american-history); [Wineburg](https://historynewsnetwork.org/article/where-howard-zinns-a-peoples-history-falls-short); [Seven Stories](https://sevenstories.com/authors/172-howard-zinn)).

One phrase often associated with this debate cannot be verified as Zinn's own wording. Searches of HowardZinn.org did not produce "the accepted version of the past," and the material that surfaced elsewhere led to tertiary sources this project excludes. The wording that can be checked is his statement that writing history "is never a neutral act," his refusal to be "a neutral historian," his description of writing "from a class conscious point of view," and his account of looking at events "from the standpoint of" workers, slaves, Native Americans, GIs and enemy civilians, all recorded in the research dossier (["If History Is to Be Creative"](https://howardzinn.org/collection/if-history-is-to-be-creative); [Book TV transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/book-tv-peoples-history-san-francisco/)).

## Related thinkers

Noam Chomsky was a contemporary and fellow antiwar critic; Sasha Lilley interviewed Zinn and Chomsky together in *Theory and Practice* (PM Press, 2010). The Tamiment finding aid quotes Chomsky calling *A People's History* Zinn's "enduring masterpiece" and describing its message about "the people who remain unknown" ([bibliography](https://howardzinn.org/bibliography); [Tamiment](http://dlib.nyu.edu/findingaids/html/tamwag/tam_542/)). Staughton Lynd was a Spelman colleague and fellow Vietnam critic, cited throughout Zinn's bibliography and present at the 1963 Emory debate; Zinn's bibliography includes Lynd's work on class conflict and the Constitution ([SNCC Digital Gateway](https://snccdigital.org/people/howard-zinn); [HUAC debate transcript](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/huac-debate-emory-1963/); [bibliography of *A People's History*](https://howardzinn.org/collection/peoples-history)). Ella Baker is named by Zinn's biography and the SNCC Digital Gateway as an adult adviser to SNCC alongside Zinn ([biography](https://www.howardzinn.org/about/biography/); [SNCC Digital Gateway](https://snccdigital.org/people/howard-zinn)).

Karl Marx and Mikhail Bakunin are the subjects of *Marx in Soho*, and Zinn's attraction to Marx's distribution principle and to anarchist anti-authoritarianism is reported by Greenberg quoting Duberman ([Marx in Soho](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/marx-in-soho/); [Greenberg](https://newrepublic.com/article/112574/howard-zinns-influential-mutilations-american-history)). Emma Goldman is the subject of Zinn's play *Emma*, described as an anarchist, feminist and opponent of the First World War ([Emma](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/emma/)). Anthony Arnove co-edited *Voices of a People's History* with Zinn, wrote the introduction to the 35th-anniversary edition of *A People's History* and wrote the foreword to the 2026 expanded edition of *SNCC* ([bibliography](https://howardzinn.org/bibliography); [book page](https://howardzinn.org/collection/peoples-history); [Haymarket](https://www.haymarketbooks.org/books/533-sncc)). Keeanga-Yamahtta Taylor wrote the foreword to the 2018 edition of the memoir, quoting Zinn's rejection of objectivity ([memoir page](https://www.howardzinn.org/collection/you-cant-be-neutral-autobiography)). Sam Wineburg, David Greenberg and Michael Kazin are the historians whose criticisms are treated above; they disagree with Zinn's method and with one another about the causes and value of his influence ([Wineburg](https://historynewsnetwork.org/article/where-howard-zinns-a-peoples-history-falls-short); [Greenberg](https://newrepublic.com/article/112574/howard-zinns-influential-mutilations-american-history)). [Michael Parenti](/michael-parenti) was grouped with Zinn as a popular left historian, but their methods differ and were not compared in the sources opened here.
