On the first of May 1926, in the pit village of Pendleton, a miner's wife told a reporter from the *Manchester Evening News* what a stoppage meant in her kitchen: "it is the women who have to find and put the dumplings in the stew." (1 May 1926, reproduced by the North West Labour History Society; [Source: nwlh.org.uk](https://www.nwlh.org.uk/General%20Strike%201926)) The sentence came from a newspaper that supported the government, and it survived because a local history society copied it out decades later. It puts the unpaid work of feeding a striking household where most accounts of 1926 leave it out, in the kitchen rather than the negotiating room.

What followed was the only general strike Britain has ever known. A general strike is a coordinated stoppage across many industries at once, so that a single trade cannot be isolated and worn down. It ran from 3 to 12 May 1926 and drew roughly three million workers out in support of coal miners who had been locked out of the pits. A lockout is the employer's version of a strike: the workplaces are closed and pay withheld until the men accept terms. The miners were represented by the Miners' Federation of Great Britain, the national body linking the coalfield unions, whose secretary A. J. Cook had made the refusal of wage cuts and longer hours into the strike's watchword. Cook wrote within weeks of the calling-off that the stand had reached far beyond Britain: "The whole world is stirred by the miners' struggle, by the refusal of the men or their leaders to compromise on either hours or wages." (A. J. Cook, "The Great Mining Crisis", July 1926; [Marxists Internet Archive: mining crisis](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sections/britain/subject/general_strike/mining_crisis.htm))

The Communist Party of Great Britain (CPGB), a small party formed in 1920 with little influence over the union leadership, printed the demand in its own manifesto: "All Together Behind the Miners Not a Penny off the Pay. Not a Second on the Day." (CPGB, "Fight to Win!" manifesto, May 1926, reproduced by J. T. Murphy; [Marxists Internet Archive: strike 04](https://www.marxists.org/archive/murphy-jt/1926/strike/04.htm)) The miners' own federation gave the same reasoning a harder edge: "from first to last there was never any possibility of the forces arrayed against the miners accepting anything but lower wages, longer hours and district agreements-unless compelled to do so by the united strength of the working class." (Miners' Federation of Great Britain, *Statement on the General Strike of May 1926*, quoted by National Mining Museum Scotland; [Source: nationalminingmuseum.com](https://nationalminingmuseum.com/projects/general-strike-1926-2026)) In the pit villages that unity had a daily, practical form. Soup kitchens were staffed by miners and their wives, and the CPGB writer Marjorie Pollitt argued that parents should press their children's teachers to act: "In all pit areas, the parents should get the teachers to join them in their demand for free meals for children." (Marjorie Pollitt, June 1926; [Marxists Internet Archive: x01](https://www.marxists.org/archive/pollitt-marjorie/1926/06/x01.htm))

## A strike that surprised its own leaders

The strike was directed by the General Council of the Trades Union Congress (TUC), the national body that brought Britain's trade unions together. The scale took even its supporters unawares. J. T. Murphy, chairman of the CPGB and involved in the strike's daily direction, recalled that his party was caught by surprise by the union executives' vote, and that workers began packing their tools on 3 May. Jack Tanner, a trade unionist and Communist reporting on rank-and-file opinion in July 1926, found that the response in the districts he knew exceeded expectations and that non-unionists came out alongside union members. Contemporaries disagreed sharply about the numbers because they counted different things: Murphy wrote of three and a half to four million on the first full day, a museum account gives over 1.5 million on 3 May, and later summaries settle around three million across the nine days. None of the first-hand sources explains how its figure was reached, so the strike's size is best held as a range rather than a single total.

Local organisation mattered as much as national votes. Councils of Action, committees set up in towns and districts by trade unionists and Communists, arranged food, transport permits and picketing. A CPGB pamphlet written by organisers who visited such committees during the strike recorded: "Broadly speaking, the Committees functioned most effectively when they were close to the workers and were amenable to mass pressure." ("The Reds and the General Strike", CPGB, 1926; [Marxists Internet Archive: reds](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sections/britain/pamphlets/1926/reds.htm)) That was the party's judgement on its own method rather than an independent assessment, but it identifies where the strike's practical strength lay. Tanner recorded that such committees drew out workers who had not been officially called, and that thousands stayed out after 12 May under the slogan "All back or none."

## The battle of the bulletins

The stoppage was fought over information as well as wages. The government took over the *Morning Post* and published the *British Gazette* under the editorship of Winston Churchill. Its first issue told readers that "Nearly all the newspapers have been silenced by violent concerted action. … In a few days, if this were allowed to continue, rumours would poison the air, raise panics and disorders" (*British Gazette* no. 1, 5 May 1926, quoted in the Modern Records Centre blog; [Source: warwick.ac.uk](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/library/mrc/blog/bulletins/)) The TUC closed the whole press, including its own *Daily Herald*, then issued the *British Worker* after police raided the Herald's offices. Ernest Bevin, a senior union leader on the General Council, insisted the stoppage was not an attack on the public: "We have deliberately determined to arrange for a voluntary service, notwithstanding that we are stopping vital services, such as transport and staple industries, to ensure the feeding of the people." (Ernest Bevin, quoted by J. T. Murphy, May 1926; [Marxists Internet Archive: strike 04](https://www.marxists.org/archive/murphy-jt/1926/strike/04.htm))

Whether either paper reached ordinary strikers is a separate question. Bob Davies, a striker in St Helens, remembered thirty-five years later that "There was no press. … the odd copies brought in were regarded more as curios than anything else" (Bob Davies, 1961, quoted in the Modern Records Centre blog; [Source: warwick.ac.uk](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/library/mrc/blog/bulletins/)) Ellen Wilkinson, a Labour MP who drove more than two thousand miles during the strike and addressed meetings throughout the country, complained that the broadcaster was one-sided: "I travelled by car over two thousand miles during the strike and addressed very many meetings. Everywhere the complaints were bitter that a national service subscribed to by every class should have given only one side during the dispute." (Ellen Wilkinson, *Radio Times*, 28 May 1926, quoted in the Modern Records Centre blog; [Source: warwick.ac.uk](https://warwick.ac.uk/services/library/mrc/blog/bulletins/)) Local Communist branches typed their own bulletins on duplicating machines; police raided their premises and imprisoned people for producing them.

## Why the leaders stopped it

The General Council had been uneasy about the strike's political logic from the start. On 2 May it was told that "the representatives of the T.U.C. are confident that a settlement could be reached along the lines of the Report within a fortnight." (Baldwin formula of 2 May 1926, quoted by J. R. Campbell; [Marxists Internet Archive: miners](https://www.marxists.org/archive/campbell-jr/1926/miners.htm)) J. R. Campbell, editor of the CPGB's *Workers' Weekly*, subjected the TUC's own post-strike report to a line-by-line critique and reproduced the General Council's admission that it could see only "capitulation of the Government" or the "disintegration of the strike by a process of attrition" as outcomes. TUC leaders presented the ending as a legal and practical necessity; Communist writers argued that they feared the strike's consequences more than they feared defeat. Both documents exist, and the disagreement is about which motive was decisive, not about the facts of the call-off. On 10 May the *British Worker* carried the appeal "Stand firm. Be loyal to instructions and trust your leaders." (TUC General Council message, *British Worker*, 10 May 1926, quoted by J. T. Murphy; [Marxists Internet Archive: strike 04](https://www.marxists.org/archive/murphy-jt/1926/strike/04.htm)) Tanner gave the rank-and-file verdict in three words: "Their feelings can be summed up in three words: disappointment, disillusion and disgust." (Jack Tanner, July 1926; [Marxists Internet Archive: rank and file](https://www.marxists.org/history/international/comintern/sections/britain/subject/general_strike/rank-and-file.htm))

## After the nine days

The miners stayed out for seven months after the TUC ended the strike without guarantees, and victimisation followed for many who had taken part. Wal Hannington, leader of the National Unemployed Workers' Movement, was serving a prison sentence during the strike and heard the news from inside: "During the General Strike the five of us who remained in prison were, of course, eager to get news of the struggle, but it was extremely difficult." (Wal Hannington, 1936; [Internet Archive: 2015.89976.Unemployed Struggles 1919 1936 djvu](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.89976/2015.89976.Unemployed-Struggles-1919-1936_djvu.txt)) William Gallacher, a Clyde shop-steward leader and Communist MP, recalled speaking to Fife miners after months of lockout while police tried to stop him: "All the time I was in the county the police were chasing after me in a couple of taxis, much to the amusement of the miners." (William Gallacher, 1936; [Internet Archive: 2015.220268.Revolt On djvu](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.220268/2015.220268.Revolt-On_djvu.txt)) These are retrospective accounts, written a decade later by men with a political case to make, and they show how the strike lived on in the careers and grievances of those who led it.

## How historians have read it

- **Jonathan Schneer, *Nine Days in May: The General Strike of 1926* (Oxford University Press, 2026).** Draws on government and TUC reports, spies' reports, special constables, volunteers, Communist activists and rank-and-file union members. In an author interview he argues that the TUC leaders "feared winning even more than they feared losing" and that the strike "threatened revolution" without becoming one. The strongest recent archival synthesis, though the interview is not the book. [Source: unseenhistories.com](https://www.unseenhistories.com/the-general-strike-of-1926-with-jonathan-schneer/)
- **Anne Perkins, *A Very British Strike* (2006).** Reads 1926 as evidence of deep-seated British moderation: "On the whole, Britain conducted itself as it always had, with studied moderation." A journalistic consensus reading, challenged by the record of picketing and rank-and-file anger. [Source: socialistworker.co.uk](https://socialistworker.co.uk/in-depth/the-1926-general-strike-nine-days-of-hope/)
- **Neil Davidson, "The 1926 general strike: nine days of hope" (2006).** Reviews Perkins and argues that the strike was more militant than she allows and that the TUC stopped it while it was still growing. A revolutionary-socialist interpretation whose causal claim about the union bureaucracy is an argument, not a finding. Same URL as above.
- **Chris Harman, "The General Strike" (*International Socialism* 48, June/July 1971).** Places the strike in a long arc of British class struggle and argues that the TUC's "left" behaved no differently from its right once control seemed to slip. [Marxists Internet Archive: genstrike](https://www.marxists.org/archive/harman/1971/06/genstrike.htm)
- **Charles Ferrall and Dougal McNeill, *Writing the 1926 General Strike: Literature, Culture, Politics* (Cambridge University Press, 2015).** Studies how the strike was written into fiction and reportage over the following fifty years, from Woolf and Lawrence to working-class novelists such as Idris Davies, Jack Jones and Gwyn Thomas. [Source: isj.org.uk](http://isj.org.uk/nine-days-that-shaped-the-words/)
- **Cal Winslow, "'Their Greatest Effort Ever': The British General Strike at 100" (*Jacobin*, 4 May 2026).** A concise centenary synthesis quoting G. D. H. Cole's phrase and Bevin's strike call. [Source: jacobin.com](https://jacobin.com/2026/05/general-strike-britain-labor-history)

Broad agreement holds that participation was immense, that the TUC ended the strike without guarantees and that the miners were left to fight on alone for months. Dispute continues over how far "British moderation" explains the outcome, whether state preparation or TUC timidity was the principal cause of defeat, and whether the CPGB helped or hindered rank-and-file initiative. Harman, Davidson and the CPGB pamphlets share a framework and should not be counted as independent corroboration of one another; Schneer's archival work and Perkins's journalistic synthesis stand at different distances from the primary record.

## What the record cannot show

The surviving evidence leaves large gaps. The rival newspapers of the strike are known today chiefly through quotations, catalogues and later recollections rather than through widely available page images, so the contest between the *British Gazette* and the *British Worker* is reconstructed from what participants and archivists reported. The strike testimony of the railway worker Harry Wicks survives as oral-history recordings, known through catalogue descriptions rather than through a published transcript. Most of the Miners' Federation's pre-1943 records were destroyed in the London Blitz, so the miners' case survives mainly in published statements and in union files kept elsewhere. The readily available CPGB material is almost all polemical and needs to be checked against hostile or independent accounts.

The voices that remain are mostly those of leaders, organisers and journalists. Cook, Bevin, Murphy, Campbell, Gallacher and Hannington wrote from positions of authority or from a party platform; ordinary strikers appear in Tanner's generalisations, in Davies's late recollection and in a handful of short newspaper quotations. Women are present through Pollitt's appeal and one unnamed wife's remark. There is no first-hand account here from a volunteer, a strike-breaker, a soldier, or a miner who accepted terms and went back early. The CPGB's claim to have been the decisive organising force remains the party's own case, and the number of strikers is best given as a range, since the sources count different things. A fuller account would need the original bulletins, bound newspaper sets and minute books held at Warwick, the Marx Memorial Library and the People's History Museum.
