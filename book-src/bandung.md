On 18 April 1955 Sukarno, the host of the Asian-African Conference at Bandung, opened a meeting of delegates from twenty-nine Asian and African states. He spoke of nuclear fear and of a colonialism that had not ended with the fall of empires. It survived, he said, in a modern dress of economic, intellectual and physical control by a small alien community within a nation. He offered "Unity in Diversity" and "live and let live" as the principles that could hold the gathering together. "We are united by a common detestation of colonialism in whatever form it appears. We are united by a common detestation of racialism." ([Sukarno, opening speech to the Asian-African Conference, 18 April 1955](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/mod/1955sukarno-bandong.asp)). The English text comes from an Indonesian government publication and is an excerpt, so the phrasing carries editorial choices as well as Sukarno's own claim to lead the "New Emerging Forces".

## Two readings of Bandung

The delegates were not there to form an alliance. Jawaharlal Nehru, India's prime minister, argued in the political committee that joining either Cold War bloc meant surrendering the identity of a country that had just won independence. "I belong to neither and I propose to belong to neither whatever happens in the world." ([Jawaharlal Nehru, speech to the Bandung Conference Political Committee, April 1955](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/mod/1955nehru-bandung2.asp)). This was the case for non-alignment, the policy of refusing to take sides between the American and Soviet blocs, made as a claim about national identity rather than tactics. He held that the unaligned area reduced the risk of war and that the moral force of Asia and Africa counted despite the bombs of the great powers. His words record a position in committee, not the conference's decisions.

Three days after the conference, Tito, the president of Yugoslavia, told Radio Belgrade that the results had been a pleasant surprise. "matters have reached an historical turning-point, in that the people of the two continents are determined to decide their own future for themselves." ([Josip Broz Tito, statement to Radio Belgrade, 27 April 1955](https://www.marxists.org/archive/tito/1955/04/27.htm)). He added that the conception which prevailed at Bandung was in harmony with his own. That is the earliest documentary link between the conference and the movement that took shape at Belgrade in 1961, but it is a favourable answer to a sympathetic questioner and a political appropriation. Bandung-style Afro-Asianism and Belgrade-style non-alignment were not the same project.

Paul Robeson read the meeting from a distance. Barred from travelling abroad as a Black American activist, he sent a greeting that appeared in *Spotlight on Africa* in April 1955. "the very fact of the convening of the Conference of Asian and African nations at Bandung, Indonesia, in itself will be recorded as an historic turning-point in all world affairs." ([Paul Robeson, "Message of Greetings to the Bandung Conference," April 1955](https://www.marxists.org/archive/robeson/1955/04/bandung.htm)). He named the Ganges, Nile, Yangtze and Niger, linked peace to sovereign rights for the nations of Asia, and connected Korea, Kenya, North Africa and South Africa. The greeting was written before or during the meeting, not after, and its praise of the Soviet Union is his own commitment. His "spirit of Bandung" was the breaking of the colour line; Tito's was a concordat between governments. The phrase never named one programme.

## From states to peoples

The second project began in Cairo in December 1957 at the First Afro-Asian People's Solidarity Conference, the founding meeting of the Afro-Asian Peoples' Solidarity Organization, or AAPSO. Anwar el Sadat, then an Egyptian minister, said the gathering met for two reasons. "Today this people's Conference of ours meets, partly in honour of the spirit of Bandung and as a reminder of the principles and ideals it stands for, and partly to push it a step forward." ([Anwar el Sadat, address to the First Afro-Asian People's Solidarity Conference, 26 December 1957](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/Halsall/mod/1957sadat-afroasian1.asp)). The distinction was institutional. Bandung had been a conference of states; this one could include movements from territories still under imperial rule. Sadat defended neutrality as a positive policy and demanded self-determination, an end to nuclear testing and disarmament. His speech was printed in the organising body's own proceedings, and his claim that a peoples' conference was broader than a states' conference was the case for his own institution.

By February 1965 the argument had moved from sovereignty to economic structure. Che Guevara spoke for Cuba as an observer at the Second Economic Seminar of Afro-Asian Solidarity in Algiers, a meeting attended, according to the source edition, by sixty-three governments and nineteen national liberation movements. He held that political liberation and the struggle against backwardness were one road, attacked trade at world-market prices as complicity in exploitation, and warned of the South Americanisation of Africa and Asia through a parasitic local bourgeoisie. "The practice of proletarian internationalism is not only a duty for the peoples struggling for a better future, it is also an inescapable necessity." ([Ernesto Guevara, "At the Afro-Asian Conference in Algeria," 24 February 1965; translator not named](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1965/02/24.htm)). The translator is unnamed and the edition's footnotes argue for Guevara's correctness, so footnotes and speech have to be kept apart. These were his positions weeks before the Congo involvement, not a report of any decision.

## Havana, January 1966

The Tricontinental Conference in Havana brought the two currents into one building and created an institution. Amílcar Cabral, leader of the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde, or PAIGC, addressed it in January 1966. He opened by saluting the Cuban Revolution and named the struggle against "our own weaknesses" as the form of struggle missing from the programme. His central warning was that revolution could not be carried from one country to another. "national liberation and social revolution are not exportable commodities; they are, and increasingly so every day, the outcome of local and national elaboration." ([Amílcar Cabral, "The Weapon of Theory," January 1966; translation](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/cabral/1966/weapon-theory.htm)). Cabral was generalising from the war in Guinea, and his speech is a participant's theory, not a finding about every liberation movement.

Fidel Castro closed the conference on 15 January at Havana's Chaplin Theater. He described its results in institutional terms: an organ called tricontinental in nature was created, a committee to aid liberation movements was set up, and Havana was chosen as the temporary seat of its executive secretariat, known as OSPAAAL. "the revolutionary representations of 82 peoples have met to discuss problems of common interest" ([Fidel Castro, closing speech to the Tricontinental Conference, 15 January 1966; translated by the US Foreign Broadcast Information Service](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1966/01/15.htm)). Castro put the declaration's demand in these words: "as the Havana Declaration says, the duty of every revolutionary is to effect the revolution, and effect it in deed not in word" ([Fidel Castro, 15 January 1966, quoting the Havana Declaration; FBIS translation](https://www.marxists.org/history/cuba/archive/castro/1966/01/15.htm)). Latin America was now inside a framework that had previously been called Afro-Asian, and the host said the field of battle against imperialism was the whole world. This translation was made from Havana radio by a United States monitoring service, the eighty-two figure was Castro's own, and the speech was a triumphant host's account of what he claimed to have built.

In April 1967 the new secretariat published Che Guevara's *Message to the Tricontinental*, written before he left for Bolivia. It turned the conference's perspective into a strategy of continental and global armed struggle. "imperialism is a world system, the last stage of capitalism — and it must be defeated in a world confrontation." ([Ernesto Guevara, *Message to the Tricontinental*, 16 April 1967; translation](https://www.marxists.org/archive/guevara/1967/04/16.htm)). The imprint is itself evidence that the organisation existed and could circulate such a call. The message was a summons to fight, not a description of what OSPAAAL did.

## Fanon's warning

Frantz Fanon, a psychiatrist and militant in the Algerian war, supplied the sharpest account of why the diplomacy of non-alignment could look insufficient. In "The Pitfalls of National Consciousness," chapter three of *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), he argued that political independence could leave the economic structure intact. The national middle class, the educated stratum that inherited the colonial state, stepped into the place of the former settlers and became an intermediary rather than a transforming force. "its mission has nothing to do with transforming the nation; it consists, prosaically, of being the transmission line between the nation and a capitalism, rampant though camouflaged, which today puts on the masque of neocolonialism." ([Frantz Fanon, "The Pitfalls of National Consciousness," 1961; English translation, 1965](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/fanon/pitfalls-national.htm)). Neo-colonialism here means control through economic and political influence rather than direct rule. Fanon traced the slide from nationalism to chauvinism, the single party as a form of bourgeois dictatorship, and the uneven development that broke African unity. His examples were his own selections, not evidence of what happened in any named state.

The book's conclusion refused imitation of Europe as a model for the newly independent world. "For Europe, for ourselves and for humanity, comrades, we must turn over a new leaf, we must work out new concepts, and try to set afoot a new man." ([Frantz Fanon, conclusion to *The Wretched of the Earth*, 1961; English translation, 1965](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/fanon/conclusion.htm)). Jean-Paul Sartre, writing a preface in 1961, described the book as an event in which the colonised spoke among themselves rather than to Europe. "The Third World finds itself and speaks to itself through his voice." ([Jean-Paul Sartre, preface to *The Wretched of the Earth*, 1961; emphasis in the original; translation](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/sartre/1961/preface.htm)). Sartre conceded that the book had no need of a preface, and his appropriation of Fanon for a European audience has been criticised. His words are evidence of how the argument landed among metropolitan intellectuals, not a neutral account of Fanon's position.

## How historians have read it

The bibliographic details below come from further-reading lists rather than the books themselves, and the claims made for them should be verified before they are relied on. Amitav Acharya argues that Bandung should be studied as a site of norm-making and a challenge to Eurocentric international relations theory rather than a failed alliance. Naoko Shimazu treats the conference as a staged diplomatic performance, a counterweight to reading the speeches as plain policy statements. Jürgen Dinkel places 1961 in an institutional lineage reaching back before Bandung, where Tito's claim of harmony could be tested. Brian Russell Roberts and Keith Foulcher pair Richard Wright's reportage with Indonesian material, and Christopher J. Lee frames Bandung through its later political afterlives, where the Robeson and Malcolm X connections belong. Jason C. Parker uses American policy records to argue that the conference exposed a contradiction between anti-communism and the colonialism of Western allies. The broad agreement is that Bandung mattered as a norm-setting and symbolic event and cannot be reduced to a simple precursor of the Non-Aligned Movement. The live disagreement concerns how radical the 1955 language was, how far the conference caused later decolonisation, and whether the 1966 Tricontinental expressed a broad revolutionary consensus or a Cuban-led current.

## Limits of the record

Several decisive documents are absent from the accessible record: the final Bandung communiqué, the Belgrade Declaration of 1961, the Havana Declaration as a standalone text and the OLAS resolution. Any account of the ten Bandung principles or of OLAS must wait for them. The surviving texts are also narrow. The Bandung and Belgrade words are those of heads of government, the 1957 and 1965 speeches are by ministers and party leaders, the 1966 texts by revolutionary leaders, and Fanon and Sartre are theorists writing in French and translated into English. No rank-and-file delegate, no intervention by a woman at any of these conferences, and no testimony from people in the territories under discussion was verified. The translation layers are substantial: Castro's speech is an American government translation of a Cuban radio broadcast, Guevara's Algiers translator is unnamed, and the Fanon pages name neither translator nor edition editor. Twenty-nine states at Bandung, eighty-two peoples at Havana and sixty-three governments and nineteen movements at Algiers are participants' or editors' counts, not audited totals. These texts establish what people said and what organisations they claimed to create. They cannot show how much material aid reached any liberation movement, how the organisations worked day to day, or how their decisions were received by the people they claimed to represent. The archive work still required lies in Jakarta, Belgrade, Havana and Cairo, in Indonesian, Serbo-Croatian, Spanish, French and Arabic.
