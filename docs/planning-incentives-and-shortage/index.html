The Plan and the Shortage: Targets, Responses and Scarcity

A miner's record, a ten per cent quota rise and a party report on empty shelves: how plans were made, how they were answered and why goods remained scarce.

MarxismPart XI: Political Economy

On the night of 30 to 31 August 1935 Aleksei Stakhanov, a hewer, meaning a miner who cuts coal at the working face, produced 102 tons in a single shift, ten per cent of the mine’s daily output (Pravda report, 1 September 1935, reproduced in Stakhanov’s memoir). A Stakhanovite was a worker whose record was held up as an example. Stalin called it “profoundly revolutionary” and “almost spontaneously… from below,” while ordering party bodies to curb resisting managers (Stalin, speech to Stakhanovites, 17 November 1935). He conceded 102 tons could not become the general norm, the output expected of an ordinary worker for full pay, yet it was used to raise expectations.

The record was collective work. Stakhanov described arranging timberers, horse-drivers and a repair shift, and wrote: “There is no secret in the fact that I cut 102 tons of coal” (Stakhanov, The Story of My Life, 1937). Sergo Ordzhonikidze told the Stakhanovite congress: “These 102 tons were produced by an entire brigade” (Ordzhonikidze, quoted by Markin, 1936). The memoir is an official self-presentation in a machine translation; Markin’s hostile digest of resentment and assaults is selected press cuttings, not court records (Markin, 1936). Both agree the figure depended on help most miners lacked.

In May 1953 the Socialist Unity Party, ruling party of the German Democratic Republic, raised the work quota by ten per cent. Builders on the Stalinallee site in East Berlin found the change in their pay packets, stopped work and marched. On 16 June the Politburo retreated: “The increase in work quotas cannot and must not be carried out through administrative methods. It can only be based on conviction and voluntary action” (SED statement, 16 June 1953). The concession followed the strike but kept the productivity goal. A secret Soviet report of 24 June put 66,000 demonstrators in East Berlin, listed lower norms and prices, recorded food interruptions, and blamed Western intelligence while calling 17 June “X-day” (Sokolovskii, Semyonov and Yudin, 24 June 1953). Its own facts point to the quota policy: the target was raised, workers stopped work, and the leadership withdrew the method while keeping the aim.

How the plan was answered#

Letters from Soviet workers show what happened when targets met material conditions. V. A. Sychov, a prize-winning herdsman at a sovkhoz, a state farm owned and run by the government, wrote in September 1935 that his brigade was “all barefoot because we have noware to buy… We have nothing not even matches.” The milk plan fell to fifty-five to sixty per cent because there was no water and the herd had to be driven seven kilometres (Sychov letter, 13 September 1935). A kolkhoz was a collective farm where land and implements were owned jointly and members worked under an elected chairman. P. I. Voronov wrote in 1936 for a village meeting asking for “an uninterrupted supply of goods for sale in our villages, especially flour,” noting that “for bread kolkhoz farmers have to travel thirty kilometers” (Voronov letter, 1936). He complained that the chairman, brigade leader and clerk did no work and asked that work-days, units of recorded collective-farm labour, be entered every ten days. These are plan-fulfilling workers demanding the means to fulfil the plan.

Tretiakov’s 1935 report on Pasha Angelina’s women’s tractor brigade described twenty-hour shifts for a promised 1,200 hectares per tractor and seven days without somewhere to sleep (Tretiakov, “Nine Girls”, 1935). Written inside the campaign, its praise is the author’s; it records a brigade winning authority under conditions the plan did not provide.

Why scarcity returned#

Shortage outlived the famine years. A photograph of 25 July 1953 showed “bursting” shelves a month after the East Berlin revolt (photograph entry). In May 1979 an internal party report still recorded: “All the first-class goods produced by our republic are either exported or sold in special stores [Exquisitläden] that ordinary people cannot afford.” It listed goods “under the table” and missing Trabant parts, treating complaints as rumours (SED report, 15 May 1979). Ksenia Chekina, a student stripped of her internal passport, the document deciding whether a person could study, work and live in a city, wrote in 1937: “I have been without a job, without shelter, without a piece of bread for seven months already” (Chekina petition, 1937). A petition may exaggerate, but the chain from work to food is plain.

The DPRK shows a broken distribution chain. Song Hee-suk, a Chongjin factory worker whose name was changed by her interviewer, described a shrinking ration, foraging for weeds, and her husband’s and son’s deaths. Recorded in Seoul in 2004, it cannot establish a national death rate but shows the ration book and work unit tied together (Demick, Nothing to Envy, 2009). Haggard and Noland blame a state-led push for self-reliance, slow response and obstructed relief; Demick’s death estimates, from incomplete evidence, range from six hundred thousand to two and a half million (Haggard and Noland, Famine in North Korea, 2007; Demick, “The Good Cook”, 2009). Hazel Smith gives more weight to how marketisation changed people’s interests (Smith, North Korea: Markets and Military Rule, 2015). The two accounts accept the same failure and dispute its cause.

Cuba’s 2021 congress report named “excessive bureaucracy, inadequate control of resources” and a model that “does not provide sufficient incentives for work or innovation,” while ruling out shock therapies (Raúl Castro, Central Report, 16 April 2021). July 2021 protests over food shortages followed, with historians describing impatience over wages and diets (Gibson, 2021). In Yugoslavia the plan remained in force after 1950, and the appointed director could delay decisions contrary to it (Basic Law of 26 June 1950). In 1969 a worker told Student: “That’s only on paper. When the managers choose their people, workers have to obey; that’s how it is here” (worker, 4 March 1969). One voice cannot stand for every factory, but it matches Tito’s admission that officials “deny these rights to the workers councils” (Tito, 25 June 1957). Scarcity here was also an absence of control over production.

Irrigation for cotton diverted the Aral Sea’s feeder rivers; a Muynak fisherman remembered the 1957 catch and the end of fishing by 1982 (Kenesbay, quoted by Hinrichsen, 1995). Goldman linked Soviet pollution to industrial priorities, Pryde to weak enforcement (Goldman; Pryde). Water was a free input to a plan that counted output, not depletion.

Documented mechanisms and contested causes#

Some mechanisms are visible in the documents. An exceptional result was publicised to reset norms. A quota was raised and withdrawn after a strike. Reports listed missing goods and diverted supplies; letters recorded low fulfilment, absent footwear and long journeys for bread. The North Korean chain tied rations to the work unit, and its breakdown is described by a defector and economists. These claims rest on named texts.

The causes are contested. The Soviet report blamed Western intelligence, Dönhoff a spontaneous revolution; the SED’s admissions support a domestic economic revolt politicised from outside (Dönhoff, 25 June 1953). Siegelbaum treats Stakhanovism as a struggle over shop-floor control; Thurston finds more worker influence than the totalitarian reading allows (Siegelbaum; Thurston). Fitzpatrick finds villagers calling the kolkhoz a “second serfdom” while winning plots; Viola’s police reports record only what the state noticed (Fitzpatrick; Viola). Steiner argues that the planned economy explains the GDR’s relative failure, while Fulbrook stresses accommodation and constraint (Steiner; Fulbrook). Harrison and Connolly agree on industrialisation and its costs but offer no single verdict (Harrison; Connolly). No source measures how many workers shared an experience, or ranks these explanations.

Limits of the record#

This is a collage, not a survey. Petitions exaggerate, state memoirs idealise, opposition digests select, and the GDR report framed complaints as rumours. Almost every voice is translated, Stakhanov’s by machine in 2026. The writers were a famous miner, a prize herdsman, a village spokesman, a student and a defector. No rank-and-file striker of 1953, no Yugoslav council minutes and no Cuban enterprise records appear here. No famine death figure is settled; no source measures bypassed councils. The evidence supports conflicts over norms, pay and rations, not a national average or single verdict.
