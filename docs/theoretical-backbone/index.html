The Theoretical Backbone

Definitions, historical materialism, the dictatorship of the proletariat, socialist political economy, currents, glossary and reading list.

These ideas were written by particular people in particular circumstances, and they changed as those circumstances changed. A mode of production is the historically specific way a society organizes the making and distributing of goods, including both the tools and skills available and the social relations through which people work. Socialism and communism, in the tradition discussed here, are names for stages in a historical process rather than fixed moral ideals. In the later Marxist-Leninist usage, socialism names the lower phase, with public ownership and distribution according to work, while communism names the higher phase, with abundance and distribution according to needs. That process was argued, not observed in advance. The texts record what writers claimed, how parties and governments restated those claims, and where scholars have disagreed about them.

Four things need to be kept apart. The first is the theory: arguments about history, value and class made by Marx, Engels and their successors. The second is intention: what revolutionaries said they wanted to build. The third is the programmatic language of states that called themselves socialist, set out in constitutions, party rules and textbooks. The fourth is what actually happened in those states, including the lives of the people who lived under them. A constitution can declare that an economy works in a certain way without establishing that it does. The discussion that follows is mainly about the first three. Evidence about the fourth comes from historical research and is treated elsewhere in this book. Where a document is a program or an official self-description, it is identified as such.

What did “socialism” and “communism” mean?#

In the Communist Manifesto “communism” names a movement and a standpoint, not a blueprint. Marx and Engels wrote that communist conclusions “are in no way based on ideas or principles that have been invented, or discovered, by this or that would-be universal reformer. They merely express, in general terms, actual relations springing from an existing class struggle” (Marx and Engels, Manifesto of the Communist Party, chapter II). Engels later identified the “basic thought running through the Manifesto”: that the economic production and social structure of each epoch form the foundation of its political and intellectual history, and that the proletariat “cannot attain its emancipation from the sway of the exploiting and ruling class, the bourgeoisie, without, at the same time, and once and for all, emancipating society at large from all exploitation, oppression, class distinction, and class struggles” (Engels, preface to the 1883 German edition of the Manifesto).

The more precise usage that became standard in Marxism-Leninism treats socialism and communism as the lower and higher phases of a single communist mode of production. The Soviet Academy of Sciences’ Political Economy textbook stated it directly: “Communist society passes through two phases of development: the lower phase known as Socialism, and the higher phase known as Communism. In the first stage, communist society cannot as yet be free from the traditions and traces of capitalism, from whose womb it has emerged” (Political Economy, chapter 40). This was the voice of an official textbook written decades after Marx, not his own wording.

Marx’s 1875 Critique of the Gotha Programme is the source on which that later terminology drew. He described the first phase as “a communist society, not as it has developed on its own foundations, but, on the contrary, just as it emerges from capitalist society; which is thus in every respect, economically, morally, and intellectually, still stamped with the birthmarks of the old society from whose womb it emerges” (Marx, Critique of the Gotha Programme, Part I). Three features follow. The individual producer “receives back from society after the deductions have been made exactly what he gives to it,” namely “his individual quantum of labour.” Equality of distribution is equality of labour rather than of needs, so “equal right” remains a “bourgeois right” because it applies “an equal standard” to unequal individuals. And the defects that follow, such as a married worker with children receiving less per head than a single worker, “are inevitable in the first phase of communist society as it is when it has just emerged after prolonged birth pangs from capitalist society,” because “right can never be higher than the economic structure of society and its cultural development conditioned thereby” (Gotha Programme, Part I).

The higher phase, in Marx’s formulation, depends on material and social conditions rather than on a declaration of principle:

“In a higher phase of communist society, after the enslaving subordination of the individual to the division of labor, and therewith also the antithesis between mental and physical labor, has vanished; after labor has become not only a means of life but life’s prime want; after the productive forces have also increased with the all-around development of the individual, and all the springs of co-operative wealth flow more abundantly — only then can the narrow horizon of bourgeois right be crossed in its entirety and society inscribe on its banners: From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs!”

(Source: Marx, Critique of the Gotha Programme, Part I.)

Marx warned against making distribution the central question. It is “in general a mistake to make a fuss about so-called distribution and put the principal stress on it,” because “any distribution whatever of the means of consumption is only a consequence of the distribution of the conditions of production themselves,” which is “a feature of the mode of production itself” (Gotha Programme, Part I).

Between capitalism and communist society, Marx placed a distinct period of revolutionary transformation. He wrote: “Between capitalist and communist society lies the period of the revolutionary transformation of the one into the other. Corresponding to this is also a political transition period in which the state can be nothing but the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat” (quoted in Lenin, State and Revolution, chapter V). Lenin commented that Marx based this conclusion “on an analysis of the role played by the proletariat in modern capitalist society, on the data concerning the development of this society, and on the irreconcilability of the antagonistic interests of the proletariat and the bourgeoisie” (State and Revolution, chapter V). The Soviet textbook later distinguished “entry into the period of socialism” from “the end of the transitional period,” noting that the former “did not yet mean the end of the transitional period, since the task of building a socialist society had not been fully accomplished” (Political Economy, chapter 26). Completion of the transition meant, in that textbook’s account, the victory of socialist production relations. It described socialism as “a system based on social ownership of the means of production in its two forms — State (public) and co-operative or collective farm property. In this system there is no exploitation of man by man; the national economy is developed in a planned way… and realisation of the principle of distribution according to work” (Political Economy, chapter 26). These are claims made by a textbook about its own society, not independent findings about it.

Lenin stabilized the terminology in State and Revolution in 1917 by reading Marx’s “first phase of communist society” as what was ordinarily called socialism and treating the higher phase as communism. He moved directly from Marx’s two-phase analysis to the claim that “the transition from the first phase of communist society to its higher phase” coincided “with the complete withering away of the state” (Lenin, State and Revolution, chapter V). He praised Marx for giving, “instead of scholastically invented, ‘concocted’ definitions and fruitless disputes over words (What is socialism? What is communism?), an analysis of what might be called the stages of the economic maturity of communism” (Lenin, quoted in the MIA introduction to the Gotha Programme). The Soviet textbook tradition followed this usage: “Communist society passes through two phases of development: the lower phase known as Socialism, and the higher phase known as Communism” (Political Economy, chapter 40).

The distribution slogans were fixed in official documents over a long period. Article 12 of the Soviet Constitution of 1936 declared: “The principle applied in the U.S.S.R. is that of socialism: ‘From each according to his ability, to each according to his work’” (Constitution of the USSR, 1936, Article 12). Stalin explained the choice in his report on the draft, drawing a distinction that matters for reading all such documents: “Whereas a program speaks of that which does not yet exist, of that which has yet to be achieved and won in the future, a constitution, on the contrary, must speak of that which already exists, of that which has already been achieved and won now, at the present time” (Stalin, report on the draft constitution, 25 November 1936). In 1977 Brezhnev defended the same principle against the idea that the Soviet Union had reached the higher phase: “Some proposals have clearly run ahead of our time, failing to take account of the fact that the new Constitution is the Fundamental Law of a state of developed socialism and not of communism. We live according to the socialist principle ‘From each according to his abilities, to each according to his work.’ To bypass this principle at the present level of economic development and the people’s consciousness is impossible” (Brezhnev, On the Draft Constitution of the USSR, 1977). Cuba’s 2011 Lineamientos and 2021 model conceptualization use the same formula for distribution according to work (PCC, Lineamientos, 2011).

The idea of a “state of the whole people” belongs to this later Soviet vocabulary, not to the classical two-phase account in Marx or Engels. Engels’s term for the transitional political form was the “semi-state,” meaning a state already beginning to wither. Brezhnev described “the gradual development of the state of proletarian dictatorship into a state of the whole people” and the transformation of Soviet statehood “into communist social self-government” (Brezhnev, On the Draft Constitution, 1977). That is a claim made by a particular government about its own development, and it should not be read back into the 1875 text.

How did the tradition explain history?#

The canonical statement is Marx’s 1859 preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy. “Productive forces” there means the labour, tools, techniques and knowledge available for production. “Relations of production” means the social arrangements people enter into to produce and distribute goods. The “base” is the whole of those relations, and the “superstructure” is the law, politics and culture that arise on it.

“In the social production of their existence, men inevitably enter into definite relations, which are independent of their will, namely relations of production appropriate to a given stage in the development of their material forces of production. The totality of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, the real foundation, on which arises a legal and political superstructure and to which correspond definite forms of social consciousness. It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness.”

(Source: Marx, Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, 1859.)

The dynamic of change is stated in the same passage: “at a certain stage of development, the material productive forces of society come into conflict with the existing relations of production. From forms of development of the productive forces these relations turn into their fetters. Then begins an era of social revolution.” Marx added a limit: “No social order is ever destroyed before all the productive forces for which it is sufficient have been developed, and new superior relations of production never replace older ones before the material conditions for their existence have matured within the framework of the old society” (1859 Preface).

Engels gave a popular exposition in Anti-Dühring. He wrote that the “materialist conception of history” starts from the claim that “the production of the means to support human life and, next to production, the exchange of things produced, is the basis of all social structure.” It follows that “the final causes of all social changes and political revolutions are to be sought, not in men’s brains, but in changes in the modes of production and exchange” (Engels, Anti-Dühring, “Socialism, Theoretical”; Engels, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part III). He defined the term itself: “historical materialism” designates “that view of the course of history which seeks the ultimate cause and the great moving power of all important historic events in the economic development of society, in the changes in the modes of production and exchange, in the consequent division of society into distinct classes, and in the struggles of these classes against one another” (Engels, Introduction to the French edition of Socialism: Utopian and Scientific).

Stalin’s 1938 pamphlet gave the formulation most widely circulated in the Marxist-Leninist tradition. “Historical materialism,” he wrote, “is the extension of the principles of dialectical materialism to the study of social life, an application of the principles of dialectical materialism to the phenomena of the life of society, to the study of society and of its history” (Stalin, Dialectical and Historical Materialism, 1938). This was a codification for party education, not a new discovery.

The meaning of “base” and “superstructure” has been contested even among writers who accept the framework. Derek Sayer points out that in the 1859 preface the key concepts are “either left altogether undefined in the text itself, or else defined circularly, in terms of one another” (Sayer, The Violence of Abstraction, chapter 2). Chris Harman asks whether the base means the economy, the productive forces, technology or the relations of production, and how far the superstructure can have “a degree of ‘autonomy’” while still being determined “in the last resort” (Harman, Base and Superstructure, 1986). G. A. Cohen’s Karl Marx’s Theory of History: A Defence reconstructed the theory using the idea that productive forces have a tendency to develop and that production relations are explained by their function in that development. Cohen’s reconstruction has been influential and disputed in equal measure; critics argue that it gives the productive forces too much explanatory weight and pushes class struggle to the margin (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Karl Marx”; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Analytical Marxism”; Princeton University Press, Karl Marx’s Theory of History). The disagreements matter because the 1859 preface is short and its central terms are compressed.

How did it explain class, the state and exploitation?#

Class struggle, in this vocabulary, means conflict between groups defined by their place in production. The Communist Manifesto opens with the claim that “the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles. Freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf, guild-master and journeyman, in a word, oppressor and oppressed, stood in constant opposition to one another” (Manifesto, chapter I). It adds that “our epoch, the epoch of the bourgeoisie, possesses this distinct feature: it has simplified class antagonisms. Society as a whole is more and more splitting up into two great hostile camps, Bourgeoisie and Proletariat” (Manifesto, chapter I). Engels’s 1888 footnote identifies the bourgeoisie as “the class of modern capitalists, owners of the means of social production and employers of wage labour” and the proletariat as “the class of modern wage labourers who, having no means of production of their own, are reduced to selling their labour power in order to live” (Manifesto, chapter I, notes).

On the state, Engels’s Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State is the source Lenin summarized. The state is “a product and a manifestation of the irreconcilability of class antagonisms” and arises “when class contradictions have become objectively irreconcilable” (Lenin, State and Revolution, chapter I). Lenin stressed the coercive element: “the state is a special organization of force: it is an organization of violence for the suppression of some class” (State and Revolution, chapter II).

Exploitation, for Marx, does not rest on a capitalist paying too little in the ordinary sense. It rests on the purchase of labour-power, the capacity to work, whose use produces more value than its cost. Engels identified the two discoveries: “the materialistic conception of history and the revelation of the secret of capitalistic production through surplus-value. With these discoveries, Socialism became a science.” He explained the second: “It was shown that the appropriation of unpaid labor is the basis of the capitalist mode of production and of the exploitation of the worker that occurs under it; that even if the capitalist buys the labor power of his laborer at its full value as a commodity on the market, he yet extracts more value from it than he paid for” (Engels, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part II).

Capital sets out the mechanism in connected steps. The value of labour-power “is determined by the value of the necessaries of life habitually required by the average labourer” (Marx, Capital, volume I, chapter 17), and its consumption “is at one and the same time the production of commodities and of surplus-value” (Capital, volume I, chapter 6). The rate of surplus value is written as s/v, surplus value over variable capital, and represents “the ratio of the times during which those values are produced,” surplus labour to necessary labour (Capital, volume I, chapter 18); absolute and relative surplus value are distinguished in chapter 16 (Capital, volume I, chapter 16). “Employing surplus-value as capital, reconverting it into capital, is called accumulation of capital,” with the result that “the more the capitalist has accumulated, the more is he able to accumulate” (Capital, volume I, chapter 24). Engels summarizes the social contradiction: “The means of production, and production itself, had become in essence socialized. But they were subjected to a form of appropriation which presupposes the private production of individuals. This contradiction contains the germ of the whole of the social antagonisms of today” (Engels, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part III).

These are analytical claims about capitalism, and scholars have scrutinized them. The transition from labour values to market prices, known as the transformation problem, is described by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy as technically contested, and some philosophers argue that the claim that only labour creates surplus value is unsupported (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Exploitation”; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Karl Marx”). G. A. Cohen has argued that the theory of exploitation does not depend on the labour theory of value at all (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Analytical Marxism”). The existence of a dispute does not settle it, but it means that the classical derivation should not be repeated as if it were an established accounting fact.

The Soviet textbook went further than Marx and asserted what would happen under socialism: the categories of capitalist political economy, including “capital, surplus-value, capitalist profit, price of production, wage-labour, the value of labour-power,” would cease to exist, and new economic laws would apply, such as planned development, rising labour productivity, distribution according to work and socialist accumulation (Political Economy, chapter 29). That is a theoretical claim within a particular tradition. It is not evidence that such categories had ceased to exist anywhere.

What part did dialectics play?#

Engels used “dialectics” to mean the study of change produced by internal tensions, as opposed to what he called the metaphysical habit of treating things as fixed and separate. In the Dialectics of Nature manuscripts he wrote that the laws of dialectics “are nothing but the most general laws of these two aspects of historical development, as well as of thought itself,” and reduced them in the main to three: “The law of the transformation of quantity into quality and vice versa; The law of the interpenetration of opposites; The law of the negation of the negation” (Engels, Dialectics of Nature, chapter 2). In plain language, the first means that gradual accumulation can produce a sudden change of kind, as heating water produces steam; the second that development is driven by tensions within a thing; and the third that a later stage can restore something of an earlier stage in a changed form.

Stalin’s 1938 pamphlet was the most widely circulated codification. It presented “the principal features of the Marxist dialectical method,” illustrating the transformation of quantity into quality with water becoming steam or ice, oxygen becoming ozone, and the melting and boiling points of substances, with change occurring at “nodal points” (Stalin, Dialectical and Historical Materialism). On the second feature he wrote that “internal contradictions are inherent in all things and phenomena of nature, for they all have their negative and positive sides, and that the struggle between these opposites constitutes the internal content of the process of development” (Stalin, Dialectical and Historical Materialism). The negation of the negation was not listed as a separate feature.

Mao’s “On Contradiction” of 1937 made the unity of opposites central: “the law of contradiction in things, that is, the law of the unity of opposites, is the basic law of materialist dialectics” (Mao, “On Contradiction”). He developed the distinction between the universality and the particularity of contradiction, and introduced the idea of the principal contradiction: “If in any process there are a number of contradictions, one of them must be the principal contradiction playing the leading and decisive role, while the rest occupy a secondary and subordinate position” (Mao, “On Contradiction,” quoted in Quotations from Chairman Mao, chapter 22). A principal contradiction is the one that dominates a given situation and determines the character of the others. Mao also distinguished the principal aspect of a contradiction, meaning the side that is dominant at a particular stage (Mao, “On Contradiction”).

The status of the “three laws” is disputed even among Marxists. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy records that Engels himself did not use the term “dialectical materialism,” that the laws take different forms in the polished Anti-Dühring and the unfinished Dialectics of Nature, and that interpretations range from Alfred Schmidt’s criticism that they have “absolutely no connection with the method of natural science itself” to Jon Elster’s qualified defence of them as descriptions of patterns of change (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Friedrich Engels”). Z. A. Jordan discusses the doctrinal difference between Engels’s three laws and Stalin’s list of principal features (Jordan, Notes for Philosophy and Ideology, chapter 7).

Alienation and crisis#

Marx’s earliest systematic treatment of alienation, the condition in which a person’s own work becomes an alien power over them, is in the 1844 Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts. “The worker becomes all the poorer the more wealth he produces,” he wrote. “The devaluation of the world of men is in direct proportion to the increasing value of the world of things.” The product of labour “confronts it as something alien, as a power independent of the producer”; objectification becomes “loss of the object and bondage to it; appropriation as estrangement, as alienation” (Marx, “Estranged Labour,” Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844). He enumerated four dimensions: separation from the product, from the activity of labour, from “man’s species-being,” and from other people (Marxists Internet Archive, “Estranged Labour”).

Later Marxist reference works connect this to commodity fetishism, the idea that in a market people treat the social relations between themselves as relations between things (Marxists Internet Archive subject index, “Marxism and Alienation”). Whether the early philosophical critique and the mature economic critique form one continuous argument is contested. Althusser’s “epistemological break” thesis places a rupture around 1845, after which Marx is said to have founded a science distinct from the humanist philosophy of his youth; other readers find substantial continuity (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Louis Althusser”). The Manuscripts were not published until 1932, so the question of continuity was largely a twentieth-century one.

Crisis theory rests on the argument that capitalism periodically destroys wealth and productive capacity rather than resolving its contradictions. In Capital, volume III, Marx’s “law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall” begins from a paradox: “the difficulty which has hitherto troubled the economist, namely to explain the falling rate of profit, gives place to its opposite, namely to explain why this fall is not greater and more rapid. There must be some counteracting influences at work, which cross and annul the effect of the general law, and which give it merely the characteristic of a tendency” (Marx, Capital, volume III, chapter 14). Among those influences he lists the increasing intensity of exploitation, the cheapening of the elements of constant capital, relative over-population, foreign trade and the increase of stock capital (Capital, volume III, chapter 14). He writes that “the law acts only as a tendency,” and that “it is only under certain circumstances and only after long periods that its effects become strikingly pronounced” (Capital, volume III, chapter 14). The contradictions of the law produce crises because the productive forces developed under capitalism “contradict the basis, which constantly narrows in relation to the expanding wealth, and for which all this immense productiveness works. Hence the crises” (Capital, volume III, chapter 15). Chris Harman defends the centrality of this law within Marx’s work, while other Marxists reject it or treat it as one crisis theory among several (Harman, “Marx’s theory of crisis and its critics,” 1981).

How did Marx describe the origins of capital?#

Marx’s Part VIII of Capital, volume I, addresses an apparent circle: accumulation requires capital, but capital requires prior accumulation. He writes that “the accumulation of capital presupposes surplus-value; surplus-value presupposes capitalistic production; capitalistic production presupposes the pre-existence of considerable masses of capital and of labour power; out of which we can only get by supposing a primitive accumulation, an accumulation not the result of the capitalistic mode of production, but its starting point” (Marx, Capital, volume I, chapter 26). Primitive accumulation therefore means the historically violent separation of producers from the means of production.

Marx ridicules the “idyllic” account in which “there were two sorts of people; one, the diligent, intelligent, and, above all, frugal elite; the other, lazy rascals.” He insists that “in actual history it is notorious that conquest, enslavement, robbery, murder, briefly force, play the great part” (Capital, volume I, chapter 26). Its structural result is “the complete separation of the labourers from all property in the means by which they can realize their labour” (Capital, volume I, chapter 26). The final chapter on “the historical tendency of capitalist accumulation” completes the arc from the expropriation of direct producers to the expropriation of the expropriators (Marx, Capital, volume I, table of contents).

What did “the dictatorship of the proletariat” mean?#

The phrase is Marx’s, but Lenin elaborated it systematically. In State and Revolution he quotes the Manifesto: “the first step in the revolution by the working class is to raise the proletariat to the position of the ruling class to win the battle of democracy.” He calls “the state, i.e., the proletariat organized as the ruling class” one of “the most remarkable and most important ideas of Marxism on the subject of the state,” and says that Marx and Engels began to use the phrase after the Paris Commune (Lenin, State and Revolution, chapter II). In this usage the phrase means the political rule of the working class during a transition, not personal rule by a dictator.

Lenin is explicit about its coercive function:

“The state is a special organization of force: it is an organization of violence for the suppression of some class. What class must the proletariat suppress? Naturally, only the exploiting class, i.e., the bourgeoisie. The working people need the state only to suppress the resistance of the exploiters, and only the proletariat can direct this suppression. For the proletariat is the only class that is consistently revolutionary, the only class that can unite all the working and exploited people in the struggle against the bourgeoisie, in completely removing it.”

(Source: Lenin, State and Revolution, chapter II.)

Two points follow. “According to Marx, the proletariat needs only a state which is withering away, i.e., a state so constituted that it begins to wither away immediately, and cannot but wither away,” and the working people need “a state, i.e., the proletariat organized as the ruling class” (State and Revolution, chapter II). In “Left-Wing” Communism Lenin grounds the need for coercion in the persistence of small-scale production: “Small-scale production engenders capitalism and the bourgeoisie continuously, daily, hourly, spontaneously, and on a mass scale. All these reasons make the dictatorship of the proletariat necessary, and victory over the bourgeoisie is impossible without a long, stubborn and desperate life-and-death struggle which calls for tenacity, discipline, and a single and inflexible will” (Lenin, “Left-Wing” Communism, chapter II).

A frequent confusion concerns the relation between the “dictatorship of the party” and the “dictatorship of the class.” Lenin reported that German “Left” Communists asked “who is to exercise this dictatorship: the Communist Party or the proletarian class?” (Lenin, “Left-Wing” Communism, chapter V). His answer described the Russian arrangement: “the dictatorship is exercised by the proletariat organised in the Soviets; the proletariat is guided by the Communist Party of Bolsheviks” (“Left-Wing” Communism, chapter VI).

Lenin devoted chapter III of State and Revolution to the Paris Commune and Marx’s analysis of it. The features he extracted were abolition of the standing army and police in favour of “the direct arming of the people”; election and recall of all officials, reduced “to the position of simple agents,” with pay “not exceeding the ordinary pay of a competent worker”; and the combination of legislative and executive functions, making the Commune “a working, not a parliamentary, body” (Lenin, State and Revolution, chapter III). He also criticized its restraint: “Would the Paris Commune have lasted more than a day if it had not used the authority of the armed people against the bourgeoisie? Cannot we, on the contrary, blame it for having made too little use of that authority?” (State and Revolution, chapter IV).

Engels wrote in Anti-Dühring that the proletariat “abolishes also the state as state,” a general claim about the seizure of power rather than a statement about the Commune specifically (Engels, Anti-Dühring, “Socialism, Theoretical”). Lenin interpreted it as the abolition of the bourgeois state: “the bourgeois state does not ‘wither away’, but is ‘abolished’ by the proletariat in the course of the revolution. What withers away after this revolution is the proletarian state or semi-state” (Lenin, State and Revolution, chapter I). He distinguished Marxism from anarchism on this point: “Marxism differs from anarchism in that it recognizes the need for a state and for state power in the period of revolution in general, and in the period of transition from capitalism to socialism in particular” (Lenin, The Tasks of the Proletariat in Our Revolution, 1917).

The mechanism of withering away is described in State and Revolution. As tasks become generalized and simplified, “the functions of control and accounting, becoming more and more simple, will be performed by each in turn, will then become a habit and will finally die out as the special functions of a special section of the population” (Lenin, State and Revolution, chapter III). At that point “the door will be thrown wide open for the transition from the first phase of communist society to its higher phase, and with it to the complete withering away of the state” (State and Revolution, chapter V). Brezhnev’s “state of the whole people” was a later Soviet restatement of this trajectory, and he called the process “a long process, but proceeding steadily” (Brezhnev, On the Draft Constitution, 1977). These are theoretical expectations. What happened to state power in the societies that invoked them is a question for historical inquiry, not something the quotations can answer.

What role did the party play?#

A vanguard is the most politically advanced and organized section of a movement. Lenin’s What Is To Be Done? of 1902 argued that the working class “exclusively by its own effort, is able to develop only trade union consciousness,” meaning awareness of immediate wage and working-condition demands rather than a broader political outlook. “The theory of socialism,” he added, “grew out of the philosophic, historical, and economic theories elaborated by educated representatives of the propertied classes, by intellectuals,” so that “social-Democratic consciousness would have to be brought to them from without” (Lenin, What Is To Be Done?, chapter II). Organizationally, “the organisation of the revolutionaries must consist first and foremost of people who make revolutionary activity their profession” (What Is To Be Done?, chapter IV). The party earns the name vanguard by generalizing the experience of the whole movement: “In our time only a party that will organise really nation-wide exposures can become the vanguard of the revolutionary forces” (What Is To Be Done?, chapter III). Stalin’s codification put it more bluntly: “The Party is the highest form of the class organisation of the proletariat” (Stalin, The Theory and Practice of Leninism, 1925). Article 6 of the 1977 Soviet Constitution gave the constitutional version: the CPSU, “armed with Marxism-Leninism, determines the general perspectives of the development of society and the course of the home and foreign policy of the USSR” (Constitution of the USSR, 1977, Article 6).

Democratic centralism is the principle that local bodies may discuss policy but must carry out the decisions of the central leadership. The Soviet constitution stated that “the Soviet state is organised and functions on the principle of democratic centralism,” and that it “combines central leadership with local initiative and creative activity and with the responsibility of each state body and official for the work entrusted to them” (Constitution of the USSR, 1977, Article 3). The DPRK Constitution says that “All State organs in the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea are formed and function on the principle of democratic centralism” (DPRK Socialist Constitution, as amended to 2009, Article 5), and the CPV identifies it as “its basic organisational principle” (CPV, quoted in Communist Review). Lenin’s own argument stressed discipline: “absolute centralisation and rigorous discipline of the proletariat are an essential condition of victory over the bourgeoisie.” He said discipline was maintained by the class-consciousness of the vanguard, by its close contact with the masses, and by correct political leadership “provided the broad masses have seen, from their own experience, that [the leaders] are correct” (Lenin, “Left-Wing” Communism, chapter II).

The mass line is a method of gathering people’s scattered ideas, systematizing them and returning them as policy. Mao wrote: “In all the practical work of our Party, all correct leadership is necessarily ‘from the masses, to the masses’. This means: take the ideas of the masses (scattered and unsystematic ideas) and concentrate them (through study turn them into concentrated and systematic ideas), then go to the masses and propagate and explain these ideas until the masses embrace them as their own, hold fast to them and translate them into action, and test the correctness of these ideas in such action. And so on, over and over again in an endless spiral” (Mao, “Some Questions Concerning Methods of Leadership,” 1943, quoted in Quotations from Chairman Mao, chapter 11). The same text warns that “however active the leading group may be, its activity will amount to fruitless effort by a handful of people unless combined with the activity of the masses,” while mass activity without a leading group “cannot be sustained for long” (Quotations from Chairman Mao, chapter 11). Mao’s two general methods were “to combine the general with the particular” and “to combine the leadership with the masses” (Mao, “Some Questions Concerning Methods of Leadership”).

The united front is the tactic of allying with intermediate forces against a common enemy while keeping the party’s independence. Mao’s directive of 25 December 1940 states: “Today our Anti-Japanese National United Front policy is neither all alliance and no struggle nor all struggle and no alliance, but a combines alliance and struggle. Within the united front our policy must be one of independence and initiative, i.e., both unity and independence are necessary” (Mao, On Policy, 25 December 1940). The underlying question is what Mao called “proletarian leadership in the united front” (Mao, “The Question of Independence and Initiative Within the United Front,” 1938). In 1963 the CPC generalized the tactic internationally as “forming a broad united front, with the socialist camp and the international proletariat as its nucleus, to oppose the imperialists and reactionaries headed by the U.S.,” a line of “boldly arousing the masses, expanding the revolutionary forces, winning over the middle forces and isolating the reactionary forces” (CPC Central Committee, A Proposal Concerning the General Line of the International Communist Movement, 14 June 1963). On Policy is the source for the united front; the definitions of democratic centralism and the mass line come from the sources cited above, not from that directive.

How did socialist states describe their economies?#

Everything in this section is a programmatic, constitutional or textbook claim about how an economy was supposed to be organized. These texts state rules and ambitions. They do not establish that the rules were followed, that the ambitions were achieved, or that people’s lives changed in the ways predicted. Stalin’s 1936 observation that a program speaks of the future while a constitution should speak of the present is a useful warning in reverse: a constitution’s statement that socialism has been achieved is itself a claim that needs to be checked against other evidence (Stalin, report on the draft constitution, 25 November 1936).

The Soviet textbook defined the economic basis of socialism as “the socialist system of national economy and socialist ownership of the means of production, which have been consolidated by elimination of the capitalist economic system, the abolition of private ownership of the means of production and the eradication of exploitation of man by man” (Political Economy, chapter 28). It also claimed that contradictions between productive forces and relations of production under socialism “are not of an antagonistic character and do not develop into conflicts, since socialist society is able in good time to bring the relations of production into accord with the level of the productive forces” (Political Economy, chapter 28). In the USSR the two stated forms of ownership were state property and co-operative or collective-farm property. The DPRK Constitution provides that “the means of production are owned by the State and social, cooperative organizations” (DPRK Socialist Constitution, as amended 2009, Article 20). China’s 20th Congress resolution describes “the system under which public ownership is the mainstay and diverse forms of ownership develop together” as an “important pillar of socialism with Chinese characteristics” (CPC, Resolution on the Party Constitution, 2022).

Planning was treated as an objective necessity in Soviet teaching. “Social, socialist ownership of the means of production, and large-scale socialist production both in industry and in agriculture, make the planned, proportional development of the whole economy objectively possible and necessary,” the textbook stated, adding that it is “an economic law of socialism” and “the regulator of socialist industry” (Political Economy, chapter 30). The “law of value” means the rule that commodities tend to exchange according to the labour socially necessary to produce them. Stalin’s Economic Problems of Socialism in the USSR (1952) gave the authoritative Soviet formulation:

“Yes, [the law of value] does exist and does operate. Wherever commodities and commodity production exist, there the law of value must also exist. But the operation of the law of value is not confined to the sphere of commodity circulation. It also extends to production. True, the law of value has no regulating function in our socialist production, but it nevertheless influences production, and this fact cannot be ignored when directing production.”

(Source: Stalin, Economic Problems of Socialism in the USSR, §3.)

Stalin insisted that economic laws “cannot be ‘transformed,’ still less ‘radically’ transformed,” and that the law of value “cannot under our system function as the regulator of production” (Economic Problems, §§1 and 3). Commodity production persisted, in this account, because of “the existence of two basic forms of socialist production: State and collective farm” (Political Economy, chapter 32). “Economic accounting,” meaning the comparison of inputs and outputs in money terms to check profitability, was “a method of planned economic management in socialist enterprises, based on the operation of the law of value” (Political Economy, chapter 34). Stalin formulated the basic economic law of socialism as “the securing of the maximum satisfaction of the constantly rising material and cultural requirements of the whole of society through the continuous expansion and perfection of socialist production on the basis of higher techniques” (Economic Problems, chapter 8). Mao criticized the book from a different angle, complaining that “Stalin’s book from first to last says nothing about the superstructure. It is not concerned with people; it considers things, not people. The basic error is mistrust of the peasants,” and arguing that “Commodity production has to be vastly developed, not for profits but for the peasantry, the agricultural-industrial alliance, and the development of production” (Mao, “Critique of Stalin’s Economic Problems of Socialism in the USSR”).

The five states often grouped together gave different official answers to the same problem of combining ownership, planning and markets. The USSR described itself in 1977 as “a developed, mature socialist society,” with the constitution being “the Fundamental Law of a state of developed socialism and not of communism,” socialist ownership as the basis of the economy, distribution according to work and planning directed by the basic economic law of socialism (Brezhnev, On the Draft Constitution, 1977; Constitution of the USSR, 1977). China described itself as being “in the primary stage of socialism and will remain so for a long time to come,” with an economy in which market forces play a “decisive role in resource allocation” while public ownership remains the mainstay (CPC Constitution, 19th Congress, 2017; 20th Congress resolution, 2022). Vietnam’s 9th CPV Congress in 2001 adopted the “socialist-oriented market economy” as “an unswerving strategy and a general model throughout the transitional period to socialism,” defined as “a modern market economy well integrated in the world,” operating by market laws while “controlled by socialist economic rules and factors that guarantee socialist orientation” (Communist Review, 2013; Communist Review, 2022). Cuba’s 6th PCC Congress in 2011 set out Guidelines to “update the Cuban economic model, aiming at guaranteeing the continuity and irreversibility of Socialism,” and the 2021 conceptualization describes “socialist ownership by all the people of the fundamental means of production” combined with “socialist planning” and “adequate functioning of the market” (PCC, Lineamientos, 2011; PCC, Conceptualización, 2021). The DPRK Constitution combines state and cooperative property with “the socialist system of responsible business operation in economic management” using “cost, price and profit” as levers, while declaring that “the national economy is a planned economy” (DPRK Constitution, 2019 text, Articles 33 and 34; Kim Jong Il, Juche-Oriented Socialist Economic Management). These are declarations of how each state intended its economy to work. They are not measurements of how it worked.

Historical research gives a different picture of results. Mark Harrison’s survey of the Soviet economy concludes that it was designed to build national power rather than to converge on Western living standards: it achieved rapid industrialization, urbanization and military strength, but sustained this by repressing consumption, and its underlying productivity growth was never lifted (Source: Mark Harrison, “The Soviet economy, 1917-1991: Its life and afterlife,” CEPR, 2017). The same account records the human costs, including famines, mass killing and pervasive repression, alongside real gains: mass education opened office work to women, and child mortality fell sharply as sanitation and infection control improved. Income was distributed more equally than before or after Soviet rule, but access to consumer goods depended on political status and connections, which is what the queues outside shops represented. Harrison also notes that life expectancy for middle-aged men and women barely changed from the 1890s to the 1980s. Richard Connolly’s The Russian Economy: A Very Short Introduction similarly describes a centrally planned system that delivered industrialization, urbanization and military success, and asks at what human cost (Source: Richard Connolly, The Russian Economy: A Very Short Introduction, Oxford University Press, 2020). The point is not that the official texts were worthless. It is that stated ambition is not evidence of success, and that ordinary people appear in this record as beneficiaries, consumers, workers and victims rather than as the abstract “producers” of the textbooks.

How were these ideas formalized as ideologies?#

Marxism-Leninism took shape as a name for Lenin’s legacy after his death. Stalin’s 1924 lectures The Foundations of Leninism supplied the canonical formula: “Leninism is Marxism of the era of imperialism and the proletarian revolution. To be more exact, Leninism is the theory and tactics of the proletarian revolution in general, the theory and tactics of the dictatorship of the proletariat in particular” (Stalin, The Foundations of Leninism, Introduction, 1924). He rejected Zinoviev’s qualification that Leninism was Marxism “which began directly in a country where the peasantry predominates” on the ground that it would “transform Leninism from an international proletarian doctrine into a product of specifically Russian conditions” (Stalin, “Concerning Questions of Leninism,” 1926). The Fifth World Congress of the Comintern in 1924 took up the question of Lenin and the Comintern; Karl Korsch’s essay of that year, “Lenin and the Comintern,” argued for smelting the sections of the International “ideologically into one firm unity” on the basis of the revolutionary Marxian method that Lenin had restored (Korsch, “Lenin and the Comintern,” 1924). Article 6 of the 1977 Soviet Constitution then named Marxism-Leninism as the ideology guiding the CPSU (Constitution of the USSR, 1977). The 2017 and 2022 CPC constitutions list “Marxism-Leninism, Mao Zedong Thought, Deng Xiaoping Theory, the Theory of Three Represents, the Scientific Outlook on Development, and Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era” as the Party’s “guides to action” (CPC Constitution, 19th Congress; CPC Constitution, 20th Congress). The CPV states that it “takes Marxism-Leninism and Ho Chi Minh Thought as its ideological foundation and guiding principle for action and democratic centralism as its basic organisational principle” (CPV, quoted in Theory and Practice).

Two usages of “Maoism” must be separated. Mao Zedong Thought is the official ideology of the CPC and a “guide to action” alongside Marxism-Leninism (CPC Constitution, 19th Congress). Marxism-Leninism-Maoism, often abbreviated MLM, is a later and contested current that holds Mao’s work to constitute a third and higher stage of Marxist theory, after Marx and after Lenin. The Revolutionary Internationalist Movement’s 1984 Declaration still spoke of “Marxism-Leninism-Mao Tsetung Thought” and of “Mao Tsetung’s qualitative development of the science of Marxism-Leninism” (RIM Declaration, 1984). In 1993 RIM declared: “our guiding ideology is Marxism-Leninism-Maoism,” explaining that Maoism “is not just the sum total of Mao’s great contributions. It is the comprehensive and all-round development of Marxism-Leninism to a new and higher stage” (RIM, Long Live Marxism-Leninism-Maoism!, 26 December 1993). The same document acknowledges that the earlier formulation “reflected a still incomplete understanding of this new stage,” which shows that the change followed an internal debate (RIM, 1993). The Communist Party of Peru states the three-stage scheme programmatically: “the scientific ideology of the international proletariat emerged as Marxism, afterwards developed into Marxism-Leninism and later Marxism-Leninism-Maoism” (PCP, Fundamental Documents). The dispute is about whether Mao’s contribution is a new stage of the theory or a development within the Leninist stage; it is not a disagreement about what Mao wrote.

Juche and Songun are the DPRK’s official ideological formulas. Kim Jong Il described Juche as based on “the philosophical principle that man is the master of everything and decides everything,” meaning that human beings shape their own destiny (Kim Jong Il, On the Juche Idea). Article 3 of the Socialist Constitution says the DPRK is guided by “the Juche idea and the Songun idea, a world outlook centred on people, a revolutionary ideology for achieving the independence of the masses of the people” (DPRK Constitution, 2009 text, Article 3). Songun means military first: “Songun politics and Songun-based revolutionary leadership constitute the life of our revolution. Apart from them, the destiny and future of socialism are inconceivable” (Kim Jong Il, Selected Works, volume 15; Kim Jong Il, The Songun-Based Revolutionary Line). Article 9 commits the DPRK to “the complete victory of socialism in the northern half of Korea” through “the three revolutions — ideological, technological and cultural” (DPRK Constitution, 2019 text).

Ho Chi Minh Thought was defined at the CPV’s 9th Congress in 2001 as “a system of comprehensive and profound theoretical and political viewpoints on the fundamental issues of the Vietnamese revolution,” resulting from “the creative application and development of Marxism-Leninism in the specific conditions of our country” (Ho Chi Minh National Academy of Politics, Theory and Practice, quoting the 9th Congress definition). The 7th Congress in 1991 had already established that the Party takes Marxism-Leninism and Ho Chi Minh Thought as its ideological foundation (Theory and Practice). Its codified content includes national liberation, class liberation and human liberation, national independence and unity, “the people’s right to mastery,” a state “of the people, by the people and for the people,” all-people national defence, and revolutionary ethics described as “industriousness, thrift, honesty, righteousness, public-spiritedness and selflessness” (Communist Review, 2007).

Socialism with Chinese characteristics is the umbrella term for the CPC’s post-1978 path, theory, system and culture. The Party Constitution states: “Ultimately, the fundamental reason for all of China’s achievements and progress since reform and opening up began is that the Party has forged a path, formed a theoretical system, established a system, and developed a culture for socialism with Chinese characteristics” (CPC Constitution, 19th Congress). Its architecture includes the “primary stage of socialism,” the basic line of “making economic development the central task, upholding the Four Cardinal Principles, and remaining committed to reform and opening up,” the “socialist market economy” with public ownership as the mainstay, and, since the 19th Congress, Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era (CPC Constitution, 20th Congress; 19th Congress resolution).

Within the tradition, anti-revisionism, Trotskyism and Eurocommunism name three different challenges. Anti-revisionism crystallized in the Sino-Soviet polemic: the CPC’s 1963 Proposal declares that “revisionism is the main danger in the international communist movement” while insisting that “while combating revisionism, Communists must also combat dogmatism” (CPC, Proposal Concerning the General Line, 1963). Trotskyism denotes the current derived from Leon Trotsky’s theory of permanent revolution and his critique of the Soviet state as a degenerated workers’ state (MIA Leon Trotsky Archive). Eurocommunism names the current of Western European and Japanese communist parties in the 1970s that asserted autonomy from Moscow and argued for a parliamentary road to socialism (Azcárate, “What is Eurocommunism?”, 1978; Harman, “Eurocommunism: The State and Revolution,” 1977). Each used the classical vocabulary differently, which is why the same words can carry different meanings in different party documents.

The arguments reviewed here operate at several levels that should not be collapsed. Marx and Engels made claims about history, value and the state; Lenin, Stalin, Mao and others developed and altered those claims in new political settings; parties and governments wrote them into constitutions and programs; and historians have tested the results against evidence about economies and people’s lives. Scholars broadly agree that the texts are central to the tradition and that they contain unresolved tensions, notably over base and superstructure, the status of dialectical laws, the continuity between the early and late Marx, and the derivation of prices from values. They disagree about how those tensions should be resolved. None of those debates can be settled by quoting a party program, because a program states what its authors wanted to be true.
