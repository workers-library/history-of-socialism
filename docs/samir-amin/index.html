Samir Amin: Unequal Development, Delinking and Eurocentrism

A research profile of the Egyptian Marxist economist's account of global capitalism, dependency and the struggle against Eurocentrism, with his critics.

Samir Amin was an Egyptian Marxist economist whose work reshaped how the left understood global capitalism. He argued that poverty in the poorer countries was not an early stage that capitalism would eventually overcome but a result of capitalism itself. He called the mechanism “unequal exchange” — the exchange of goods whose production involves greater differences in wages than in productivity — and held that the rich countries drew value from the poor ones. His best-known political idea was “delinking”, by which he meant refusing to adjust a country’s development to the needs of capitalist global expansion and pursuing an independent strategy instead. He also argued that Eurocentrism, a systematic distortion in dominant social theories that treats Europe as the centre of world history, had to be fought inside social theory itself. This profile reports those arguments as arguments, sets out the evidence his interpreters used, and records the objections made against them.

Biography#

Amin was born in Egypt in 1931 and died in Paris on 12 August 2018, aged 86 (Tricontinental 2018; Global Dialogue 2020; Developing Economics 2021). Tricontinental and the Arab Council for the Social Sciences give Cairo as his birthplace, while the New Socialist obituary says he lived in Port Said until 1946 and then moved to Paris, and the Arab Council records secondary education at a French lycée in 1947 (ACSS; New Socialist 2018). These details are compatible but come from different institutions. No birth certificate or equivalent official record was found for this profile.

His own account of his political formation comes from a 2017 interview with Leo Zeilig for Review of African Political Economy. Amin said he considered himself a communist already at secondary school, understanding the word to mean equality between people and between nations and taking the Russian Revolution as his example. After secondary school he contacted the Egyptian Communist Party and joined it. As a student in France he joined the French Communist Party, following what he called an internationalist rule that communists belonged to the party of the country where they lived. He returned to Egypt in 1957, during Nasser’s rule, and disagreed with Soviet advice that communists should accept the “non-capitalist road” and support Nasser without an independent party. He was in the minority and moved to Mali in 1960 (ROAPE interview, 2017). This first-person account is the strongest evidence for his early political development.

Most scholarly and memorial sources date his doctorate to 1957 (New Socialist 2018; Global Dialogue 2020; Valiani 2021). Tricontinental’s introduction to his last interview says “1956”, and in the interview itself Amin speaks of his PhD dissertation of “1956” (Tricontinental 2018). The discrepancy is not resolved here. What the sources agree on is that the dissertation studied underdevelopment and the mechanisms that produced it, and that it became the basis of Accumulation on a World Scale.

Tricontinental records that Amin worked in Nasser’s Institute for Economic Management in Cairo from 1957 to 1960 and then as an adviser in the Ministry of Planning in Bamako from 1960 to 1963. Amin confirmed the Cairo period in the ROAPE interview and described sitting on the boards of public companies, where he saw a state apparatus being used to build what he called a “bourgeois caste” of private interests inside public enterprises (Tricontinental 2018; ROAPE interview, 2017). He later headed the African Institute for Economic Development and Planning (IDEP) in Dakar and directed the Third World Forum there. In 2017 he listed his continuing affiliations as the Third World Forum, the World Forum for Alternatives and the Arab Centre for Research in Cairo. Global Dialogue credits him with co-founding the Council for the Development of Social Science Research in Africa (CODESRIA) in 1973 and serving as its first executive secretary; other sources say more cautiously that he helped establish CODESRIA and IDEP (Global Dialogue 2020; ROAPE 2021). He divided his later years between Dakar and Cairo.

Two memoirs survive. A Life Looking Forward: Memoirs of an Independent Marxist was published by Zed Books in 2006; the publisher’s current page could not be read, but the title and year appear in the New Socialist obituary and in a Science & Society review noticed by Monthly Review Press (New Socialist 2018; Monthly Review Press 2020). Re-Reading the Postwar Period is dated 1994 by Monthly Review Press, which lists it as a separate book (Monthly Review Press); New Socialist calls it “Rereading the Postwar Era” and describes it as an intellectual autobiography, but the publisher’s title is used here. A second volume of memoirs, The Long Revolution of the Global South, was published posthumously in 2019 (Monthly Review Press). No work by Amin called A Life of Learning appears in any publisher or author record opened for this profile; that title could not be verified and is not attributed to him. The verified memoir is A Life Looking Forward (2006).

Intellectual tradition#

Amin called himself a communist without qualification in 2017 and described Marxism as a method to be carried forward rather than repeated. In Tricontinental’s last interview he said: “I think Marxism is more important and relevant today than ever”, and added that socialists should not simply repeat what Marx wrote but “continue his mode of thinking – that is, to give Marxist answers to present challenges” (Tricontinental 2018). Global Dialogue describes him as an “independent Marxist” who treated Marx as a starting point rather than an authority, and as an opponent of economism and of the “stageist” idea that backward societies must simply catch up with advanced ones (Global Dialogue 2020). The scholars who edited a 2021 special issue on his work report that he called himself a “creative Marxist” (Developing Economics 2021).

Monthly Review Press describes him as “one of Africa’s leading Marxists and world-systems analysts”, known for non-Eurocentric Marxism, imperial rent, the global monopoly phase of capitalism, delinking and the call for a Fifth International (Monthly Review Press author record). He is routinely placed in dependency theory — the view that poorer countries are kept poor by economic ties to richer ones — and in world-systems analysis, but he did not treat those labels as a settled school. In his 1973 paper he located himself in an argument with Arghiri Emmanuel, Charles Bettelheim and Christian Palloix over international value, and criticised what he saw as the silence of Marxists on international trade (Amin, “The End of a Debate”, 1973). Valiani’s survey in the Journal of World-Systems Research argues that he was central to the emergence of world-historical analysis while remaining distinctive in how he combined structure, historical contingencies and political commitment (Valiani 2021).

Amin rejected the idea that a delinking project could be led by a national bourgeoisie. Asked in 2017 whether critics had been unfair to accuse him of promoting one, he answered that a project led by bourgeois forces “will never go beyond a small class”, whereas one powered by popular forces would raise the questions of industrialisation, peasant agriculture, food and control of foreign capital (ROAPE interview, 2017). That answer is the clearest evidence for how he wanted his political label understood.

Major contributions#

The first contribution was to treat “underdevelopment” not as an early stage that poor countries were passing through but as a product of capitalism itself. His doctoral work asked why capitalism in the Third World did not resemble capitalism in the First World. In Unequal Development he described peripheral social formations — the poorer economies on the edge of the world system, as against the rich “centre” — as a dominant mode of production articulated with subordinate modes, and identified three distortions: an export bias that absorbed capital arriving from the centre, a bias toward tertiary activities, and a bias toward light industry using modern techniques. These distortions weakened the “multiplier effects” (the knock-on growth that spreads spending through an economy) needed for accumulation driven by the home market, which he called autocentric accumulation (New Socialist 2018, quoting Unequal Development pp. 288, 294 and 76; Jackson et al. 1979, quoting Accumulation on a World Scale pp. 169–70). The quotations are from the secondary works; the books themselves were not opened for this profile.

His second contribution was a revision of unequal exchange. In the 1973 IDEP paper “The End of a Debate”, later reprinted in Imperialism and Unequal Development, he argued that Emmanuel had been right to challenge Ricardian comparative advantage but wrong to treat wage levels as an independent variable and wrong to assume that the products of centre and periphery had “irreducible use values”. Amin’s own definition, in the English text of that paper, is “the exchange of products whose production involves wage differentials greater than those of productivity”. He added that “the ‘specificity’ of products was always a myth, the result of commodity alienation”, and that most Third World exports were produced with techniques similar to those used in the centre (Amin 1973). The downloaded PDF is a scan with noisy optical character recognition; the wording above is legible in the extracted text but should be checked against a clean edition before it is printed as an exact quotation. The paper carries the IDEP reference IDEP/ET/R/2558 and is dated September 1973.

The third contribution was delinking. In the 2017 ROAPE interview Amin called it “a principle of strategy. It is not a blueprint.” He explained it as refusing unilateral adjustment to the needs of capitalist global expansion and instead trying “to have your own strategy, independent of the trends of the unequal global system” (ROAPE interview, 2017). In his last interview he was blunter: “De-link is a slogan. I use it as a slogan.” He said total delinking was impossible, that large countries might delink “fifty per cent” or “seventy per cent” of their economies, and that “De-linking only means compelling imperialism to accept your conditions or part of those conditions” (Tricontinental 2018). Tricontinental’s 2026 research publication quotes the book itself at greater length. The passage, cited there to Delinking: Towards a Polycentric World (Zed Books, 1990), p. 62, says that delinking is a “refusal to subject the national development strategy to the imperatives of ‘worldwide expansion’”, is “in no way synonymous with autarky” (self-sufficiency without trade), and means “pursuit of a system of rational criteria for economic options founded on a law of value on a national basis with popular relevance, independent of such criteria of economic rationality as flow from the dominance of the capitalist law of value operating on a world scale” (Tricontinental 2026). A second formulation, quoted by New Socialist from Maldevelopment pp. 109–110, calls delinking “not synonymous with autarky but only the subjection of external relations to the logic of internal development” and “the refusal to submit to the demands of the worldwide law of value” (New Socialist 2018).

The fourth contribution was the concept of Eurocentrism as a structure within social theory rather than merely a prejudice held by Europeans. Georg Stauth’s 1992 review quotes Amin’s definition: Eurocentrism is “a distortion – albeit a systematic and important one – from which the majority of dominant social theories and ideologies suffer”, a paradigm that works “spontaneously, often in the gray areas of seemingly obvious facts and common sense” (Stauth 1992, quoting Eurocentrism, Monthly Review Press, 1989). Amin’s alternative was the “tributary mode of production”, a general pattern for pre-capitalist societies in which religion was dominant, and a reading of European history that put the Islamic world and China at the centre and treated Europe as a latecomer. In 2017 he told Zeilig that he had “dared even to write that the most advanced parts of the pre-capitalist world, were not where change starts”, and that China had a civil service a thousand years before Europe (ROAPE interview, 2017).

Analysis of capitalism#

Amin’s unit of analysis was the world capitalist system. Global Dialogue summarises his position as the claim that polarisation between centre and periphery, imperial rent and unequal development are built into that system, not temporary deviations (Global Dialogue 2020). The 1973 paper set out the mechanism: the extension of capitalism raises the rate of profit of central capital, peripheral growth takes place under competition from the centre, and an “unequal specialization” perpetuates underdevelopment through a growing divergence in the rewards of labour (Amin 1973; Jackson et al. 1979, quoting Accumulation on a World Scale pp. 89–90).

In the last interview he divided monopoly capitalism into two stages. The first, from the late nineteenth century to the Second World War, was national in character; Hobson and Hilferding analysed it, and Lenin argued that the imperialist powers fought one another as well as subjugating the periphery. The second, which he dated from the mid-1970s, was “generalized monopoly capital”, in which monopoly capital had turned other forms of social production into sub-contractors and absorbed much of the value created worldwide as “imperialist rent” — the extra value captured by the dominant countries from the rest of the world. He insisted that the state did not dissolve under globalisation: monopoly capital needed its military and police apparatus, and it had been “domesticat[ed] to serve the exclusive interests of the imperialists” (Tricontinental 2018). Global Dialogue lists the five monopolies he associated with this phase: weapons of mass destruction, technology, financial flows, planetary resources and communications (Global Dialogue 2020).

Monthly Review Press’s descriptions of the later books add the economic detail. The Law of Worldwide Value extends Marx’s scheme in Capital Volume II with a third department devoted to surplus absorption and treats “imperialist rent” as an extension of the law of value into a “law of globalized value”; the 2010 edition places the present crisis within a second long crisis of capitalism dating from the early 1970s (Monthly Review Press, The Law of Worldwide Value). Modern Imperialism, Monopoly Finance Capital, and Marx’s Law of Value describes the same system as global oligopolistic capitalism in which finance capital dominates production and distribution, and says Amin answered critics of his value theory in letters included in the volume (Monthly Review Press, 2018). The Liberal Virus argues that the American state serves capital alone and imposes its model through “low-intensity democracy”, a form in which elections displace class politics (Monthly Review Press, The Liberal Virus). The Implosion of Contemporary Capitalism presents financial crisis, emerging powers, financialisation, political Islam and the eurozone crisis as parts of one historically grounded account of monopoly capitalism in its global form (Monthly Review Press, 2013). These passages are publisher descriptions of his arguments, not independent confirmation that the arguments are sound.

Analysis of socialism#

Amin did not write a detailed blueprint for a future society, and in 2017 he said directly of his scheme for international organisation, “I don’t have a blueprint for this” (ROAPE interview, 2017). What he did offer was a strategy of transition. Delinking required a “sovereign popular national project”: national because state power had to be changed in the existing countries before it could be changed regionally or globally, and popular because the project could not be a bourgeois one. He rejected foreign capital only insofar as it could not be controlled; the aim was sovereignty through industrialisation, revived peasant agriculture and food production, and control over capital flows (ROAPE interview, 2017). Global Dialogue adds that he gave delinking a regional dimension, through blocs in West or Southern Africa, and linked it to food sovereignty and to the idea of a polycentric world (Global Dialogue 2020).

His view of actually existing socialism was critical but not dismissive. In the 2017 interview he called the Soviet, Chinese and Bandung “pillars” and said each reached its historical limits by the 1980s and 1990s. He used the term “Thermidor” — retreat from revolutionary goals — for the Soviet Union at different possible dates and for China from around 1950, while still treating the Chinese revolution as a real revolution whose later turn under Deng Xiaoping moved China toward capitalist globalisation. He preferred the phrase “revolutionary advances” to a single revolution, and he rejected the idea that the world could be changed without seizing state power, though he said he did not know what organisational forms would work now (ROAPE interview, 2017; Tricontinental 2018).

For the international dimension he proposed an “internationalism of peoples” rather than an international of states. Monthly Review Press’s description of The World We Wish to See says he argued that effective opposition must be built through a “convergence in diversity” of workers, peasants, students and others, and through a new, open international that could coordinate resistance to global capitalism (Monthly Review Press, 2008). In the last interview he said any new International could not reproduce the Third International, which had depended on a strong Soviet state; it had to be built from the common ground of different movements (Tricontinental 2018). Global Dialogue reports that in his last years he argued for a Fifth International of workers and peoples, and had grown critical of the limits of the World Social Forum (Global Dialogue 2020).

Recommended reading#

These works are starting points into Amin’s argument rather than a measure of his whole output; the complete list is above.

  • Accumulation on a World Scale: A Critique of the Theory of Underdevelopment (1974) — This is the best place to begin with the doctoral work that asked why capitalism in the Third World did not resemble capitalism in the First World and made underdevelopment a product of capitalism itself.
  • Unequal Development: An Essay on the Social Formations of Peripheral Capitalism (1976) — It is the clearest place to see the three distortions of peripheral economies that weaken the home-market accumulation he called autocentric: an export bias, a bias toward tertiary activities, and a bias toward light industry using modern techniques.
  • “The End of a Debate” (1973) — This is the clearest starting point for his revision of unequal exchange, where he accepts Emmanuel’s challenge to comparative advantage but argues that wage levels cannot be treated as an independent variable.
  • Delinking: Towards a Polycentric World (1985/1990) — This is the best place to see his strategy of refusing to subject national development to the imperatives of capitalist global expansion without turning it into autarky, subordinating external relations instead to the logic of internal development.
  • Eurocentrism (1989/2010) — It is the best entry to his claim that Eurocentrism is a systematic distortion in dominant social theories, and to the alternative reading of pre-capitalist history that puts the Islamic world and China at the centre.

Selected lectures and interviews#

The strongest verified audiovisual-related item is a written interview. Tricontinental: Institute for Social Research interviewed Amin shortly before his death; the interviewers were Jipson John and Jitheesh P. M., and the notebook was published on 29 October 2018 as Globalisation and Its Alternative: An Interview with Samir Amin (landing page; PDF text). The full PDF was opened and its text read. It is the main source here for his own account of globalisation, monopoly capital and delinking.

Leo Zeilig interviewed Amin in Dakar on 5 February 2017 for ROAPE. The interview followed a talk Amin gave on the 150th anniversary of Capital at the “Economic Saturdays” series organised by ARCADE, the Dakar initiative associated with Ndongo Sylla (ROAPE interview, 2017). The page contains photographs of Amin speaking at that event, but the event itself was not verified as a recording. The interview text was opened and read in full.

The Arab Council for the Social Sciences runs a Samir Amin Lecture Series in Beirut. Its page records a first lecture in 2024 by Amr Adly on “The Legacy of Samir Amin and the Future of Critical Political Economy” and a second in 2026 on “Dependency Theory and Delinking: Contemporary Perspectives and Relevance”, and links to a YouTube video and two lecture publications (ACSS). These are lectures about Amin, not lectures by him. The specific speaker for the linked video and the recording date were not confirmed from the page, so no attribution is made.

Intellectual influences#

The evidence for influence comes mostly from Amin’s own statements. Marx is the central figure: he told Tricontinental that the Communist Manifesto remained more relevant than any mid-nineteenth-century text and that socialists should continue Marx’s method (Tricontinental 2018). He credited Lenin with the analysis of national monopoly capital and its rivalries, and Hobson and Hilferding with the earlier analysis of monopoly; he quoted approvingly Mahdi Amel on freedom and referred to Mao’s slogan of “fire on the party headquarters” during the Cultural Revolution in accounting for his own radicalisation (Tricontinental 2018; ROAPE interview, 2017). In his 1973 paper he engaged directly with Arghiri Emmanuel’s L’échange inégal, with Marx’s remarks on international trade, with Bukharin, and with Bettelheim and Palloix (Amin 1973).

Monthly Review Press’s description of Modern Imperialism says his later value theory drew on Michal Kalecki, Josef Steindl, Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy as well as Marx (Monthly Review Press, 2018). Stauth’s review notes his use of Martin Bernal’s thesis about nineteenth-century “Hellenomania” in Eurocentrism (Stauth 1992). Valiani’s survey places him in dialogue with Braudel, Wallerstein, Frank, Quijano and others without claiming that any one of them determined his position (Valiani 2021). Political influences he named in interview included Nasser, Nehru and the Bandung generation, while his criticisms of Nasser’s and Sadat’s Egypt and of post-Mao China show that these were points of departure rather than models to copy (ROAPE interview, 2017).

Influence and legacy#

Amin helped build institutions as well as arguments. His work at IDEP and the Third World Forum in Dakar, his part in CODESRIA, and his involvement in the World Forum for Alternatives and the World Social Forum are documented in Global Dialogue, in the ROAPE interviews with scholars who knew him, and in his own 2017 interview (Global Dialogue 2020; ROAPE 2021; ROAPE interview, 2017). Tricontinental’s 2026 publication treats delinking as a concept with “significant scholarly attention” and tests its predictive value against the fragmentation of the world order (Tricontinental 2026).

His concepts have been taken up by decolonial and African scholars. Sabelo Ndlovu-Gatsheni told ROAPE that Delinking, Eurocentrism and the idea of “extraversion” shaped his work on decolonisation and epistemic freedom, and that he read Amin alongside Walter Mignolo and Paulin Hountondji (ROAPE 2021). Ndongo Samba Sylla used Amin’s “imperialist rent” in work on fair trade and the CFA franc, and reported that Amin donated his personal library to ENDA Tiers Monde in Dakar in 2018 (ROAPE 2021). The editors of the 2021 ROAPE special issue record that younger scholars used his categories to study finance, Chinese and East Asian development, agrarian questions and ecological dependency (Developing Economics 2021). Valiani argues that his influence on world-systems research came from his insistence on combining structure with historical specificity and from his political commitment (Valiani 2021).

His legacy is also a matter of continuing dispute. The Arab Council’s 2026 lecture description asks whether dependency theory remains viable after the rise of China and other manufacturing powers, and notes that the term “Global South” now covers cases with little in common (ACSS). That question, rather than a settled verdict, is what the sources support.

Criticisms and controversies#

Criticism of Amin falls into three groups. The first concerns the logical status of his theory. S. Smith’s 1980 article in The Journal of Development Studies was titled “The ideas of Samir Amin: Theory or tautology?” (Crossref record). Only the bibliographic record was opened; the full text was not, so no quotation from it is used. Charles A. Barone’s 1982 article in Review of Radical Political Economics, whose abstract was opened through Crossref, argues that although Amin improved on dependency theory, he remained within its methodological limits and restricted his analysis to the sphere of circulation, which Barone considers damaging to a full understanding of imperialism (Barone 1982). This is a criticism from within the Marxist tradition, not from an opponent of it.

The second group concerns empirical testability. Jackson, Russett, Snidal and Sylvan’s 1979 survey in Latin American Research Review finds that Amin offers a synthesis of earlier dependency strands and quotes his chain of reasoning about unequal specialisation, but it also stresses that dependencia arguments differ in how much causal weight they give to external forces and in how they define classes; the authors call for closer examination of the specific mechanisms and for testing across countries and over time (Jackson et al. 1979). ACSS’s 2026 lecture summary gives the standard objections: excessive emphasis on external constraints, neglect of internal class conflict and political struggle, and the challenge supposedly posed by East Asian industrialisation within the capitalist system (ACSS). These are summaries by an academic council, not the full text of a research article.

The third group concerns his cultural and political analysis. Stauth’s review of Eurocentrism calls the book’s central concept vague, says Amin ignores earlier work on “Occidentalism”, remains tied to a Marxist evolutionary sequence and to a “teleology” for the Third World, and argues that the framework of capitalist culture leaves little room for mass or consumer culture or for newer theorising about cultural relativism (Stauth 1992). Ama Mazama’s 1995 review in Journal of Black Studies, pp. 760–764, is cited by the ROAPE editors as an example of criticism that Amin’s Eurocentrism is economically reductionist (Crossref record; Developing Economics 2021). Mazama’s review itself was not opened.

The ROAPE editors also identify blind spots in Amin’s framework: his analysis largely excluded gender, treated the national state as the main locus of change, and needed to be extended to ecological and financial questions (Developing Economics 2021). In a 2017 interview he defended the national level as necessary for changing state power, and critics in the same tradition disputed whether that gave too much weight to the state and not enough to working-class struggle; those reader comments are opinion, not evidence, and are not used here as scholarly criticism. One further factual caution: ACSS says Amin “coined” Eurocentrism, but the term predates his book and the sources opened here do not establish authorship of the word. That claim is not repeated here.

Related thinkers#

Arghiri Emmanuel is the closest theoretical interlocutor: Amin accepted his challenge to comparative advantage but rejected his treatment of wages and international value (Amin 1973). Charles Bettelheim and Christian Palloix were the other named opponents in that debate. André Gunder Frank, Immanuel Wallerstein and Giovanni Arrighi were collaborators and fellow world-systems thinkers; the four co-authored Dynamics of Global Crisis (Monthly Review Press). Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy supplied the monopoly-capital framework that Amin extended, according to the publisher’s description of his 2018 collection (Monthly Review Press, 2018). Lenin, Hobson, Hilferding, Bukharin and Mao appear in his own accounts of imperialism and revolution (Tricontinental 2018; Amin 1973).

Among later thinkers, Ndlovu-Gatsheni, Mignolo, Hountondji, Ndongo Samba Sylla, Jayati Ghosh and Catherine Scott either extend or revise his categories, particularly on decolonisation, monetary dependency, finance and gender (ROAPE 2021; Developing Economics 2021). Bill Warren is a point of dispute in the literature on whether capitalism in the periphery develops or merely reproduces dependence (Barone 1982). These figures are named in plain text rather than linked because no profile pages exist for them in this collection.
