Malcolm X

Life, the OAAU, the Pan-African and anti-imperialist turn, and all of his available speeches and writings.

Malcolm X was born Malcolm Little in Omaha, Nebraska, in 1925 and was shot dead in the Audubon Ballroom in Harlem on 21 February 1965, aged thirty-nine (New York Times, 22 February 1965; National Archives, “Malcolm X”). In the twelve years before his death he moved through several distinct roles: a street hustler and prison convert, a minister of the Nation of Islam, the breakaway founder of two new organisations, and, in the last year of his life, a Black nationalist and internationalist who argued that the struggle of Black Americans belonged before the United Nations. His ideas changed, and the record of that change survives in speeches, interviews, letters and one famous book. The Autobiography of Malcolm X, told to the journalist Alex Haley, was published in 1965, nine months after the assassination, and has shaped nearly every later account of the man. It is also a collaborative and retrospective text, assembled under conditions that historians have only recently begun to reconstruct. This chapter follows the documented sequence of Malcolm’s positions and uses the Autobiography for what it is: an extraordinary source that must be read alongside the speeches and the scholarship.

The written record on which this account rests has one unusual feature. Many of Malcolm’s speeches survive in the Malcolm X Files, an activist transcript archive published as a blog with no stated editorial procedure. Where a text also exists in the Marxists Internet Archive, the Internet Archive, the Malcolm-X.org document collection, or the Columbia University Malcolm X Project, those versions are cited here as well. For a number of the shorter talks and interviews the blog transcription is the only version readily available, and the reader should treat those transcripts as one witness rather than as a settled text. Dates given for undated items follow the archive’s own index.

Omaha, the Littles, and Garveyism#

The Autobiography opens with a threat rather than a birth date. Malcolm wrote that when his mother was pregnant with him, “a party of hooded Ku Klux Klan riders galloped up to our home in Omaha, Nebraska, one night. Surrounding the house, brandishing their shotguns and rifles, they shouted for my father to come out.” The Klansmen had come because his father was “spreading trouble” among Black people in Omaha with “the ‘back to Africa’ preachings of Marcus Garvey” (The Autobiography of Malcolm X, Chapter One, “Nightmare”). Garveyism was the movement founded by Marcus Garvey, which urged people of African descent to build their own institutions, take pride in African identity and consider Africa a homeland. It was a framework of Black self-organisation that reached this family before any contact with the Nation of Islam.

Malcolm identified his father as a Baptist minister and a Garvey organiser: “My father, the Reverend Earl Little, was a Baptist minister, a dedicated organizer for Marcus Aurelius Garvey’s U.N.I.A. (Universal Negro Improvement Association)” (Ibid., Chapter One). His mother is named in the same chapter: “My mother was twenty-eight when I was born on May 19, 1925, in an Omaha hospital” and “Louise Little, my mother, who was born in Grenada, in the British West Indies, looked like a white woman. Her father was white” (Ibid.). Attallah Shabazz’s foreword to the 1996 edition spells the family name “Louisa” and describes both grandparents as “crusaders for Marcus Garvey’s Universal Negro Improvement Association, acting as chapter president and writer/translator for more than a decade” (Ibid., Foreword). Malcolm’s narrative uses “Louise”; the foreword uses “Louisa”. Both spellings appear in the book.

The Autobiography records the murder of Earl Little and the breakup of the household. Malcolm states his family’s conviction that “my father was finally himself to die by the white man’s hands,” and he expected the same fate: “It has always been my belief that I, too, will die by violence. I have done all that I can to be prepared” (Ibid., Chapter One). After the death, “the welfare people” intervened and the children were placed in foster homes. Malcolm described the system from a child’s point of view: “I think they felt that getting children into foster homes was a legitimate part of their function, and the result would be less troublesome, however they went about it. And when my mother fought them, they went after her — first, through me” (Ibid., Chapter Four, “Laura”). His sister Ella arranged his removal from the foster system to Boston. School in Lansing and Boston ended in dropping out. At sixteen he worked on the railroad as a waiter, then moved into petty crime and hustling in Harlem, the period covered by Chapters Five through Nine of the Autobiography (“Harlemite,” “Detroit Red,” “Hustler,” “Trapped,” “Caught”). He was arrested for burglary in 1941 and sentenced to Charlestown State Prison (Ibid., Chapters Eight and Nine).

Manning Marable’s Malcolm X: A Life of Reinvention, a major scholarly biography published in 2011 and awarded the Pulitzer Prize for History, argues that the Autobiography exaggerated the extent and drama of Malcolm’s early criminal life. Interviewing Malcolm’s friends in Boston and New York, the historians Les and Tamara Payne established that he had worked as a marijuana dealer, a racketeer and an armed robber before his conviction for larceny, but Marable holds that Haley pressed for anecdotes that would contrast with the prison conversion (Manning Marable, Malcolm X: A Life of Reinvention; Alex White, “How Alex Haley wrote and reframed the life of Malcolm X,” Aeon, 31 July 2025). Marable’s book is itself contested. A collection of essays by Jared A. Ball and Todd Steven Burroughs, A Lie of Reinvention (2012), challenges his accuracy and interpretation, and some scholars have argued that he overstated the discontinuity between the early and late Malcolm (AAIHS, “Marable’s Malcolm: A Revisitation,” 2016). The Autobiography’s opening chapters are therefore evidence for how Malcolm and Haley wanted the early years to be remembered, not a transparent record of them.

Prison, conversion, and the Nation of Islam#

The Autobiography narrates Malcolm’s prison years as an intellectual and moral conversion. In Charlestown he was repeatedly confined in solitary, “pacing for hours like a caged leopard, viciously cursing aloud to myself.” His “favorite targets” were “the Bible and God,” until “the men in the cellblock had a name for me: ‘Satan.’ Because of my antireligious attitude” (Autobiography, Chapter Ten, “Satan”). The first prisoner who made “any positive impression” on him was “Bimbi,” an older burglar whose learning and self-possession were such that “guards, even… wander over close to hear Bimbi on any subject” (Ibid.). Malcolm presents Bimbi’s example, a Black convict who mastered books and commanded respect, as the beginning of his own study habit.

The doctrinal turn came through his brother Reginald. Malcolm quotes him: “He told me that this God had come to America, and that he had made himself known to a man named Elijah — ‘a black man, just like us.’ This God had let Elijah know, Reginald said, that the devil’s ‘time was up.’” When Malcolm asked whether all white people were meant, Reginald answered: “Them … The white man is the devil. … especially Masons” (Ibid., Chapter Ten). Malcolm records that he later reproduced this teaching to other prisoners: “I’d drop it on him, what Mr. Muhammad taught: ‘The white man is the devil’” (Ibid.). The Nation of Islam was the Black Muslim religious movement led by Elijah Muhammad, which taught that Black people were the original people and that white society was morally corrupt; it is not the same as mainstream Islam.

The line appears verbatim in the movement’s doctrinal text. Elijah Muhammad’s The Supreme Wisdom: Solution to the so-called NEGROES’ Problem, first edition 26 February 1957, is catalogued by the Internet Archive with the author lines “Mr. Elijah Muhammad,” “Messenger of Allah,” and “to the Lost-Found Nation of Islam in North America” (Elijah Muhammad, The Supreme Wisdom, Vol. I). The introduction, written by Abdul Basit Nacem, describes Muhammad as “the leader and spiritual head of several thousand Moslems in the United States of America, all of whom are of African descent and converts from Christianity,” and notes his weekly column in the Pittsburgh Courier with “a circulation of almost 200,000” (Ibid.).

Malcolm was transferred in late 1948 to the Norfolk, Massachusetts, Prison Colony, which he called “comparatively… a heaven,” with “no bars, only walls” and “each inmate had his own room” (Autobiography, Chapter Eleven, “Saved”). After his release in 1952 he became a minister of the Nation of Islam, eventually minister of Temple No. 7 in Harlem and a prominent national spokesman for the movement. The 1960 Harlem Freedom Rally speech shows his function inside the organisation at that date. It is built around “Mr. Muhammad says,” calling on Black people to “stop carrying guns and knives to harm each other,” to “Save your money,” and to “Elevate the black woman; respect her and protect her” (Malcolm X, Harlem Freedom Rally, 1960).

Two themes already present in this period would later detach from the Nation of Islam framework. The first was internationalism: “When I was in Africa last year I was deeply impressed by the desire of our African Brothers for peace, but even they agree that there can be no peace without freedom from colonialism, foreign domination, oppression and exploitation” (Ibid.). The second was a refusal to confine the struggle to the United States. At Yale in October 1962 he spoke of “the refugees” and identified “black, brown, red and yellow” peoples worldwide as part of one historical movement (Malcolm X, Yale Law School, 20 October 1962).

The break with Elijah Muhammad#

Three documents record the break from Malcolm’s own side. The Pierre Berton interview of 19 January 1965 explains the remark that precipitated the suspension. Asked about President Kennedy’s assassination, Malcolm said:

“I pointed out that it was a case of the chickens coming home to roost, by which I meant that this was the result of seeds that had been sown, that this was the harvest. This was taken out of context, and reported in one of the papers, and Elijah Muhammad, who had been waiting for me to make a move that would enable him to suspend me and get the support of the public in doing so, took advantage of that opportunity.”

Source: Malcolm X, Pierre Berton Interview, 19 January 1965

The letter to Elijah Muhammad of 11 March 1964 states: “The National Officials there at the Chicago Headquarters know that I never left the Nation of Islam of my own free will. It was they who conspired with Captain Joseph here in New York to pressure me out of the Nation. In order to save the National Officials and Captian Joseph [sic] the disgrace of having to explain for forcing me out, I announced through the press that it was my own decision to leave” (Malcolm X, Letter to Elijah Muhammad, 11 March 1964). The same letter adds: “I will always be a Muslim, teaching what you have taught me, and giving you full credit for what I know and what I am. You are still my leader and teacher” (Ibid.). At that moment, in his own words, Malcolm presented the break as a dispute about authority and organisational conduct, not as a repudiation of the Nation of Islam’s religious teaching.

The last interview with Al-Muslimoon in February 1965 gives a sharper retrospective account: “Elijah Muhammad himself had already given the order to have me killed because he feared I would expose to his followers the secret of his extreme immorality” (Malcolm X, Al-Muslimoon Interview, February 1965). The two statements were made in different circumstances and should not be merged into one. The movement-press chronology of the independent period is precise: “Malcolm left the Nation of Islam (‘Black Muslims’) in March 1964… That month he organized the Muslim Mosque, Inc., and in June, 1964, he founded the non-religious Organization of Afro-American Unity. He was abroad — in Africa, the Middle East and Europe — during half of this independent phase of his life, which ended after a short fifty weeks with his assassination in New York on February 21, 1965” (George Breitman, Introduction to “Afro-American History,” March 1967).

Two organisations: the Muslim Mosque and the OAAU#

In the Al-Muslimoon interview Malcolm described the two bodies. “With what little finance we could raise, we have founded the Muslim Mosque, Inc., with headquarters here in Harlem. Our sole interest is to help undo the distorted image [that] we have helped spread about Islam. Our mosque also is for those who want to learn how to live the life of a true Muslim.” Then came the institutional distinction: “However, since we live as Black Americans in a white racist society, we have established another organization which is non-religious, known as the Organization of Afro-American Unity (OAAU), and which is designed to unite all Black Americans regardless of their religious affiliation into a group that can fight against American racism and the economic, political, and social evils that stem from white racism here in this American society” (Al-Muslimoon Interview). His “Declaration of Faith” of 8 April 1964 is a brief statement of the religious turn: “I do hereby declare that I am a true Muslim. I bear witness that there is no God but Allah. And I bear witness that Muhammad [ibn Abdullah] is the Apostle of Allah, and His Last Prophet” (Malcolm X, Declaration of Faith, 8 April 1964).

The OAAU’s founding rally of 28 June 1964 is preserved in full. Malcolm explained the model: “One of the first things that the independent African nations did was to form an organization called the Organization of African Unity. This organization consists of all independent African states who have reached the agreement to submerge all differences and combine their efforts toward eliminating from the continent of Africa colonialism and all vestiges of oppression and exploitation being suffered by Africa” (Malcolm X, OAAU Founding Rally, 28 June 1964; the same passage is independently preserved at the ICIT Digital Library). He drew the conclusion: “Once we saw what they were able to do, we determined to try and do the same thing here in America among Afro-Americans who have been divided by our enemies. So we have formed an organization known as the Organization of Afro-American Unity which has the same aim and objective: to fight whoever gets in our way, to bring about the complete independence of people of African descent here in the Western Hemisphere, and first here in the United States, and bring about the freedom of these people by any means necessary” (Ibid.). The charter he read to the rally defined “peace and security” politically: “we have to eliminate the barking of the police dogs, we have to eliminate the police clubs, we have to eliminate the water hoses, we have to eliminate all of these things that have become so characteristic of the American so-called dream” (Ibid.). Its second heading was “Self Defense,” and its asserted right was “to protect themselves by whatever means necessary” (Ibid.).

The programme opened with a sentence that summarised the change of strategy: “The time is past due for us to internationalize the problems of Afro-Americans. We have been too slow in recognizing the link in the fate of Africans with the fate of Afro-Americans” (Program of the Organization of Afro-American Unity). George Breitman noted that Malcolm approved this programme although he did not write it, while the founding-rally speech was Malcolm’s own words (Breitman, March 1967). This distinction matters, because the OAAU documents are an organisational text that Malcolm endorsed rather than a speech he delivered verbatim.

The Autobiography’s silence about the OAAU is one sign of editorial shaping. Marable observed that “there’s only a fleeting reference to the OAAU … inside of the book that’s supposed to be his political testament,” and linked the omission to three chapters Malcolm wrote that were removed before publication, titled “The Negro,” “Twenty Million Black Muslims” and “The End of Christianity” (Democracy Now! interview with Manning Marable; Amy E. Earhart, “Alex Haley’s ‘The Malcolm X I Knew’ and Notecards from The Autobiography of Malcolm X,” Scholarly Editing 35, 2014). The literary scholar Keith Miller of Arizona State University, who has spent a decade studying the book’s composition, describes it as “a composite — it’s written by Malcolm X with his collaborator, and as it turns out, a lot of other people; his editors and libel lawyers” (ASU News, 2016). Haley’s notes for the book survive at Texas A&M University, written in green ink while Malcolm used red, and Earhart has published a selection of them alongside Haley’s 1965 essay “The Malcolm X I Knew” (Scholarly Editing).

The hajj and the travels of 1964#

Malcolm established the sequence himself: “I have traveled to the Middle East and Africa twice since leaving Elijah Muhammad in March of 1964, mainly to get a better understanding of Islam and the African countries, and in turn to give the Muslim world a better understanding of problems facing those of us here in America who are trying to become Muslims” (Al-Muslimoon Interview).

The hajj, the Muslim pilgrimage to Mecca, changed his public position on race. The “Letter from Mecca” of April 1964 records what he saw: “There were tens of thousands of pilgrims, from all over the world. They were of all colors, from blue-eyed blonds to black-skinned Africans. But we were all participating in the same ritual, displaying a spirit of unity and brotherhood that my experiences in America had led me to believe never could exist between the white and non-white” (Malcolm X, Letter from Mecca, April 1964; the same text, dated 20 April 1964, is in the collected speeches and the Internet Archive collection). He drew the consequence explicitly:

“You may be shocked by these words coming from me. But on this pilgrimage, what I have seen, and experienced, has forced me to re-arrange much of my thought-patterns previously held, and to toss aside some of my previous conclusions. This was not too difficult for me. Despite my firm convictions, I have always been a man who tries to face facts, and to accept the reality of life as new experience and new knowledge unfolds it.”

Source: Malcolm X, Letter from Mecca, April 1964

The letter is signed “El-Hajj Malik El-Shabazz (Malcolm X)” (Ibid.). The Autobiography’s Chapters Seventeen and Eighteen narrate the same events at book length, and large parts of those chapters were taken directly from Malcolm’s travel diary (Alex White, Aeon, 2025). Attallah Shabazz summarises the political result: “With my mother’s blessings for his journey, my father set out to visit Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana, Nasser of Egypt, Prince Faisal of Saudi Arabia, and more” (Autobiography, Foreword).

In West Africa the “Letter from Lagos” records the reception: “here in Africa they love me as soon as they learn that I am Malcolm X of the militant American Muslims. Africans in general and Muslims in particular love militancy.” It states the strategic purpose: “I spoke at Ibadan University in Nigeria, Friday night, and gave the true picture of our plight in America, and of the necessity of the independent African nations helping us bring our case before the United Nations.” And it records the name given to him: “They made me an honorary member of the ‘Muslim Students Society of Nigeria,’ and renamed me ‘Omowale,’ which means ‘the child has come home’ in the Yoruba language” (Malcolm X, Letters from Lagos and Accra). The Accra letter gives the Pan-African conclusion in one sentence. Pan-Africanism is the belief that people of African descent everywhere share a common political and cultural interest: “it is time for all African-Americans to become an integral part of the world’s Pan-Africanists, and even though we might remain in America physically while fighting for the benefits the Constitution guarantees us, we must ‘return’ to Africa philosophically and culturally and develop a working unity in the framework of Pan-Africanism” (Ibid.).

At the University of Ghana on 13 May 1964, Malcolm told his audience: “There is probably no more enlightened leader on the African continent than President Nkrumah, because he lived in America. He knows what it is like there,” and more politically, “President Nkrumah is doing something there that the government in America does not like to see done, and that is he’s restoring the African image” (Malcolm X, University of Ghana, 13 May 1964). His retrospective list of audiences in the Autobiography names “President Gamal Abdel Nasser, of Egypt; President Julius K. Nyerere, of Tanzania; President Nnamoi Aziki-we [sic], of Nigeria; Osagyefo Dr. Kwame Nkrumah, of Ghana; President Sekou Toure, of Guinea; President Jomo Kenyatta, of Kenya; and Prime Minister Dr. Milton Obote, of Uganda” (Autobiography, Chapter Eighteen). On Algeria the Autobiography is precise: “It was Tuesday, May 19, 1964-my thirty-ninth birthday-when I arrived in Algiers… I walked around Algiers, hearing rank-and-file expressions of hatred for America for supporting the oppressors of the Algerians. They were true revolutionists, not afraid of death” (Ibid., Chapter Seventeen). His published judgment had already been given in April: “Another example is the Algerian revolution, led by Ben Bella; they threw out the French who had been there over 100 years” (Ibid., Chapter Eighteen; compare “The Black Revolution,” 8 April 1964).

Two articles written by Malcolm himself in Cairo in August and September 1964 belong to this Egyptian phase. “Zionist Logic” of 17 September 1964 analyses “Zionist dollarism” as “the modern 20th century weapon of neo-imperialism” (MIA reprint; Malcolm-X.org text). “Racism: The Cancer That is Destroying America” of 25 August 1964 states: “In the past I permitted myself to be used by Elijah Muhammad… to make sweeping indictments of all white people, the entire white race, and these generalizations have caused injuries to some whites who perhaps did not deserve to be hurt. Because of the spiritual enlightenment which I was blessed to receive as the result of my recent pilgrimage to the Holy City of Mecca, I no longer subscribe to sweeping indictments of any one race” (archived text).

At the OAU summit in Cairo in August 1964 Malcolm attended as an observer for the OAAU and said so in the opening sentence of his address: “The Organization of Afro-American Unity has sent me to attend this historic African Summit Conference as an observer to represent the interests of 22 million African-Americans whose human rights are being violated daily by the racism of American imperialists” (Malcolm X, Speech to the African Summit Conference, 21 August 1964). The address put the civil-rights and human-rights distinction at the centre: “Our problem is your problem. It is not a Negro problem, nor an American problem. This is a world problem, a problem for humanity. It is not a problem of civil rights, it is a problem of human rights” (Ibid.). It ended with a warning that fused anti-colonialism and economic criticism: “Don’t let American racism be ‘legalized’ by American dollarism. America is worse than South Africa, because not only is America racist, but she is also deceitful and hypocritical” (Ibid.). In the second summit speech he identified the material stakes: “The entire continent of Africa and her awakening people is the richest prize yet in the key struggle for the ‘balance of power’ currently waged between East and West” (Second African Summit Conference, 21 August 1964).

Internationalising the struggle: the United Nations question#

The shift from civil rights to human rights was a central strategic change of Malcolm’s final year. The earliest clear statement came in the March 1964 A. B. Spellman interview: “if they expanded the civil rights movement to a human rights movement then they would be eligible to take the case of the Negro to the United Nations the same as the case of the Angolans is in the UN and the case of the South Africans is in the UN,” and, when asked about collaborators, “We will work with anybody who is sincerely interested in eliminating injustices that Negroes suffer at the hands of Uncle Sam” (A. B. Spellman Interviews Malcolm X, 19 March 1964).

In “The Ballot or the Bullet” of 3 April 1964 he gave the central argument:

“When you expand the civil-rights struggle to the level of human rights, you can then take the case of the black man in this country before the nations in the UN. You can take it before the General Assembly. You can take Uncle Sam before a world court. But the only level you can do it on is the level of human rights. Civil rights keeps you under his restrictions, under his jurisdiction. Civil rights keeps you in his pocket. Civil rights means you’re asking Uncle Sam to treat you right. Human rights are something you were born with. Human rights are your God-given rights. Human rights are the rights that are recognized by all nations of this earth.”

Source: Malcolm X, “The Ballot or the Bullet,” 3 April 1964

After his African journey, Malcolm said African governments had promised to support a complaint to the United Nations. In the Milton Henry interview of 12 April 1964 he reported: “several of them promised officially that, come the next session of the UN, any effort on our part to bring our problem before the UN… will get support and help from them. They will assist us in showing us how to bring it up legally. So I am very, very happy over the whole result of my trip here” (Milton Henry Interviews Malcolm X, 12 April 1964). The African Summit address translated this into a concrete request: “we beseech the heads of the independent African states to recommend an immediate investigation into our problem by the United Nations Commission on Human Rights” (African Summit address). The OAAU programme codified it institutionally. Its “Worldwide Concerns” section declares that “The time is past due for us to internationalize the problems of Afro-Americans,” and its self-defence section invokes “the guarantees of the United Nations Charter of Human Rights” alongside the United States Constitution (Program of the OAAU).

The theoretical content of the shift is a claim about jurisdiction. Civil rights can be enforced only in the courts of the government that grants them. Human rights, on Malcolm’s argument, belong to a person by birth and are recognised by many states. That is why he thought the case had to be moved from the domestic to the international level. The practical consequence was coalition with the newly independent states. In the Spellman interview he named the constituency in global terms: “we have friends outside the UN — 700,000,000 Chinese who are ready to die for human rights” (Spellman interview). The point was not adherence to Chinese politics. It was the assertion that the American Black struggle had allies among the world’s majority, an extension of the “dark majority” argument he developed in the January 1965 lectures.

Black nationalism as self-determination#

A common misreading of Malcolm’s nationalism is that it was a doctrine of hatred toward white people. His own texts rule out that reading again and again. In “The Ballot or the Bullet”: “Now in speaking like this, it doesn’t mean that we’re anti-white, but it does mean we’re anti-exploitation, we’re anti-degradation, we’re anti-oppression. And if the white man doesn’t want us to be anti-him, let him stop oppressing and exploiting and degrading us. Whether we are Christians or Muslims or nationalists or agnostics or atheists, we must first learn to forget our differences” (Ballot or the Bullet, 3 April 1964). He added the political identification five days later: “I’m still a Muslim but I’m also a nationalist, meaning that my political philosophy is black nationalism, my economic philosophy is black nationalism, my social philosophy is black nationalism” (“The Black Revolution,” 8 April 1964).

His definition was institutional rather than biological: “the political philosophy of black nationalism is that which is designed to encourage our people, black people, to gain complete control over the politics and politicians of our own community. Our economic philosophy is that we should gain control over the economy of our community, the businesses and other things that create employment so that we can provide jobs for our own people instead of having to picket and boycott and beg someone else for a job” (Ibid.). The 12 April 1964 recording gives the same formulation: “The economic philosophy of Black Nationalism only means that we should own and operate and control the economy of our community” (MIA Malcolm X Archive). The OAAU charter gives the organisational translation: “We assert that we Afro-Americans have the right to direct and control our lives, our history, and our future rather than to have our destinies determined by American racists” (Program of the OAAU).

It also explains why the organisation had to be exclusively Black: “The exclusive ethnic quality of our unity is necessary for self-preservation. We say this because our experiences backed up by history show that African culture and Afro-American culture not be accurately recognized and reported and cannot be respectably expressed nor be secure in its survival if we remain the divided, and therefore the helpless, victims of an oppressive society” (Ibid.). On “integration” the programme is explicit, and the ground of the objection is economic: “Careful evaluation of recent experiences shows that ‘integration’ actually describes the proccess [sic] by which a white society is (remains) set in a position to use, whenever it chooses to use and however it chooses to use, the best talents of nonwhite people. This power-web continues to build a society wherein the best contributions of Afro-Americans, in fact of all nonwhite people, would continue to be absorbed without note or exploited to benefit a fortunate few while the masses of both white and nonwhite people would remain unequal and unbenefited” (Ibid.). This was not separatism as an end. It was a critique of a form of incorporation that left “the masses of both white and nonwhite people… unequal and unbenefited.”

Revolution, land, and the “house Negro / field Negro” analysis#

“Message to the Grassroots” of 10 November 1963 supplied Malcolm with a durable vocabulary for distinguishing reform from revolution. His claim was that the American civil-rights style of protest was not a revolution:

“There’s no such thing as a nonviolent revolution. The only kind of revolution that’s nonviolent is the Negro revolution. The only revolution based on loving your enemy is the Negro revolution… That’s no revolution. Revolution is based on land. Land is the basis of all independence. Land is the basis of freedom, justice, and equality.”

Source: Malcolm X, “Message to the Grassroots,” 10 November 1963

The speech points to the Chinese, Kenyan, Algerian and Cuban cases as instances of “a real revolution” and concludes: “The black revolution is sweeping Asia, sweeping Africa, is rearing its head in Latin America. The Cuban Revolution — that’s a revolution. They overturned the system” (Ibid.). The “house Negro” and “field Negro” passage is a class analysis of collaboration, not a racial one. Malcolm explicitly identified with the second: “You’ve got field Negroes in America today. I’m a field Negro. The masses are the field Negroes. When they see this man’s house on fire, you don’t hear these little Negroes talking about ‘our government is in trouble.’ They say, ‘The government is in trouble’” (Ibid.). The target was an intermediary leadership layer: “the same old slavemaster today has Negroes who are nothing but modern Uncle Toms, 20th century Uncle Toms, to keep you and me in check, keep us under control, keep us passive and peaceful and nonviolent” (Ibid.). The April 1964 address sharpened this into a general theory: “Revolution is always based on land. Revolution is never based on begging somebody for an integrated cup of coffee. Revolutions are never fought by turning the other cheek. Revolutions are never based upon love-your-enemy and pray-for-those-who-despitefully-use-you. And revolutions are never waged singing ‘We Shall Overcome.’ Revolutions are based on bloodshed. Revolutions are never compromising. Revolutions are never based upon negotiations. Revolutions are never based upon any kind of tokenism whatsoever” (“The Black Revolution,” 8 April 1964). The Marxists Internet Archive uses this passage as the epigraph to its Malcolm archive (MIA archive index).

Anti-colonialism and Pan-Africanism#

Malcolm’s anti-colonialism was stated as a description of power on a world scale rather than as a moral appeal. In the January 1965 Audubon lecture he said: “So the era that we’re living in is an era in which we see the people in the East on the rise and the people in the West on the decline. That is, the dark world is rising and the white world or the Western world is having its power curtailed.” The base of power, he continued, “has been in London and Paris and Brussels and Washington, D.C…. Now the bases of power are changing. You have a base of power in Accra, in Ghana, in Africa. Another base of power in Zanzibar. Another base of power in Cairo. Another base of power in Algiers. Another base of power in Tokyo. Another base of power in Peking” (“Afro-American History,” 24 January 1965). He immediately added the qualification that this was a structural, not a racial, claim: “And I’ve got to point out right here that what I’m saying is not racist; I’m not speaking racism, I’m not condemning all white people. I’m just saying that in the past the white world was in power, and it was. This is history, this is fact. They called it European history, or colonialism” (Ibid.). The formula he reached was: “The rise of the dark world is producing the fall of the white world” (Ibid.).

The same lecture gives a concrete statement of the connection between the local and the global: “what happens in South Vietnam can affect him if he’s living on St. Nicholas Avenue, or what’s happening in the Congo affected his situation on 8th Avenue or 7th Avenue or Lenox Avenue” (Ibid.). It reframes the minority question: “we look upon ourselves not as a dark minority on the white American stage, but rather we look upon ourselves as a part of the dark majority who now prevail on the world stage… You approach demanding rather than using the begging approach” (Ibid.). Pan-Africanism was the organisational form of this analysis. The Lagos and Accra letters state the programme: “it is time for all African-Americans to become an integral part of the world’s Pan-Africanists… we must ‘return’ to Africa philosophically and culturally and develop a working unity in the framework of Pan-Africanism” (Letters from Lagos and Accra). At the founding rally Malcolm put the institutional case: “Africa will not go forward any faster than we will and we will not go forward any faster than Africa will. We have one destiny and we’ve had one past” (OAAU Founding Rally).

At the African Summit he described the method he admired, “the spirit of Bandung”: “Some were Buddhists, some were Hindus, some were Christians, some were Muslim, some didn’t have any religion at all. Some were socialists, some were capitalists, some were communists, and some didn’t have any economy at all. But with all of the differences that existed, they agreed on one thing, the spirit of Bandung was, from there on in, to de-emphasize the areas of difference and emphasize the areas that we had in common” (“Not Just an American Problem, But a World Problem,” 16 February 1965). Bandung referred to the 1955 conference of Asian and African states that had adopted this principle of setting aside differences in the interest of anti-colonial unity.

Self-defence: “the ballot or the bullet” and “by any means necessary”#

Self-defence ran through Malcolm’s politics from 1960 to 1965, and its limits matter as much as its assertion. In “Message to the Grassroots” the religious and the political are joined in a single formulation: “Be peaceful, be courteous, obey the law, respect everyone; but if someone puts his hand on you, send him to the cemetery. That’s a good religion. In fact, that’s that old-time religion. That’s the one that Ma and Pa used to talk about: an eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth, and a head for a head, and a life for a life” (Message to the Grassroots). He also stated the general principle of reciprocal violence: “If violence is wrong in America, violence is wrong abroad. If it’s wrong to be violent defending black women and black children and black babies and black men, then it’s wrong for America to draft us and make us violent abroad in defense of her. And if it is right for America to draft us, and teach us how to be violent in defense of her, then it is right for you and me to do whatever is necessary to defend our own people right here in this country” (collected speech text).

In “The Ballot or the Bullet” the same right is anchored in constitutional language: “The only thing that I’ve ever said is that in areas where the government has proven itself either unwilling or unable to defend the lives and the property of Negroes, it’s time for Negroes to defend themselves. Article number two of the constitutional amendments provides you and me the right to own a rifle or a shotgun. It is constitutionally legal to own a shotgun or a rifle. This doesn’t mean you’re going to get a rifle and form battalions and go out looking for white folks” (Ballot or the Bullet). The condition is explicit: the right arises where the state is “unwilling or unable” to protect life and property. The electoral alternative is stated in the same speech: “It’s either the ballot or the bullet. If you’re afraid to use an expression like that, you should get on out of the country” (Ibid.).

The Pierre Berton interview gives a pointed exchange on the same limit. Asked “suppose that a church is bombed. Will you bomb back?” Malcolm answered: “I believe that any area of the United States, where the federal government has shown either its unwillingness or inability to protect the lives and the property of the black American, then it is time for the black Americans to band together and do whatever is necessary to see that we get the type of protection we need” (Berton Interview). Pressed on what he meant by “whatever is necessary,” he replied:

“I mean just that. Whatever is necessary. This does not mean that we should go out and initiate acts of aggression indiscriminately in the white community. But it does mean that, if we are going to be respected as human beings, we should reserve the right to defend ourselves by whatever means necessary. This is recognized and accepted in any civilized society.”

Source: Pierre Berton Interview, 19 January 1965

The OAAU founding rally made the phrase an organisational motto: “We want freedom by any means necessary. We want justice by any means necessary. We want equality by any means necessary” (OAAU Founding Rally). Its charter’s self-defence section declares: “Since self-preservation is the first law of nature, we assert the Afro-American’s right to self-defense” and “in those areas where the government is either unable or unwilling to protect the lives and property of our people, that our people are within our rights to protect themselves by whatever means necessary” (Ibid.). Two telegrams show how the principle was applied politically. To Martin Luther King during the St. Augustine crisis of 30 June 1964: “If the Federal Government will not send troops to your aid, just say the word and we will immediately dispatch some our brothers there to organize self defense units among our people and the Ku Klux Klan will then receive a taste of its own medicine. The day of turning the other cheek to those brute beasts is over” (Telegram to Martin Luther King). To George Lincoln Rockwell in 1965: “you and your Ku Klux Klan friends will be met with maximum physical retaliation from those of us who are not hand-cuffed by the disarming philosophy of nonviolence, and who believe in asserting our right of self-defense — by any means necessary” (Telegram to Rockwell).

The anti-capitalist critique and “dollarism”#

Malcolm’s critique of capitalism developed from a moral indictment into an economic analysis. A concentrated statement appears in the Audubon Ballroom address of 20 December 1964:

“Among Asian countries, whether they are communist, socialist, you don’t find any capitalist countries over there too much nowadays. Almost every one of the countries that has gotten independence has devised some kind of socialistic system, and this is no accident… before you start trying to be incorporated, or integrated, or disintegrated, into this capitalistic system, should look over there and find out what are the people who have gotten their freedom adopting to provide themselves with better housing and better education and better food and better clothing. None of them are adopting the capitalistic system because they realize they can’t. You can’t operate a capitalistic system unless you are vulturistic; you have to have someone else’s blood to suck to be a capitalist. You show me a capitalist, I’ll show you a bloodsucker. He cannot be anything but a bloodsucker if he’s going to be a capitalist. He’s got to get it from somewhere other than himself.”

Source: Malcolm X at the Audubon Ballroom, 20 December 1964

The same speech contains a warning against easy conclusions: “Nasser took everything Russia could give him, and then put all the communists in jail. Not that I mean the communists should necessarily have been put in jail. For the communist is a man, a capitalist is a man, and a socialist is a man. Well, if all of them are men, why should they be put in jail, unless one of them is committing a crime? And if being a communist or being a capitalist or being a socialist is a crime, first you have to study which of those systems is the most criminal. And then you’ll be slow to say which one should be in jail” (Ibid.). The point was a defence of independent judgment, including the right not to be jailed for one’s political beliefs.

“Dollarism” is Malcolm’s name for the economic form of what is now often called neo-colonialism, meaning indirect control of a formally independent country through loans, aid, investment and political pressure. “Zionist Logic” states: “The modern 20th century weapon of neo-imperialism is ‘dollarism.’ The Zionists have mastered the science of dollarism: the ability to come posing as a friend and benefactor, bearing gifts and all other forms of economic aid and offers of technical assistance” (Zionist Logic). The same article declares that the imperialists “dread Gamal Abdul Nasser’s call for African-Arab Unity under Socialism” (Ibid.). The African Summit address generalises the term beyond the Middle East: “We pray that our African brothers have not freed themselves of European colonialism only to be overcome and held in check now by American dollarism. Don’t let American racism be ‘legalized’ by American dollarism” (African Summit address). Its closing warning is in the same register: “Don’t escape from European colonialism only to become even more enslaved by deceitful… American dollarism” (Ibid.).

The economic program of the OAAU is the domestic counterpart. Its “Economic security” section identifies the historical transition: “After the Emancipation Proclamation, when the system of slavery changed from chattel slavery to wage slavery, it was realized that the Afro-American constituted the largest homogeneous ethnic group with a common origin and common group experience in the United States and, if allowed to exercise economic or political freedom, would in a short period of time own this country” (Program of the OAAU). It proposes a “technician bank”: “In the same manner that blood banks have been established to furnish blood to those who need it at the time it is needed, we must establish a technician bank. We must do this so that the newly independent nations of Africa can turn to us who are their Afro-American brothers for the technicians they will need now and in the future” (Ibid.). The same document proposes “support rent strikes. Yes, not little, small rent strikes in one block We’ll make Harlem a rent strike” (OAAU Founding Rally).

The “international black revolutionary movement”#

In the 15 February 1965 speech Malcolm set out his late view of a single world struggle with two faces:

“You and I are living at a time when there’s a revolution going on. A worldwide revolution. It goes beyond Mississippi. It goes beyond Alabama. It goes beyond Harlem. There’s a worldwide revolution going on. And it’s in two phases. Number one, what is it revolting against? The power structure. The American power structure? No. The French power structure? No. The English power structure? No. Then what power structure? An international Western power structure. An international power structure consisting of American interests, French interests, English interests, Belgian interests, European interests. These countries that formerly colonized the dark man formed into a giant international combine. A structure, a house that has ruled the world up until now.”

Source: Malcolm X, “There’s A Worldwide Revolution Going On,” 15 February 1965

The second phase, in the same speech, is “the awakening of the dark world,” the colonial and post-colonial majority as the subject of revolution (Ibid.). The “Prospects for Freedom in 1965” address reviews this as a balance sheet of victories: “the oppressed people of South Vietnam, and in that entire Southeast Asia area, were successful in fighting off the agents of imperialism… Little rice farmers, peasants, with a rifle — up against all the highly-mechanized weapons of warfare — jets, napalm, battleships, everything else, and they can’t put those rice farmers back where they want them. Somebody’s waking up.” On the Congo: “the People’s Republic of the Congo, headquartered at Stanleyville, fought a war for freedom against Tshombe, who is an agent for Western imperialism — and by Western imperialism I mean that which is headquartered in the United States, in the State Department” (“Prospects for Freedom in 1965,” 7 January 1965).

The internationalism was Black-led but not racially exclusive in its theory of alliance. The OAAU charter states: “We, the Organization of Afro-American Unity pledge to join hands and hearts with all people of African origin in a grand alliance by forgetting all the differences that the power structure has created to keep us divided and enslaved” (Program of the OAAU). Its “National Concerns” section adds: “We Afro-Americans feel receptive toward all peoples of goodwill. We are not opposed to multiethnic associations in any walk of life” (Ibid.). At the founding rally Malcolm put the same idea polemically: “instead of you and me running around here seeking allies in our struggle for freedom in the Irish neighborhood or the Jewish neighborhood or the Italian neighborhood, we need to seek some allies among people who look something like we do” (OAAU Founding Rally).

Ordinary people appear in this period not only as audiences but as witnesses whose words entered the record. Alice Windom, a social worker from St. Louis living in Ghana, helped welcome Malcolm to Accra in May 1964 and wrote a six-page letter about the visit to friends in the United States. Historians Erik McDuffie and Komozi Woodard found that fifty-six sentences from that letter appear verbatim in the Autobiography, sometimes with only the third person changed to the first. Windom said she had not known her words were used and was not sure how the letter reached Haley (Alex White, Aeon, 2025). The episode is a small but concrete example of what the scholarly editing of the book has uncovered: parts of the published “I” are someone else’s report of what she saw.

Socialism and communists#

Malcolm X never joined the Communist Party, the Socialist Workers Party, or any other political party. He rejected the suggestion on more than one occasion. His anti-capitalism should not be inflated into party communism, nor should it be erased. The sequence of his statements matters, because his position changed between 1963 and 1965.

The 1963 disclaimer. At UC Berkeley on 11 October 1963 a questioner told him, “you know that Islam believes in socialism rather than capitalism.” Malcolm’s recorded answer was: “I have no knowledge of socialism. That’s something else” (UC Berkeley, 11 October 1963). This was an early and explicit disavowal.

The 1964 coalition position. Asked by A. B. Spellman in March 1964 whether he intended to collaborate with labour unions or socialist groups, Malcolm answered: “We will work with anybody who is sincerely interested in eliminating injustices that Negroes suffer at the hands of Uncle Sam” (Spellman interview). This is a united-front statement, meaning a temporary alliance between groups that disagree on fundamentals, not a declaration of ideological identity. The same logic underlies letters to Martin Luther King and Whitney Young: “If capitalistic Kennedy and communistic Khrushschev [sic] can find something in common on which to form a United Front despite their tremendous ideological differences, it is a disgrace for Negro leaders not to be able to submerge our ‘minor’ differences in order to seek a common solution to a common problem posed by a Common Enemy” (Letter to Martin Luther King; Letter to Whitney Young).

The Nasser position. In December 1964 Malcolm described African non-alignment, the policy of accepting aid from both cold-war blocs without joining either, in terms that refuse both sides: “They want to take out of any other philosophy that which they can adopt to their own needs and to their own development. But to be identified with either the Communist bloc or the capitalist bloc, I don’t think you’ll find any African country or African leader who will buy that — he’s for Africa” (Bernice Bass interview, 27 December 1964). He added, approvingly, that “President Nasser took all of the aid that was forthcoming from Russia to build the Aswan Dam and turned around and put the Communists in jail in his country. Which shows he doesn’t take aid for those countries to tell him what he can do” (Ibid.).

The December 1964 anti-capitalist turn. The Audubon address of 20 December 1964 contains his positive statements about socialistic systems: “Almost every one of the countries that has gotten independence has devised some kind of socialistic system, and this is no accident,” while simultaneously refusing anti-communist orthodoxy: “For the communist is a man, a capitalist is a man, and a socialist is a man” (Audubon, 20 December 1964). This is a materialist observation about decolonisation, meaning an explanation that looks to economic conditions rather than to the virtue or vice of a system, not an announcement of party affiliation.

The February 1965 denial. A detailed exchange appears in the Stan Bernard interview of 18 February 1965. An interlocutor alleged that Malcolm had “been breaking bread with the communists downtown” and identified the Socialist Workers Party. Malcolm replied: “You are absolutely out of your mind, I have never broken bread with…” When it was put to him that the SWP had reprinted his speeches, he said: “Well, that’s not breaking bread. I speak anywhere.” The exchange culminates: “If speaking on the socialist platform makes me a socialist, then when I speak in a Methodist church… I was in Selma, Alabama, last week, speaking in Martin Luther King’s church. Does that make me a follower of Martin Luther King? No, your line of reasoning, sir, doesn’t fit me” (Stan Bernard interview, 18 February 1965). In the same interview he defended the existence of socialist states against American denial: “China is a nation of 700 million people. Physically they exist; physically they exist. I don’t go along with the American reaction of pretending that 700 million Chinese don’t exist” (Ibid.).

The Bandung position. In February 1965 he enumerated the diversity of the Bandung participants, “Some were socialists, some were capitalists, some were communists,” and identified the common ground as anti-colonialism: “the spirit of Bandung was, from there on in, to de-emphasize the areas of difference and emphasize the areas that we had in common” (Not Just an American Problem, But a World Problem).

Taking these statements together, Malcolm was a Black nationalist and anti-imperialist who developed an explicit anti-capitalist critique from 1964 onward, praised the socialistic systems adopted by newly independent countries, defended the right of communist and socialist countries to exist, and advocated tactical co-operation with “anybody” genuinely opposed to injustice. At the same time he declined to be labelled a socialist or a communist, and he never claimed membership in any socialist or communist party.

Islam and politics#

Malcolm separated religious and political organisation. In the Al-Muslimoon interview he says the Muslim Mosque is for religious teaching and the OAAU is “non-religious” and “designed to unite all Black Americans regardless of their religious affiliation” (Al-Muslimoon interview). In the Berton interview he makes the same point historically: “the Muslim world rejected the Black Muslim movement as a bona fide Islamic group, so it found itself maneuvered into a religious vacuum — or a sort of religious hybrid” (Berton interview). His own post-break position, as he states it there, was orthodox Islam plus a secular political organisation. The “Declaration of Faith” of 8 April 1964 is the religious text; the OAAU charter is the political text. Neither is subordinate to the other in his own presentation, and neither is a substitute for the other.

The final months and the assassination#

The January 1965 series at the Audubon Ballroom was to build toward a new OAAU programme. A movement chronology records the sequence: on 24 January Malcolm spoke on Afro-American history; on 31 January on present conditions; the third meeting, first set for 7 February and then postponed to 15 February because of travel to England and France, was preceded by the firebombing of his home early on 14 February (Breitman, Introduction, March 1967). Malcolm’s own speech of 15 February confirms the disruption: “tonight we were going to unfold a program which we felt would be beneficial to the struggle of our people in this country. But because of events which are beyond our control we feel that it is best to postpone unfolding the program that we had in mind until a later date. Sunday morning about three oclock, somebody threw some bombs inside my house” (Malcolm X, “There’s A Worldwide Revolution Going On,” 15 February 1965). His last written interview, the Al-Muslimoon answers, includes his own note of the bombing: “I retired at 12:30 and exploding bombs that were thrown into my home by would be murderers rocked me and my wife and four baby daughters from sleep at 2:30 a.m. Only Allah saved us from death” (Al-Muslimoon Interview).

The Epilogue to the Autobiography, written by Alex Haley from his contemporaneous investigation, records Betty Shabazz being asked “if she felt it would be too much trouble for her to get all of the four children dressed and bring them to the two o’clock meeting that afternoon at the Audubon Ballroom in Harlem,” and Malcolm’s report of a threatening telephone call at eight o’clock, “‘Wake up, brother’ and hung up” (Autobiography, Alex Haley, Epilogue). Then: “Malcolm X said good-bye to Sister Betty. And four hours later, Malcolm X left his room and took an elevator down to the lobby, where he checked out. He got his car and in the clear, warm midday of Sunday, February 21, he drove uptown to the Audubon Ballroom” (Ibid.). The same Epilogue describes the ballroom as “between Broadway and St. Nicholas Avenue, on the south side of West 166th Street,” and the 400 chairs set up for the OAAU meeting (Ibid.). The movement press recorded the event in the same terms: Malcolm “was killed” as “he started to speak at one of those rallies” in the Audubon Ballroom (Breitman, Introduction). Marable’s biography used estate papers, FBI and police files and new interviews to reopen questions about the handling of the case, and argued that the evidence remained incomplete enough to justify a fresh investigation (AHA memorial notice for Manning Marable, 2011).

Coda#

The Autobiography made Malcolm X legible to millions of readers, but the version of him it presents was assembled under particular conditions. Malcolm worked on the early chapters with Haley in 1963 and approved what went into them. After the break with Elijah Muhammad and the travels of 1964, Haley was often short of money, behind schedule and short of material. He drew on Malcolm’s letters and travel diary, reused his own published writing, and in the Ghana chapters copied sentences from Alice Windom’s private letter. He left out the OAAU and most of Malcolm’s religious organising after Mecca, and three chapters Malcolm wrote were removed. Doubleday dropped the book after the assassination and Grove Press published it in 1965. It sold about two million copies between 1965 and 1969 (Alex White, Aeon, 2025). Marable’s rewriting of the book’s political meaning generated its own scholarly dispute, with some historians defending the Autobiography’s account of Malcolm’s development against what they see as excessive scepticism (AAIHS, 2016; Ball and Burroughs, A Lie of Reinvention, 2012). The speeches and interviews remain essential evidence for what Malcolm argued at a given moment. The Autobiography remains essential evidence for how a remarkable life was turned into a book.
