Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels

Lives, works and theory: historical materialism, surplus value, the state, and the two phases of communist society.

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels wrote at a time when industrial capitalism was transforming Europe and political revolution seemed, for a period, possible. Marx was born in 1818 and died in London on 14 March 1883. Engels was born in 1820 and died in 1895. Between them they produced a body of work that later socialists, communists and historians have returned to for arguments about class, capital, the state and the possibility of a different social order. Their central claim was that ideas, law and political institutions have to be explained through the ways people produce the things they need. Historical materialism is the name later given to that approach: the view that the organization of production shapes social institutions and prevailing ideas, while people in turn act on the conditions they inherit. Surplus value is their term for the extra value that wage workers create beyond what they are paid. Alienation describes the condition in which the product of a person’s work confronts them as something foreign that controls them. By the state they meant the institutions, such as government, courts, police and army, that exercise organized power over a territory. By crisis they meant a recurring breakdown in which goods and productive capacity are destroyed or left idle. By the transition to communism they meant a political period in which the working class was expected to take power and begin to reshape property and production.

These arguments survive in published books, articles, letters and editorial prefaces. Every one of those documents has a date, an author and a purpose, and each must be read as evidence rather than as a verdict on itself. Engels’s account of how he and Marx divided their work, written more than three decades after their first collaboration and after Marx’s death, is a participant’s retrospective statement, not an independent audit. Editors at the Marxists Internet Archive have supplied headnotes and indexes that are useful guides but are not primary texts. Modern scholars do not all agree about how the two men related to one another, or about how much of the theory belongs to each. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that the question of their intellectual relationship remains “sharply contested,” and that the standard critical editions of their work are always issued as the collected works of Marx and Engels together rather than separately. Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Friedrich Engels”.

Marx’s road to political economy#

Marx studied law but, as he later recalled, pursued it “as a subject subordinated to philosophy and history” (Marx, Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, 1859). The practical turn came in 1842 and 1843, when he edited the Rheinische Zeitung. In the 1859 preface he described what pushed him toward economic questions: “the deliberations of the Rhenish Landtag on forest thefts and the division of landed property,” the controversy over “the condition of the Moselle peasantry,” and “the debates on free trade and protective tariffs caused me in the first instance to turn my attention to economic questions.” In the same years he met “an echo of French socialism and communism, slightly tinged by philosophy.” He said he objected to it because he lacked knowledge, and admitted that lack openly (Marx, 1859 Preface). These were concrete grievances, not abstract puzzles: poor people gathering wood from landed estates, peasants in the Moselle valley facing poverty, and arguments over whether free trade would help or harm them.

His first sustained reply was a critique of Hegel’s philosophy of law. Hegel had argued that the state and legal institutions are the rational outcome of a developing spirit. Marx inverted that claim, arguing that legal and political forms arise from actual social conditions. The introduction to the critique appeared in the Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher in Paris in 1844. In the 1859 preface he summarized the result:

“neither legal relations nor political forms could be comprehended whether by themselves or on the basis of a so-called general development of the human mind, but that on the contrary they originate in the material conditions of life, the totality of which Hegel, following the example of English and French thinkers of the eighteenth century, embraces within the term ‘civil society’; that the anatomy of this civil society, however, has to be sought in political economy.”

(Source: Marx, Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, 1859.)

The philosopher Ludwig Feuerbach mattered to this shift. Feuerbach argued that religion is a human creation rather than a divine revelation, and Marx took from him the idea that ideas must be explained by the human conditions that produce them. In the preface to the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 Marx said that “positive criticism as a whole” owed its true foundation to Feuerbach’s discoveries, and that “it is only with Feuerbach that positive, humanistic and naturalistic criticism begins” (Marx, Preface to the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844). The Manuscripts themselves were written between April and August 1844 and were not published until 1932, long after Marx’s death, so they could not have shaped the movements that later claimed his inheritance (MIA editorial headnote, Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844).

In 1845 Marx wrote the short Theses on Feuerbach. Its eleventh thesis, “The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it,” was published by Engels in 1888 as an appendix to his Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy (MIA editorial headnote, Theses on Feuerbach). That publication history matters. A line widely treated as the sum of Marx’s project reached readers through Engels, in a book issued forty-three years after the thesis was written.

Marx moved to Brussels in 1845, where Engels joined him. The two decided “to set forth together our conception as opposed to the ideological one of German philosophy, in fact to settle accounts with our former philosophical conscience,” producing The German Ideology (Marx, 1859 Preface). The manuscript was left with a publisher in Westphalia and, as Marx drily put it, was abandoned “to the gnawing criticism of the mice” once its authors had achieved their purpose of “self-clarification” (Marx, 1859 Preface). It therefore remained unpublished in both authors’ lifetimes. Engels’s letter to Joseph Bloch of 21 September 1890 contains an editorial interpolation stating that the work was not published in Marx’s or Engels’s lifetime; that sentence belongs to the editor, not to Engels (Engels to Bloch, 21 September 1890, MIA edition).

The years from 1847 to 1849 were years of organization and revolution: the Communist League, the Communist Manifesto of 1848, the Neue Rheinische Zeitung of 1848 and 1849, and the defeats of those revolutions. Engels’s graveside oration in 1883 summarized this period as “work in organisations in Paris, Brussels and London, and finally, crowning all, the formation of the great International Working Men’s Association” (Engels, “Speech at the Grave of Karl Marx,” 17 March 1883). That association, founded in 1864, is known as the First International. A draft of the speech published in La Justice on 20 March 1883 said Marx “was the acknowledged leader from 1864–72” (Engels, draft graveside speech, La Justice).

After 1849 Marx lived in London. He resumed economic study at the British Museum in 1850 and wrote journalism for the New York Tribune, a circumstance that, in his own words, “necessitated an excessive fragmentation of my studies” (Marx, 1859 Preface). Capital, Volume I, appeared in 1867. Marx died in 1883 and was buried at Highgate Cemetery on 17 March (Engels, burial speech).

Engels’s road: Manchester and the condition of the workers#

Engels came to the same subject by a different route. The son of a manufacturer with a firm in Manchester, he spent two years in England from 1842 and observed industrial production and working-class life at first hand. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes his Condition of the Working-Class in England as a book written after that stay, based on close observation of production techniques and living conditions, and still widely regarded as his masterpiece (Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Friedrich Engels”). It was written between September 1844 and March 1845 and published in Leipzig in 1845 (MIA editorial headnote, The Condition of the Working Class in England).

This is the closest the two men came to giving ordinary people a voice in their early work. Engels described the towns, the housing, the Irish immigrant labourers, the mines and the agricultural workers, drawing on what he saw and on official reports. He was not a worker himself, and he wrote as a young middle-class radical with a political purpose. His descriptions are evidence about conditions as an observer recorded them, not a transcript of workers’ own words, and they must be read with that limitation in mind. The book nevertheless supplied the empirical substance behind his later claim that class relations explain social institutions.

His first important essay on economics, “Outlines of a Critique of Political Economy” (Umrisse zu einer Kritik der Nationalökonomie), appeared in the Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher in 1844. Marx edited that journal and was impressed by the essay. In the preface to the 1844 Manuscripts Marx identified Engels’s essay and the essays of Hess as the only original German works of substance in political economy apart from the writings of Weitling. He added that he himself had indicated “the basic elements of this work” in a very general way in the same journal. Weitling was explicitly excluded from the “works of substance,” and the phrase about basic elements referred to what Marx had written in the journal, not to Engels’s essay (Marx, Preface to the 1844 Manuscripts). In the 1859 preface Marx said that Engels, “with whom I maintained a constant exchange of ideas by correspondence since the publication of his brilliant essay on the critique of economic categories,” had arrived “by another road,” namely that of The Condition of the Working Class in England, “at the same result as I” (Marx, 1859 Preface).

Engels fought in the Baden campaign during the revolutions of 1848 and 1849. After the defeat he returned to Manchester and entered the family firm, partly in order to support Marx and his family financially, while continuing his own writing and organizational work. He moved to London after 1870 (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Friedrich Engels”).

In a footnote to Ludwig Feuerbach in 1886 Engels described the division of credit between them:

“I cannot deny that both before and during my 40 years’ collaboration with Marx I had a certain independent share in laying the foundation of the theory, and more particularly in its elaboration. But the greater part of its leading basic principles, especially in the realm of economics and history, and, above all, their final trenchant formulation, belong to Marx. What I contributed — at any rate with the exception of my work in a few special fields, Marx could very well have done without me. What Marx accomplished I would not have achieved. Marx stood higher, saw further, and took a wider and quicker view than all the rest of us. Marx was a genius; we others were at best talented. Without him the theory would not be by far what it is today. It therefore rightly bears his name.”

(Source: Engels, Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy, Part 4, note 1, 1886.)

The modesty is real, but so is its date. Engels wrote this three years after Marx’s death, at a moment when he was managing Marx’s literary estate and presenting himself as the guardian of his friend’s work. Georg Lukács later argued that Engels distorted Marx by extending dialectics to nature, and much twentieth-century scholarship followed that line, often in a Cold War setting that separated the two men in order to rehabilitate Marx for western readers. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy stresses that this divergence thesis was never a consensus, that a number of scholars defend Engels, and that the ongoing publication of the original manuscripts in the Marx-Engels Gesamtausgabe has shifted attention toward the question of how well Engels edited volumes two and three of Capital. On that question too, scholars remain divided (Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Friedrich Engels”; Darren Roso, “Engels after Marx: a (critical) defence,” Historical Materialism, 2024).

How they worked together#

The earliest joint products were genuinely shared. The 1859 preface names “the Manifesto of the Communist Party, jointly written by Engels and myself” (Marx, 1859 Preface). The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy adds a detail that the collaborators themselves did not spell out: the Manifesto was drafted first by Engels and then revised by Marx (Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Friedrich Engels”). The Holy Family of 1845 was their first book together, with individually signed chapters, most of it written by Marx. The German Ideology was the abandoned follow-up in which they first sketched their materialist account of history. In Anti-Dühring, written by Engels between 1876 and 1878, the last chapter of Part II, “The Historical,” was written by Marx (MIA editorial headnote, Anti-Dühring). Engels described the project to Marx as “an encyclopaedic survey of our conception of the philosophical, natural-science and historical problems” (MIA editorial headnote, Anti-Dühring).

From the late 1850s a division of labour became clearer. Marx concentrated on political economy and the historical sciences; Engels wrote on natural science and dialectics, military and international questions, and the state and the family, and took on much of the public defence of their views. Engels later said that circumstances “made it necessary for me to present our views for the most part in a polemical form” (Engels, preface to the second edition of The Housing Question, 1887, quoted in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). After Marx’s death Engels edited the unfinished economic manuscripts. “the ever-increasing bulk of work had to be done by myself alone,” he wrote in 1894, and he described the burden of translations, prefaces and supplements that followed (Engels, Preface to Capital, Volume III, 4 October 1894).

What they wrote, and when it appeared#

The publication dates are part of the history, because several of the most influential texts appeared decades after they were written. The Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts were written in 1844 and published in 1932. The Theses on Feuerbach were written in 1845 and published in 1888. The German Ideology was written in 1845 and 1846 and appeared posthumously. The Poverty of Philosophy, a critique of Proudhon published in 1847, set out Marx’s early account of value, division of labour, machinery, competition and monopoly (MIA editorial headnote, The Poverty of Philosophy). The Manifesto of the Communist Party followed in 1848, and The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte in 1852, applying the materialist method to the French revolution and its aftermath. The Grundrisse notebooks of 1857 and 1858, a rough draft for Capital, were not published until 1939 to 1941 and 1953 (MIA editorial headnote and analytical contents, Grundrisse). The 1859 Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy gave the canonical statement of base and superstructure. Capital, Volume I, appeared in 1867; The Civil War in France in 1871; the Critique of the Gotha Programme was written in 1875 but published in abridged form in Die Neue Zeit in 1890 and 1891, with a foreword by Engels (MIA editorial headnote, Critique of the Gotha Programme).

Engels’s Anti-Dühring was serialized in Vorwärts from 3 January 1877 to 7 July 1878 and published as a book in Leipzig in 1878. A second edition appeared in Zurich in 1886 and a third revised edition in Stuttgart in 1894; the work was banned in Germany under the Anti-Socialist Law of 1878 (MIA editorial headnote, Anti-Dühring). Socialism: Utopian and Scientific of 1880 was a popular extract from it. Dialectics of Nature was written mainly in the 1870s and 1880s and first published in Russian and German in the Soviet Union in 1925; two essays had appeared earlier, in 1896 and 1898 (MIA editorial index, Dialectics of Nature). The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State was written in March and May 1884 and published in October 1884 in Hottingen-Zurich. A second edition was prepared in 1890 and a fourth in 1892; the Russian translation of 1894 was “the first of Engels’s works published legally in Russia” (MIA editorial introduction, The Origin of the Family). Engels edited Capital, Volume II, in 1885, with a second edition in 1893 (MIA editorial contents, Capital, Volume II), and Volume III in 1894. Ludwig Feuerbach appeared in 1886. Engels died in 1895, leaving further manuscripts unpublished.

How they explained history and class#

The 1859 preface contains the passage most often quoted as the statement of historical materialism. In it, “relations of production” means the social arrangements by which people produce and distribute goods, and “forces of production” means the workers, tools, techniques and knowledge available at a given time. The “base” is the sum of those production relations; the “superstructure” is the law, politics, religion and culture that rise upon it.

“In the social production of their existence, men inevitably enter into definite relations, which are independent of their will, namely relations of production appropriate to a given stage in the development of their material forces of production. The totality of these relations of production constitutes the economic structure of society, the real foundation, on which arises a legal and political superstructure and to which correspond definite forms of social consciousness. The mode of production of material life conditions the general process of social, political and intellectual life. It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness. At a certain stage of development, the material productive forces of society come into conflict with the existing relations of production… From forms of development of the productive forces these relations turn into their fetters. Then begins an era of social revolution.”

(Source: Marx, Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, 1859.)

Marx added two cautions that are often dropped in summaries. He distinguished “the material transformation of the economic conditions of production, which can be determined with the precision of natural science, and the legal, political, religious, artistic or philosophic — in short, ideological forms in which men become conscious of this conflict and fight it out.” He also wrote that “no social order is ever destroyed before all the productive forces for which it is sufficient have been developed, and new superior relations of production never replace older ones before the material conditions for their existence have matured within the framework of the old society” (1859 Preface). He listed “the Asiatic, ancient, feudal and modern bourgeois modes of production” as “epochs marking progress in the economic development of society,” and called the bourgeois mode “the last antagonistic form of the social process of production,” with which “the prehistory of human society accordingly closes” (1859 Preface).

The German Ideology gives the polemical version. Its “first premises” are “not arbitrary ones, not dogmas, but real premises… the real individuals, their activity and the material conditions under which they live” (The German Ideology, Part I, “First Premises of Materialist Method”). Production of the means of subsistence, new needs, and procreation and the family are “three aspects” of social activity that existed simultaneously “since the dawn of history” (German Ideology, “History: Fundamental Conditions”). The way people produce what they need determines what they become: “what they are, therefore, coincides with their production, both with what they produce and with how they produce” (German Ideology, “First Premises of Materialist Method”). On ideology it is equally direct:

“In direct contrast to German philosophy which descends from heaven to earth, here we ascend from earth to heaven… We set out from real, active men, and on the basis of their real life-process we demonstrate the development of the ideological reflexes and echoes of this life-process… Morality, religion, metaphysics, all the rest of ideology and their corresponding forms of consciousness, thus no longer retain the semblance of independence. They have no history, no development; but men, developing their material production and their material intercourse, alter, along with this their real existence, their thinking and the products of their thinking. Life is not determined by consciousness, but consciousness by life.”

(Source: The German Ideology, Part I, “The Essence of the Materialist Conception of History”.)

The work traces property forms from tribal through ancient and feudal to modern bourgeois society, and treats the state as arising from the conflict between particular and common interests. Struggles over the franchise and over forms of government are, in its words, “merely the illusory forms in which the real struggles of the different classes are fought out among one another” (German Ideology, footnote to “Private Property and Communism”). Communism is defined not as an ideal but as “the real movement which abolishes the present state of things,” requiring developed productive forces and a proletariat that exists on a world scale (German Ideology, “Development of the Productive Forces as a Material Premise of Communism”).

The Communist Manifesto compressed the argument into a sentence: “The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles,” and in the modern epoch “society as a whole is more and more splitting up into two great hostile camps… Bourgeoisie and Proletariat” (Manifesto, Chapter I). “The ruling ideas of each age have ever been the ideas of its ruling class,” it adds (Manifesto, Chapter II). Engels’s 1888 footnote defines the two classes: the bourgeoisie are “the class of modern capitalists, owners of the means of social production and employers of wage labour,” while the proletariat are “the class of modern wage labourers who, having no means of production of their own, are reduced to selling their labour power in order to live” (Manifesto, Chapter I, notes).

Late letters show that Engels rejected a crude economic reading of his own formula. Writing to Bloch in 1890, he said:

“According to the materialist conception of history, the ultimately determining element in history is the production and reproduction of real life. Other than this neither Marx nor I have ever asserted. Hence if somebody twists this into saying that the economic element is the only determining one, he transforms that proposition into a meaningless, abstract, senseless phrase. The economic situation is the basis, but the various elements of the superstructure… also exercise their influence upon the course of the historical struggles and in many cases preponderate in determining their form.”

(Source: Engels to J. Bloch, 21 September 1890.)

He added that “history is made in such a way that the final result always arises from conflicts between many individual wills,” producing “one resultant — the historical event”, and that “we make our history ourselves, but, in the first place, under very definite assumptions and conditions.” Economic conditions are “ultimately decisive” (Engels to Bloch). To Borgius in 1894 he defined “economic conditions” as “the methods by which human beings in a given society produce their means of subsistence and exchange the products among themselves,” including “the entire technique of production and transport,” and restated the position as “interaction on the basis of the economic necessity, which ultimately always asserts itself” (Engels to Borgius, 25 January 1894). For a worked example he pointed to the Eighteenth Brumaire, whose opening line, “Men make their own history, but they do not make it as they please… under circumstances existing already, given and transmitted from the past,” applies the same principle to particular people and events (The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, Chapter I).

Scholars have argued about exactly what “determining” means here. G. A. Cohen’s Karl Marx’s Theory of History: A Defence (1978) offered a rigorous reconstruction built on the idea that productive forces tend to develop and that production relations are explained by their function in developing them. Philosophers have disputed both the reconstruction and its claims to be a defence of Marx (Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Karl Marx”; Princeton University Press, Karl Marx’s Theory of History: A Defence). The texts themselves leave room for the argument: Marx’s preface speaks of relations becoming “fetters,” which suggests conflict between forces and relations, while Engels’s letters insist that law, politics and ideas also shape outcomes.

How capital produces profit#

The argument of Capital turns on a distinction between labour and labour-power. Labour-power is the capacity to work, which a worker sells for a wage; labour is the activity itself. The value of labour-power is the labour-time needed to produce and reproduce it, while its use-value, the value it has for the buyer, is greater than its own value. Marx’s shorthand in volume I is that “the labour-time necessary for the production, and consequently also the reproduction, of this special article” determines its value (Capital, Volume I, Chapter 6), whereas consuming it yields “a source not only of value, but of more value than it has itself” (Capital, Volume I, Chapter 7, section 2). Engels summarized what he saw as the resulting breakthrough in his 1885 preface to volume II:

“He found that value was nothing but congealed labour of this kind… He analysed the transformation of money into capital and demonstrated that this transformation is based on the purchase and sale of labour-power. By substituting labour-power, the value-producing property, for labour he solved with one stroke one of the difficulties which brought about the downfall of the Ricardian school. By establishing the distinction of capital into constant and variable he was enabled to trace the real course of the process of the formation of surplus-value in its minutest details. He analysed surplus-value further and found its two forms, absolute and relative surplus-value.”

(Source: Engels, Preface to Capital, Volume II, 1885.)

Marx illustrated the process with yarn. A capitalist advances 27 shillings and ends with yarn worth 30:

“27 shillings have been transformed into 30 shillings; a surplus-value of 3 shillings has been created. The trick has at last succeeded; money has been converted into capital… Equivalent has been exchanged for equivalent. This metamorphosis… takes place both within the sphere of circulation and also outside it: within the circulation, because conditioned by the purchase of the labour-power in the market; outside the circulation, because what is done within it is only a stepping-stone to the production of surplus-value, a process which is entirely confined to the sphere of production.”

(Source: Capital, Volume I, Chapter 7, section 2.)

Engels restated the conclusion for a wider readership: “It was shown that the appropriation of unpaid labor is the basis of the capitalist mode of production… that even if the capitalist buys the labor power of his laborer at its full value as a commodity on the market, he yet extracts more value from it than he paid for” (Engels, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part II).

The step from values to prices has been disputed by economists ever since. Marx acknowledged that in actual economic life prices differ systematically from labour values and attempted a solution in volume III. Whether that solution works is described by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy as “a matter of great controversy,” with critics arguing that the claim that only labour creates surplus value is ungrounded and defenders offering different mathematical treatments (Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Exploitation”; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Karl Marx”). The disagreement does not erase the descriptive claim about unpaid labour, but it does mean that the labour theory of value cannot simply be treated as a settled accounting rule.

Alienation, and the dispute about the early Marx#

The 1844 Manuscripts examine what Marx called estranged labour through four relations: the worker’s relation to the product as “an alien object exercising power over him,” to the act of production as “an alien activity not belonging to him,” the estrangement of “man’s species-being,” and “the estrangement of man from man” (Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts, “Estranged Labour”). Species-being was Marx’s term for the idea that human beings are by nature conscious, creative producers who shape their world together. Alienation reverses that: the product and the process of work become forces that dominate the producer.

“First, the fact that labor is external to the worker, i.e., it does not belong to his intrinsic nature; that in his work, therefore, he does not affirm himself but denies himself, does not feel content but unhappy, does not develop freely his physical and mental energy but mortifies his body and ruins his mind… His labor is therefore not voluntary, but coerced; it is forced labor. The worker therefore only feels himself outside his work, and in his work feels outside himself.”

(Source: Marx, “Estranged Labour,” Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844.)

The same text states the paradox: “The worker becomes all the poorer the more wealth he produces, the more his production increases in power and size… the devaluation of the world of men is in direct proportion to the increasing value of the world of things” (“Estranged Labour”). Marx derived private property from alienated labour, writing that “Private property is thus the product, the result, the necessary consequence, of alienated labour,” before noting that the relation becomes reciprocal (“Estranged Labour”). The Theses on Feuerbach then rejected Feuerbach’s abstract human essence: “The human essence is no abstraction inherent in each single individual. In its reality it is the ensemble of the social relations” (Theses on Feuerbach, VI).

It is tempting to conclude that the early critique of alienation and the mature critique of exploitation are simply two stages of one argument, with the first discovering the inversion of human powers and the second explaining its capitalist mechanism through surplus value. That is a coherent reading, but it is contested. Louis Althusser argued in “On the Young Marx” (1961) that the early writings belong to a humanist and Hegelian “problematic” and that a decisive epistemological break occurred around 1845, after which Marx founded a new science of history (Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Louis Althusser”; Althusser, “On the Young Marx”). Other scholars read the later economic writings as a development of the early critique rather than a rejection of it. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that “the problem, then, involves the continuity between the work of the young and the mature Marx,” and that neither a strong discontinuity nor an absolute continuity commands universal agreement (Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Louis Althusser”). Since the Manuscripts were unpublished until 1932, readers of the Manifesto and Capital in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries encountered the mature works without that supposed key.

Crisis and the falling rate of profit#

Crisis, in these texts, is not an accident but a recurring form of the contradiction between socialized production and private appropriation. The Manifesto describes how “the productive forces at the disposal of society no longer tend to further the development of the conditions of bourgeois property; on the contrary, they have become too powerful for these conditions,” so that “in these crises, a great part not only of the existing products, but also of the previously created productive forces, are periodically destroyed” (Manifesto, Chapter I). Engels gave the periodicity: “since 1825, when the first general crisis broke out, the whole industrial and commercial world are thrown out of joint about once every 10 years” (Engels, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part III). The bourgeoisie get over crises by “enforced destruction of a mass of productive forces” and “by the conquest of new markets,” thereby preparing “more extensive and more destructive crises” (Manifesto, Chapter I).

The law of the tendency of the rate of profit to fall is developed in Capital, Volume III, Part III. Engels’s preface records that this was “the most complicated subject in the entire volume” and that illness overtook Marx there, leaving “only the beginning of an elaboration — often just a disorderly mass of notes, comments and extracts” (Engels, Preface to Capital, Volume III). Marx’s point of departure is paradoxical:

“There must be some counteracting influences at work, which cross and annul the effect of the general law, and which give it merely the characteristic of a tendency, for which reason we have referred to the fall of the general rate of profit as a tendency to fall.”

(Source: Capital, Volume III, Chapter 14, “Counteracting Influences”.)

Marx, not Engels, enumerates the counteracting factors in chapter 14: increasing intensity of exploitation; depression of wages below the value of labour-power; cheapening of the elements of constant capital; relative over-population, meaning the industrial reserve army; foreign trade; and the increase of stock capital (Capital, Volume III, Chapter 14). he writes that “the law acts only as a tendency. And it is only under certain circumstances and only after long periods that its effects become strikingly pronounced” (Capital, Volume III, Chapter 14). He adds that “the tendency of the rate of profit to fall is bound up with a tendency of the rate of surplus-value to rise, hence with a tendency for the rate of labour exploitation to rise,” and that “the rate of profit does not fall because labour becomes less productive, but because it becomes more productive” (Capital, Volume III, Chapter 14). The Grundrisse contains the earlier formulation under headings such as “Increase of surplus value and decrease in rate of profit” (MIA analytical contents, Grundrisse).

How central this law should be within Marx’s crisis theory is itself disputed. Chris Harman defended its centrality on the ground that it connected the economic mechanisms of capitalism to a general account of successive modes of production, while other Marxists reject the law or treat it as one theory among several (Source: Chris Harman, “Marx’s theory of crisis and its critics,” 1981).

Primitive accumulation and the state#

“So-called primitive accumulation” is Marx’s name for the historical process that created the preconditions of capital by separating producers from the means of production. In chapter 32 of volume I he summarizes it:

“What does the primitive accumulation of capital, i.e., its historical genesis, resolve itself into? In so far as it is not immediate transformation of slaves and serfs into wage labourers, and therefore a mere change of form, it only means the expropriation of the immediate producers, i.e., the dissolution of private property based on the labour of its owner. Its annihilation, the transformation of the individualised and scattered means of production into socially concentrated ones, of the pigmy property of the many into the huge property of the few, the expropriation of the great mass of the people from the soil, from the means of subsistence, and from the means of labour, this fearful and painful expropriation of the mass of the people forms the prelude to the history of capital.”

(Source: Capital, Volume I, Chapter 32, “Historical Tendency of Capitalist Accumulation”.)

Once capitalist production stands on its own feet, expropriation takes a different form: “one capitalist always kills many,” through the centralization of capital (Capital, Volume I, Chapter 32). The conclusion follows:

“Centralisation of the means of production and socialisation of labour at last reach a point where they become incompatible with their capitalist integument. This integument is burst asunder. The knell of capitalist private property sounds. The expropriators are expropriated… It is the negation of negation.”

(Source: Capital, Volume I, Chapter 32.)

Marx distinguishes “the expropriation of the mass of the people by a few usurpers” from “the expropriation of a few usurpers by the mass of the people” (Capital, Volume I, Chapter 32). The Grundrisse treats the same theme under “Original Accumulation of Capital” and “Forms which precede capitalist production” (MIA analytical contents, Grundrisse).

The state, in this framework, is a historical product rather than an eternal form. Engels’s Origin of the Family gives the general theory:

“The state is therefore by no means a power imposed on society from without. Rather, it is a product of society at a particular stage of development; it is the admission that this society has involved itself in insoluble self-contradiction and is cleft into irreconcilable antagonisms which it is powerless to exorcise… The state, therefore, has not existed from all eternity. There have been societies which have managed without it. We are now rapidly approaching a stage in the development of production at which the existence of these classes has not only ceased to be a necessity, but becomes a positive hindrance to production. They will fall as inevitably as they once arose. The state inevitably falls with them.”

(Source: Engels, The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State, Chapter IX, “Barbarism and Civilization”.)

Engels distinguishes “exceptional periods” in which the state “as apparent mediator, acquires for the moment a certain independence in relation to both” classes, namely absolute monarchy and especially Bonapartism, from its normal character as “the state of the most powerful, economically ruling class” (Origin of the Family, Chapter IX). He calls the democratic republic “the highest form of the state” and “the form of state in which alone the last decisive battle between proletariat and bourgeoisie can be fought out,” while warning that “wealth here employs its power indirectly, but all the more surely” (Origin of the Family, Chapter IX). He linked the Bonapartism analysis to Marx’s Eighteenth Brumaire in his letters of 1890 and 1894 (Engels to Bloch; Engels to Borgius).

Marx’s own compressed statement of his contribution came in an 1852 letter to Joseph Weydemeyer:

“Now as for myself, I do not claim to have discovered either the existence of classes in modern society or the struggle between them. Long before me, bourgeois historians had described the historical development of this struggle between the classes, as had bourgeois economists their economic anatomy. My own contribution was 1. to show that the existence of classes is merely bound up with certain historical phases in the development of production; 2. that the class struggle necessarily leads to the dictatorship of the proletariat; 3. that this dictatorship itself constitutes no more than a transition to the abolition of all classes and to a classless society.”

(Source: Marx to Joseph Weydemeyer, 5 March 1852.)

“Dictatorship of the proletariat” here means rule by the organized working class during a transition, not the rule of a single dictator. The Manifesto describes the immediate aim as “formation of the proletariat into a class, overthrow of the bourgeois supremacy, conquest of political power by the proletariat,” and the first step as raising the proletariat “to the position of ruling class, to win the battle of democracy” (Manifesto, Chapter II). The working class would then use “its political supremacy to wrest, by degrees, all capital from the bourgeoisie, to centralise all instruments of production in the hands of the State, i.e., of the proletariat organised as the ruling class” (Manifesto, Chapter II). The ten measures listed there include abolition of landed property, a progressive income tax, abolition of inheritance, centralization of credit and communications, extension of state factories, equal liability to work, combining agriculture and manufacturing, and free public education. Marx says they “will, of course, be different in different countries” but “in most advanced countries will be pretty generally applicable,” and that they will “outstrip themselves, necessitate further inroads upon the old social order” as the movement develops (Manifesto, Chapter II).

The Paris Commune#

The Paris Commune was the government elected by the people of Paris in March 1871, after France’s defeat by Prussia and the collapse of the Second Empire. It lasted from 18 March to 28 May 1871, when Versailles troops crushed it. Marx wrote his three addresses to the General Council of the International Working Men’s Association during and immediately after those events, the third on 30 May, two days after the last barricades fell (Source: Stathis Kouvelakis, “On the Paris Commune: Part 2,” Verso, 2021). The Civil War in France was published as a pamphlet in early June. It is the fastest piece of analysis among the works discussed here, and that speed is a limitation as well as a strength: much of what Marx describes was taken from contemporary newspapers and reports, some of which repeated claims that later research corrected.

Its central warning is that “the working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready-made state machinery, and wield it for its own purposes” (Marx, The Civil War in France, Third Address, “The Paris Commune”). The reasons follow: “the centralized state power, with its ubiquitous organs of standing army, police, bureaucracy, clergy, and judicature,” originated in absolute monarchy, and as “the class antagonism between capital and labor” developed, “the state power assumed more and more the character of the national power of capital over labor, of a public force organized for social enslavement, of an engine of class despotism” (Civil War in France, Chapter 5).

Marx treated the Commune’s measures as a positive model. The standing army was to be replaced by the armed people; councillors were “chosen by universal suffrage” and “responsible and revocable at short terms”; the Commune was “a working, not a parliamentary body, executive and legislative at the same time”; police and officials were to be “responsible, and at all times revocable”; “public service had to be done at workman’s wage”; churches were to be disestablished and education made free; magistrates and judges were to be elected, responsible and revocable; and a national structure of delegates would be “at any time revocable and bound by the mandat imperatif (formal instructions) of his constituents” (Civil War in France, Chapter 5). Marx insisted that “the few but important functions which would still remain for a central government were not to be suppressed, but were to be discharged by Communal and thereafter responsible agents” (Civil War in France, Chapter 5).

His assessment of its meaning was unambiguous:

“Its true secret was this: It was essentially a working class government, the product of the struggle of the producing against the appropriating class, the political form at last discovered under which to work out the economical emancipation of labor… The Commune was therefore to serve as a lever for uprooting the economical foundation upon which rests the existence of classes, and therefore of class rule.”

(Source: Marx, The Civil War in France, Chapter 5.)

He stressed the Commune’s internationalism: it “annexed to France the working people all over the world,” admitted foreign workers, made a German worker its Minister of Labor, and placed Polish revolutionaries at the head of Paris’s defenders (Civil War in France, Chapter 5). He also stressed its appeal to the peasantry, whose “true originators of the war would be made to pay its cost” (Civil War in France, Chapter 5). The lessons were drawn in the same text: the working class “have no ready-made utopias to introduce par décret du peuple,” but “will have to pass through long struggles, through a series of historic processes, transforming circumstances and men,” and “have no ideals to realize, but to set free the elements of the new society with which old collapsing bourgeois society itself is pregnant” (Civil War in France, Chapter 5).

Historians have disputed how far Marx’s account matches what happened in Paris. Robert Tombs argues that classic Marxist analyses contained “myths, illusions of hindsight and polemical distortions,” including the treatment of measures that existed only on paper as if they had been applied. Jacques Rougerie, by contrast, came to regard Marx as a partial but privileged witness, and his research on arrests and court records established that the Commune was overwhelmingly a working-class insurrection. Stathis Kouvelakis reports that almost 70 percent of roughly 36,000 arrestees were workers, and that of the 78 members who took seats on the Commune Council, 33 were workers. Even Tombs concedes that the proportion of working-class leaders, about half the council, had probably never been matched in a European revolutionary government (Source: Stathis Kouvelakis, “On the Paris Commune: Part 2,” Verso, 2021; Robert Tombs, The Paris Commune 1871, Longman, 1999). Marx’s text is therefore a political intervention written in haste, and also a source whose central claim about the class character of the Commune has found substantial support in later social history.

The two phases in the Critique of the Gotha Programme#

The Critique of the Gotha Programme was written in April or early May 1875 against the draft programme of the United Workers’ Party of Germany. It was first published in abridged form in Die Neue Zeit in 1890 and 1891, with a foreword by Engels and Marx’s letter to Bracke (MIA editorial headnote, Critique of the Gotha Programme). Its subjects are the dictatorship of the proletariat, the transition from capitalism to communism, the two phases of communist society, the production and distribution of social goods, proletarian internationalism and the party of the working class (MIA editorial headnote).

Marx rejected the Lassallean slogan of the “undiminished proceeds of labor.” From the total social product, he argued, deductions must be made to replace used-up means of production, to expand production, to maintain reserve or insurance funds, to cover general administration, to provide “for the common satisfaction of needs, such as schools, health services, etc.,” and to support “those unable to work” (Critique of the Gotha Programme, Part I). These deductions “are an economic necessity” and “in no way calculable by equity” (Gotha, Part I).

The first phase is communist society “not as it has developed on its own foundations, but just as it emerges from capitalist society,” still “stamped with the birthmarks of the old society” (Gotha, Part I). In it:

“The individual producer receives back from society — after the deductions have been made — exactly what he gives to it. What he has given to it is his individual quantum of labor. He receives a certificate from society that he has furnished such-and-such an amount of labor, and with this certificate, he draws from the social stock of means of consumption as much as the same amount of labor cost. Hence, equal right here is still in principle bourgeois right. The right of the producers is proportional to the labor they supply; the equality consists in the fact that measurement is made with an equal standard, labor.”

(Source: Marx, Critique of the Gotha Programme, Part I.)

Marx did not romanticize this. He called it a case where “this equal right is an unequal right for unequal labor,” since it “tacitly recognizes unequal individual endowment, and thus productive capacity, as a natural privilege.” “One worker is married, another is not; one has more children than another,” he wrote, and “these defects are inevitable in the first phase of communist society,” because “right can never be higher than the economic structure of society and its cultural development conditioned thereby” (Gotha, Part I).

The higher phase is defined by the famous formula:

“In a higher phase of communist society, after the enslaving subordination of the individual to the division of labor, and therewith also the antithesis between mental and physical labor, has vanished; after labor has become not only a means of life but life’s prime want; after the productive forces have also increased with the all-around development of the individual, and all the springs of co-operative wealth flow more abundantly — only then can the narrow horizon of bourgeois right be crossed in its entirety and society inscribe on its banners: From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs!”

(Source: Marx, Critique of the Gotha Programme, Part I.)

Later Marxist-Leninist usage conventionally summarized the first-phase principle as “to each according to his work,” in contrast to the higher-phase “to each according to his needs.” Marx’s own text expresses the former as a proportional return of “an equal amount of labor in another form” after the necessary deductions (Gotha, Part I). He insisted that distribution is not the central question: “Any distribution whatever of the means of consumption is only a consequence of the distribution of the conditions of production themselves,” so that “vulgar socialism has taken over from the bourgeois economists the consideration and treatment of distribution as independent of the mode of production” (Gotha, Part I).

Engels’s own contributions#

Anti-Dühring and the systematic form of the theory#

Anti-Dühring (Herr Eugen Dühring’s Revolution in Science) was written between September 1876 and June 1878. Its three parts, Philosophy, Political Economy and Socialism, appeared in that order, with Marx writing the final chapter of Part II (MIA editorial headnote, Anti-Dühring). Engels’s importance lies in connecting philosophy, economics and socialism into one survey, and in the popular formula of “the two great discoveries”:

“These two great discoveries, the materialistic conception of history and the revelation of the secret of capitalistic production through surplus-value, we owe to Marx. With these discoveries, Socialism became a science. The next thing was to work out all its details and relations.”

(Source: Engels, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part II, “Dialectics”.)

Part III argues that state ownership of the productive forces “is not the solution of the conflict, but concealed within it are the technical conditions that form the elements of that solution” (Engels, Anti-Dühring, “Socialism, Theoretical”). Engels warned in a note against “a kind of spurious socialism” that “without more ado declares all state ownership, even of the Bismarckian sort, to be socialistic” (Engels, note 10 to Anti-Dühring, “Socialism, Theoretical”). That qualification matters because later states did declare state ownership to be socialist, and historians must distinguish Engels’s argument from those later claims.

Dialectics and natural science#

Engels used “dialectics” to mean the study of change through internal tensions, and “metaphysics,” in his own unusual sense, to mean treating things as fixed and isolated. His account of Hegel is famous:

“We again took a materialistic view of the thoughts in our heads, regarding them as images of real things instead of regarding real things as images of this or that stage of the absolute concept. Thus dialectics reduced itself to the science of the general laws of motion, both of the external world and of human thought. The dialectic of Hegel was turned over; or rather, turned off its head, on which it was standing, and placed upon its feet.”

(Source: Engels, Ludwig Feuerbach, Part 4.)

The inversion means that for Hegel ideas and spirit shape reality, whereas for Engels and Marx material conditions shape ideas. Engels pointed to the cell, the transformation of energy and Darwinian evolution as “three great discoveries” that allowed “a comprehensive view of the interconnection in nature” and made older natural philosophy unnecessary (Ludwig Feuerbach, Part 4). He distinguished blind natural processes from human history, where “the actors are all endowed with consciousness,” while insisting that “the course of history is governed by inner general laws” (Ludwig Feuerbach, Part 4). Socialism: Utopian and Scientific defined metaphysics as observing things “in isolation… in repose, not in motion… in their death, not in their life,” and dialectics as grasping them “in their essential connection, concatenation, motion, origin and ending.” “Nature is the proof of dialectics,” Engels added, naming Darwin first (Engels, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part II).

Dialectics of Nature is the manuscript collection in which these ideas were worked out. Its completed essay “The Part Played by Labour in the Transition from Ape to Man” (1876) argues that “labour created man himself.” Engels writes that “the hand is not only the organ of labour, it is also the product of labour,” and that “first comes labour, after it, and then side by side with it, articulate speech — these were the two most essential stimuli under the influence of which the brain of the ape gradually changed into that of man” (Engels, “The Part Played by Labour in the Transition from Ape to Man,” Chapter IX of Dialectics of Nature). He also warns that “we by no means rule over nature like a conqueror over a foreign people, but that we, with flesh, blood, and brain, belong to nature, and exist in its midst” (Dialectics of Nature, Chapter 9).

The status of these “laws of dialectics” is debated. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy records that Engels himself never used the compound term “dialectical materialism,” that the three laws appear in different forms in the published polemic and the unfinished manuscripts, and that interpretations range from outright rejection to qualified defences by thinkers such as Jon Elster, who treated them as plausible descriptions of patterns of change (Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Friedrich Engels”). Darren Roso’s study of Engels’s later work also shows that Engels revised the Manifesto’s opening claim about class struggle in the 1888 English edition in light of Lewis Morgan’s research on pre-class societies, an example of a doctrine being adjusted in response to new evidence (Source: Darren Roso, “Engels after Marx: a (critical) defence,” Historical Materialism, 2024).

The withering away of the state#

The classical formulation occurs in Anti-Dühring:

“The first act by virtue of which the state really constitutes itself the representative of the whole of society — the taking possession of the means of production in the name of society — this is, at the same time, its last independent act as a state. State interference in social relations becomes, in one domain after another, superfluous, and then dies out of itself; the government of persons is replaced by the administration of things, and by the conduct of processes of production. The state is not ‘abolished’. It dies out.”

(Source: Engels, Anti-Dühring, “Socialism, Theoretical”.)

The parallel passage in Socialism: Utopian and Scientific adds that “in proportion as anarchy in social production vanishes, the political authority of the State dies out,” and closes with “the ascent of man from the kingdom of necessity to the kingdom of freedom” (Engels, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part III). The Origin of the Family supplies the image: a society reorganizing production “on the basis of free and equal association of the producers will put the whole state machinery where it will then belong — into the museum of antiquities, next to the spinning wheel and the bronze ax” (Origin of the Family, Chapter IX). Both texts distinguish this process from the anarchist demand for “the abolition of the state out of hand” (Anti-Dühring, “Socialism, Theoretical”; Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part III).

The family and women’s oppression#

The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State was based on Marx’s 1880 and 1881 extracts from Lewis H. Morgan’s Ancient Society (1877), together with Engels’s own studies of Greece, Rome, Ireland and the ancient Germans (MIA editorial introduction, The Origin of the Family). Engels connected the family form to the division of labour and property. Monogamy, he argued, arose with the accumulation of wealth in the hands of men who wanted to transmit it to their own children. It “is based on the supremacy of the man, the express purpose being to produce children of undisputed paternity; such paternity is demanded because these children are later to come into their father’s property as his natural heirs” (Origin of the Family, Chapter II, section 4, “The Monogamous Family”).

The link between class society and women’s oppression is explicit:

“Monogamous marriage comes on the scene as the subjugation of the one sex by the other; it announces a struggle between the sexes unknown throughout the whole previous prehistoric period… The first class opposition that appears in history coincides with the development of the antagonism between man and woman in monogamous marriage, and the first class oppression coincides with that of the female sex by the male.”

(Source: Engels, The Origin of the Family, Chapter II, section 4.)

The material condition of women’s emancipation was stated just as directly:

“The emancipation of woman will only be possible when woman can take part in production on a large, social scale, and domestic work no longer claims anything but an insignificant amount of her time. And only now has that become possible through modern large-scale industry, which does not merely permit of the employment of female labor over a wide range, but positively demands it.”

(Source: Engels, The Origin of the Family, Chapter IX.)

Engels added that “the legal inequality of the two partners, bequeathed to us from earlier social conditions, is not the cause but the effect of the economic oppression of the woman,” and that “the first condition for the liberation of the wife is to bring the whole female sex back into public industry” (Origin of the Family, Chapter II, section 4). He treated prostitution as the “dark shadow” inseparable from monogamy under class society, and argued that with common ownership “private housekeeping is transformed into a social industry” and “the care and education of the children becomes a public affair” (Origin of the Family, Chapter II, section 4). These arguments were written for readers in 1884, when married women in much of Europe had few property rights and little access to paid work, and they should be read in that setting rather than as a description of later societies.

Engels as editor of Capital#

Marx left volumes II and III as extensive but unfinished manuscripts, and Engels’s prefaces are the primary record of how he made them publishable. For volume II (1885) he found “a great number of available, mostly fragmentary, texts,” of which “at best one single manuscript (No. IV) had been revised throughout and made ready for press.” The rest was “not finally polished, in point of language,” written in a “careless style full of colloquialisms, often containing coarsely humorous expressions and phrases interspersed with English and French technical terms” (Engels, Preface to the First Edition, Capital, Volume II, 5 May 1885). His method was “to reproduce these manuscripts as literally as possible, changing the style only in places where Marx would have changed it himself and interpolating explanatory sentences or connecting statements only where this was absolutely necessary.” The passages he remodelled or interpolated “cover barely ten pages in print and concern only matters of form” (Engels, Preface to Capital, Volume II).

For volume III (1894) the task “was essentially different”: “there was nothing to go by outside a first extremely incomplete draft” (Engels, Preface to Capital, Volume III, 4 October 1894). Engels dictated the whole manuscript into readable copy, then limited himself “to the essential,” preserving the first draft where possible and enclosing in brackets and initialling passages where “my alterations or additions exceeded the bounds of editing” (Engels, Preface to Capital, Volume III). Chapter IV, on turnover and the rate of profit, was written by Engels himself and is wholly bracketed. The final chapter on the three great classes was only begun, because “Marx used to leave such concluding summaries until the final editing, just before going to press” (Engels, Preface to Capital, Volume III). These prefaces establish what Engels said he did. Whether the edited volumes faithfully represent Marx’s intended argument is a question that the publication of the original manuscripts has reopened, and scholars continue to disagree about it (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Friedrich Engels”).

Who deserves credit?#

Engels’s footnote of 1886 assigns “the greater part of its leading basic principles, especially in the realm of economics and history, and, above all, their final trenchant formulation” to Marx (Ludwig Feuerbach, Part 4, note 1). His preface to Capital, Volume II, lists what he regarded as Marx’s specific discoveries: labour-power as distinct from labour, constant as distinct from variable capital, and absolute and relative surplus value (Engels, Preface to Volume II). His graveside speech of 1883 reduces these to “the law of development of human history” and “the special law of motion governing the present-day capitalist mode of production,” adding that “Marx was before all else a revolutionist” (Engels, burial speech, 17 March 1883).

Those statements are the best evidence of how the two men presented their partnership, but a participant’s own verdict is not the last word. Engels had reasons to stress Marx’s primacy: he was the surviving executor, the editor of the unfinished volumes and the public defender of a legacy. Modern scholarship, aided by the original manuscripts, treats the division of credit as a question that is still open, especially where Engels’s own books, his dialectics and his editing of Capital are concerned. The most that can be said with confidence is that Marx wrote the core of the economic critique, that Engels wrote major works in his own name and made Marx’s unfinished work available, and that both men presented the result as a shared undertaking (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Friedrich Engels”; Darren Roso, “Engels after Marx: a (critical) defence”).
