Thomas Sankara and the Burkina Faso Revolution

The Democratic and Popular Revolution, women's emancipation, self-reliance and Sankara's collected speeches.

Thomas Isidore Noël Sankara was born on 21 December 1949 at Yako in Upper Volta, then a French colony in West Africa. He was an army captain when he became head of state on 4 August 1983, and he was killed in office on 15 October 1987. The four years between those dates are known as the Democratic and Popular Revolution, or RDP from its French name, Révolution démocratique et populaire. The body that held power was the National Council of the Revolution, usually called the CNR, and the mass organisations it called into being were the Committees for the Defence of the Revolution, the CDRs. These are not neutral descriptions. They are the names the revolution gave itself, and any account of the period has to keep them distinct from the judgements of later writers.

The surviving record is unusually rich in the revolution’s own voice. The Marxists Internet Archive preserves twelve dated texts by Sankara, including his radio proclamation of 4 August 1983 and his speech to the Organisation of African Unity on debt in 1987 (Thomas Sankara Archive). The site thomassankara.net, maintained by people who identify with the movement, publishes the ordinances, the chronology, the speeches and interviews with participants (official site). Pathfinder Press issued a collected volume, Thomas Sankara Speaks, in English and French editions (Pathfinder, English edition; French edition). These are primary sources, and they are indispensable. They are also interested witnesses, so historians have had to put them alongside other evidence. Brian J. Peterson’s Thomas Sankara: A Revolutionary in Cold War Africa drew on government archives and more than a hundred interviews with family, friends and colleagues, and treats Sankara as a polarising figure rather than a consensual hero (Indiana University Press, 2021). Ernest Harsch, who reported from Burkina Faso during the revolution and met Sankara, wrote both a short biography and a wider national history (Ohio University Press, 2014; Zed Books, 2017). The 2018 collection A Certain Amount of Madness, edited by Amber Murrey, brings together scholars who examine the revolution’s limits as well as its appeal, including its difficult relations with trade unions (Pluto Press). James E. Genova has studied the revolution’s use of film as political education (Michigan State University Press, 2022). This chapter uses the movement’s documents as evidence, and sets beside them the testimony of participants and the judgements of these historians where they help to explain what happened.

The making of an officer, 1949 to 1983#

The date and place of Sankara’s birth appear in both the archive headnote and the movement’s chronology, whose first entry reads: “21/12/49. Naissance de Thomas Sankara à Yako,” meaning “21 December 1949. Birth of Thomas Sankara at Yako.” (MIA archive index; official chronology). The official site reproduces a biographical note issued by the Union pour la Renaissance, Mouvement Patriotique Sankariste, or UNIR MPS, for the seventy-sixth anniversary of his birth. It gives the movement’s account of his upbringing and education. He spent his early childhood at Gaoua, where his father was a soldier, and went to secondary school at the Lycée Ouezzin Coulibaly in Bobo-Dioulasso, studying Greek and Latin. As a schoolboy at the moment of independence he replaced a colonial flag with that of the future Upper Volta. The note names his history and geography teacher, Adama Abdoulaye Touré, who introduced him to the idea of class struggle and to the underground literature of the African Independence Party, the PAI, and the Patriotic League for Development, LIPAD (UNIR MPS declaration, 19 December 2025). Such a note is a piece of movement memory, written long after the events, and it reads Sankara’s childhood in the light of what he later became.

The chronology supplies the institutional stages. Sankara won a place at the Prytanée Militaire de Kadiogo in 1966, entered the Military Academy at Antsirabé in Madagascar in October 1969, and completed his training in June 1972, staying on for an extra year of civic service. He returned to Upper Volta in October 1973 and was posted to Bobo-Dioulasso. In December 1974 he led his men in a border clash with Mali. In 1976 he took command of the national commando training centre at Pô. He trained alongside Blaise Compaoré in Rabat in 1978, and Compaoré became his deputy at Pô. He married Mariam Sermé in December 1979, joined the army general staff and was promoted captain in 1981 (official chronology). These stages matter because they contradict the idea that Sankara was a spontaneous military ruler. He was a professionally trained officer with a political education acquired over many years.

The Madagascar period carries weight in the movement’s own story. The UNIR MPS declaration says that at Antsirabé he “discovered popular organisation, citizen participation, community mobilisation and institutional transformation”, and that he asked to stay an extra year because “one does not understand a revolution from a distance, but by living it more closely”. The declaration calls that choice “foundational” and says it inspired the later CDRs. It names Amílcar Cabral, Patrice Lumumba and Che Guevara as the three figures he took as principles: Cabral for the view that liberation is first a moral and cultural work, Lumumba for the sacrifice of African dignity, and Guevara for total commitment and the fraternity of struggles (UNIR MPS declaration).

Inside the army Sankara joined, with Compaoré and others, a clandestine grouping of young officers known by its French initials, ROC, the Regroupement des officiers communistes. The same source says the grouping refused to repress people during the general strike of December 1975. The movement tells a story about the well at Pô that it treats as the practical sketch of his later idea of authority. Soldiers pushed women aside at the water point; Sankara made them queue and later obtained a motor pump. The moral drawn by the movement is that “authority has value only if it protects”. It says the habits that became policy were already present in the young officer: recruiting activists from the peasantry, creating the CDRs as organs of popular power, refusing privilege and describing the army as fused with the people (UNIR MPS declaration).

A witness who lived in Pô gives the well story a more specific setting. Apérika Gomgnimbou, a farmer who says he saw events there from Sankara’s arrival in 1976 to the revolution of August 1983, recalled that there was no running water in Pô and that everyone drew from a well known as the UNESCO well. Soldiers forced the women to let them draw first. “The old men of the neighbourhood, including my own father, rebelled and formed a delegation to go and explain the problem to Thomas Sankara”, he said. Sankara came with the soldiers and told them to queue and wait their turn. Gomgnimbou added that the incident led Sankara to promise better water supply, and that the water utility ONEA was established in Pô towards the end of 1978 (interview with Apérika Gomgnimbou, published by thomassankara.net in 2009). His account was recorded decades after the events and published by a movement website, so it cannot be treated as a neutral record. It does, however, name a place, a disputed resource and local people who acted, and it broadly corroborates the movement’s story while giving the initiative to the elders rather than to Sankara alone.

Three coups preceded the August revolution. On 25 November 1980 Colonel Sayé Zerbo led the coup of the Committee of Military Redressment for National Progress, which suspended the institutions. The movement’s historical summary attributes it to blocked wages and rising prices (chronology; Histoire du Burkina Faso). Sankara entered Zerbo’s government as Secretary of State for Information in September 1981 and resigned in April 1982, in the movement’s memory with the words Malheur à ceux qui bâillonnent le peuple!, meaning “Woe to those who gag the people!” He was arrested at Dédougou in May 1982 with Compaoré and Henri Zongo. The coup of 7 November 1982 brought the military doctor Jean-Baptiste Ouédraogo to power through the Council of Popular Salvation, whose constituent assembly proceedings the official site publishes (CSP procès-verbal). Sankara became prime minister in January 1983, attacked neocolonialism at the Non-Aligned summit in New Delhi in March, was arrested in May after the arrival of President Mitterrand’s African adviser Guy Penne, and was released under house arrest later that month. Popular demonstrations demanded his release. On 4 August 1983 the assault came from Pô (chronology; MIA headnote).

The Political Orientation Speech, delivered on 2 October 1983, gives the movement’s reading of these events. The May 1983 “imperialist plot” did not simply provoke a reaction; it brought about “a large-scale consolidation of democratic and revolutionary forces and organisations”, and the speech says the masses made rapid political progress in understanding their situation. It puts the point in a famous image: “There are days that teach lessons with a wealth comparable to that of an entire decade. During these days, the people learn with a rapidity and an incredible depth of spirit, so that one thousand days of study are nothing next to them.” (Political Orientation Speech). The phrase about a “qualitative leap”, meaning a sudden change in kind rather than a gradual increase, belongs to the vocabulary of the dialectic. By dialectic the speech means the idea that change comes from the collision of opposing social forces. It was a term the revolution used about itself, not a description invented by historians.

4 August 1983: the proclamation and the new organs#

At ten in the evening on 4 August 1983, after Compaoré and 250 men had marched from Pô to Ouagadougou, Sankara spoke on the radio as president of the new CNR. The MIA edition preserves the text in full (A radiant future for our country). The proclamation is more than a victory bulletin. It is an act that creates a new state. It names the enemy, “the submissive and groveling regime established on May 17, 1983”. It announces: “formed the National Council of the Revolution. This body now assumes state power”. It dissolves the parties of the old regime and, in the same breath, calls for the mass organisations:

“The National Council of the Revolution invites the Voltaic people to form committees for the Defense of the Revolution everywhere, in order to participate in the CNR’s great patriotic struggle and to prevent our enemies here and abroad from doing our people harm.”

Source: Proclamation of 4 August 1983

The closing lines fixed the ritual formula of the revolution: “Homeland or death, we will win! … Long live the Voltaic people! Long live the National Council of the Revolution!” An archive headnote adds a qualification the document itself does not make. Only parties associated with the former neocolonial regime were dissolved, and a number of groups that supported the revolution continued to function openly (Proclamation).

The Political Orientation Speech of 2 October explains the place of the CDRs in the system. “That’s why the first act of the revolution, after the Proclamation of August 4, was sent to people calling for the creation of Committees for the Defense of the Revolution (CDR).” It describes their role in two short sentences: “The CDRs are organizing genuine people in the exercise of revolutionary power”, and “The CDRs are then the assault detachments that will address all sources of resistance.” They were to be the leaven that carried the revolution into every province, village, service and home (Political Orientation Speech). In a January 1984 interview Sankara insisted that the event had been “a thoroughly prepared popular insurrection in which progressives, revolutionaries and democrats came together to end a regime of submission to imperialism”, and called the CDRs “the main weapon, the frontline shock troops in the battle that will allow our revolution to triumph” (We Didn’t Import Our Revolution, 23 January 1984).

The same interview shows how the revolution understood political consciousness. Sankara rejected the idea that decrees alone could change a country: “There is a first step, which consists in issuing decrees and ordinances; we reject this because it is essentially bureaucratic. The second consists in lifting the popular masses out of obscurantism. That is the measure we endeavor to undertake.” He described the revolution as the result of a historical process that was, in his words, “scientifically verified and inevitable”, and said the soldiers of Upper Volta were politicised and linked to their people (We Didn’t Import Our Revolution). On the source page the sentence reads with spaced hyphens: “It is the result of a historical process - scientifically verified and inevitable - in the transformation of the struggles that the social classes have to wage against each other” (MIA text).

The Political Orientation Speech and the revolution’s class analysis#

The DOP was written during September and October 1983 and broadcast on radio and television on 2 October. The official site is explicit that it was a collective work. A CNR committee of Philippe Ouédraogo for the PAI, Valère Somé for the Union of Communist Struggles Rebuilt, or ULCR, and Blaise Compaoré for the military was formed. Somé did most of the drafting, Sankara amended it and delivered it, and the foreign-policy section was written in the hours before the broadcast. The site calls it “the theoretical foundation of the revolution” and “the reference document of the revolution” (Political Orientation Speech page).

One of the participants has described the drafting from the inside. Philippe Ouédraogo was a former leader of the PAI and the party’s representative on the drafting committee. Interviewed in 2023 by Yasmine Bouland for a Sorbonne master’s thesis, and published by thomassankara.net with an introduction by Bruno Jaffré, he said that at the first session he argued that the argument over which slogan should define the revolution was not fundamental, because everyone on the committee agreed about the situation and the measures needed. “I therefore accepted that RDP should be retained as the slogan defining the struggle”, he said. After that first session he had no chance to take part in the rest of the discussions. As he understood it, Valère Somé of the ULCR wrote most of the draft (interview with Philippe Ouédraogo). This is a retrospective account given forty years later by someone with a stake in the PAI’s place in the story. It is valuable precisely because it locates the text in a negotiation between organisations rather than in one man’s inspiration, and it agrees with the movement’s own account of who drafted what.

The speech’s class analysis is the source of the revolution’s self-definition. Its enemies are the state bourgeoisie, meaning the political and bureaucratic elite; the comprador bourgeoisie, meaning merchants whose wealth depended on foreign capital; and the middle bourgeoisie. It names “the retrograde forces that draw their power from the traditional structures of feudal society”, meaning customary authorities whose position rested on land and lineage, and the lumpen-proletariat, meaning people outside regular employment, whom it treats as a reserve of reaction that the revolution might win over by giving them useful work. The people are the working class, “young and small, but has managed to prove in his constant struggles against the employers, it is a really revolutionary class”; the petty bourgeoisie, meaning small shopkeepers, clerks, students and artisans; and the peasantry, which the speech calls “the main force” by number (Political Orientation Speech).

On the character of the revolution the speech is precise. It calls the August Revolution “a democratic and popular revolution” and explains the two words separately. Its democratic character comes from the task of ending imperialist domination and the social barriers that kept the country backward. Its popular character comes from the mobilisation of the masses around democratic demands and their action against the reactionary classes allied to imperialism. The speech also states a limit: the revolution “is still done within the limits of bourgeois social and economic regime”. That is why, it says, calling oneself a revolutionary is not enough; “learn to link revolutionary theory with revolutionary practice” is the decisive test (Political Orientation Speech).

The movement’s vocabulary drew on a wide inheritance. The DOP uses the language of Marxism-Leninism, the body of ideas developed by Marx, Engels and Lenin and later claimed by parties that called themselves communist. The movement’s own biography names Cabral, Lumumba and Guevara. In his UN speech of 1984 Sankara claimed the inheritance of the American revolution, the French revolution of 1789 and the revolution of October 1917, while insisting that none of the three was a model to be imported. “Open to all the wishes of the peoples and their revolutions, learning also from the terrible failures that have led to truly sad infringements of human rights, we want to preserve from each revolution only that essence of purity that prohibits us from becoming servants to the realities of others”, he said (UN speech, 4 October 1984). The Third World was reclaimed rather than denied: he spoke of “a three-continent whole” binding Asia, Latin America and Africa in the same struggle. He quoted José Martí on feeling “every blow struck against every other man in the world”, and Fidel Castro’s figures on what military spending could build (UN speech). At Harlem in October 1984 he invoked Maurice Bishop of Grenada and Jerry John Rawlings of Ghana by name (Our White House is in Black Harlem). At Harare in 1986 he called on “Tito, Nehru, Nasser, Kwame Nkrumah” to wake up, because the Non-Aligned Movement was dying. The original continues: On peut se faire assister sans se faire assujettir, meaning “one can receive help without being subjugated” (Harare speech, 3 September 1986). Frantz Fanon’s The Wretched of the Earth supplied the phrase “these wretched of the earth” in the DOP, and the movement held a symposium on Cheikh Anta Diop in 1986 (DOP; Blacks Must Take Responsibility for Their Own History).

Whether the revolution was in fact Marxist-Leninist is disputed. Many historians and commentators describe it in those terms. Philippe Ouédraogo rejects the label. “No! No leader or official of any of the political organisations in the CNR ever claimed that”, he said in the 2023 interview. He granted that influential members called themselves Marxists and that the revolution borrowed Marxist vocabulary, organisation and methods, but argued that it could not be a Marxist-Leninist revolution without a ruling Marxist party, and that it was in the end absorbed by officers, most of whom had no Marxist education (interview with Philippe Ouédraogo). Peterson’s biography reaches a more qualified conclusion from a very different body of evidence, describing Sankara as a Marxist and a populist whose appeal crossed ideological lines and whose rivals eventually isolated him (Indiana University Press). The disagreement is worth keeping open. A participant’s memory and a historian’s archive can both be right about different parts of the question.

The organisations of the revolution#

The documents record a post-1987 coalition around Compaoré called the Front populaire. No source consulted for this chapter mentions a Rally of the Patriotic Left or an organisation called Akwara. What the primary record does show is the following structure.

The official site lists four governments between 24 August 1983 and September 1987, each dissolved in August in a deliberate rejection of ministerial permanence (Governments of the CNR). In a 1985 interview Sankara explained the relationship: the CNR was the political directorate and the Council of Ministers carried out its decisions. On democracy he rejected the ballot box as a measure in a country with mass illiteracy. His words were la démocratie ce n’est pas le bulletin de vote. C’est de la mystification. C’est un viol et un vol des consciences dans un pays où il y a à peine 2% d’alphabétisés, that is, “democracy is not the ballot box. It is mystification. It is the rape and theft of consciences in a country where barely two per cent are literate” (interview of 20 September 1985).

From 1986 the CDRs were supplemented by People’s Commissions in the ministries and by sectoral unions of youth, women, peasants and pioneers. The movement’s own chronology says this followed the discrediting of the CDRs by abuses and describes the aim as tightening the web of social control. The first national CDR conference, held from 31 March to 4 April 1986, denounced “pillagers” and “terrorists” in the base organisations, and Sankara’s closing speech insisted: L’abus de pouvoir doit être étranger aux CDR, or “abuse of power must be alien to the CDRs” (chronology).

Two participants describe what that abuse meant in practice. Inoussa Ouédraogo, a former rank-and-file CDR activist, told Bruno Jaffré by email in December 2011 that he was recruited the day after 4 August and became his class delegate and then the students’ delegate for political and ideological formation at his school. He remembered working on a student collective farm at Tapoa, with a 6 a.m. parade, flag raising, work until noon and evening discussions. His verdict was positive but critical: the CDR experience was highly positive and should be repeated, “with better political guidance in particular”. Without serious political and ideological training, he said, a CDR was “a brute who knows no language but that of force” (interview with Inoussa Ouédraogo). The academic collection edited by Murrey makes the same point about the revolution’s relations with organised labour, where Craig Phelan examines the collision between the CNR and the unions, and Leo Zeilig writes of an “elusive revolution” (Pluto Press). The gap between the movement’s claim of popular power and the experience of some of its own activists is part of the history, not a footnote to it.

The name and symbols of Burkina Faso#

On 4 August 1984, the first anniversary of the revolution, Upper Volta became Burkina Faso. The chronology records the whole package in one entry: the country took a new name, a new flag and a new anthem, the Ditanyé, a word in the Lobi language meaning “the song of victory” (official chronology). The official site gives the meaning of the name throughout the corpus as “the land of upright people”, in French le pays des hommes intègres (Sa politique). Sankara used the phrase himself in the 1985 anniversary speech, where he contrasted the punishment of a citizen who stole a bowl of rice with the impunity of officials who diverted food aid (4 August 1985 speech).

The legal act was Ordinance number 84-043/CNR/PRES of 2 August 1984 on the change of name and national symbols, cited in the preamble of the land ordinance (Ordonnance 84-050). The revolution’s newspaper, Sidwaya, meaning “the truth has arrived”, was founded on 5 April 1984 (chronology). Sankara described the flag in Harlem: red for the revolutionary struggle, green for agriculture, abundance and hope, and a yellow star for the country’s riches (Harlem speech). He explained the new name as a project of mental decolonisation: On ne peut rien faire tant qu’on n’impose pas aux mentalités des schémas de rigueur. Il nous faut travailler à décoloniser les mentalités, et réaliser le bonheur à la limite des sacrifices auxquels nous devons consentir. In English, “we can do nothing unless we impose rigorous patterns on people’s minds. We must work to decolonise mentalities and achieve happiness within the limits of the sacrifices we must consent to” (La révolution burkinabé, les grandes orientations). Genova’s cultural history of the period argues that this effort to make new people, through film as well as through speeches, was one of the revolution’s most durable innovations (Michigan State University Press).

Land reform#

The land reform is set out in full on the official site. Ordinance number 84-050/CNR/PRES of 4 August 1984, signed “Capitaine Thomas SANKARA”, created a National Land Domain, and Decree number 85-404 of 4 August 1985 applied it (Ordonnance 84-050; Décret 85-404). The preamble states the object without euphemism: to give real meaning to the principle “Power and all power to the people” by drawing up a new status for land and new guiding principles for rural life.

The operative articles show what this meant. Article 1 created a National Land Domain covering all land within the national territory. Article 3 made it the exclusive property of the state. Article 4 cancelled existing private titles, which could be replaced by use-rights. Article 5 declared the domain inalienable, imprescriptible and immune from seizure; that is, it could not be sold, could not pass into private ownership through long use, and could not be seized for debt. Article 18 provided that urban and rural plots were to be allocated to those with a real social need, “without distinction of sex or marital status”. Articles 19 and 20 required allocation commissions to include the relevant CDR office, one plot per person or household in towns and the participation of the village CDR in the countryside. Article 25 said rural masses must be organised in democratic structures, and that every modernisation plan must follow this collective strategy. Articles 27 and 32 provided for self-managed savings and credit funds and for farmers’ and herders’ organisations to be involved in setting purchase and sale prices. The ordinance ends with the revolutionary formula and Sankara’s signature (Ordonnance 84-050).

This was not a redistribution of private title to peasants. It was the abolition of private landed property and its replacement by use-rights administered with the CDRs. The site’s editorial note says the reform had a double aim: to raise agricultural productivity and to solve the urban housing problem. It was meant to free the peasantry from the customary chieftaincy, described as feudal, to open access to housing for all, and to end land speculation by the bourgeoisie, giving the CDRs a monopoly of land management. The note records that the reform was only partly applied and was buried by the Compaoré regime, which restored private property through later reforms in 1991 and 1996 (Ordonnance 84-050).

Philippe Ouédraogo, the former PAI leader, gives a participant’s account of the reform’s effects. He says its aims were to reaffirm that the land belongs to the state, to stop speculation and the seizure of lowlands, water points and forests by customary chiefs or wealthy individuals, to place land and subsoil resources at the disposal of the state for public works, housing and reserves, and to formalise state ownership of minerals. Under the revolution, he says, the reform made citizens aware of the importance of land, accelerated urban plot allocation and briefly checked speculation in city plots. After the revolution speculation resumed in both town and countryside, though later changes to the law left the administration with better texts for managing land disputes (interview with Philippe Ouédraogo). Sankara’s own account in the January 1984 interview had been about political confidence: “For the first time, peasants dare to inform the authorities of the abuses they suffer. We do not see this as an act of snitching … but rather as the beginning of an awareness among our peasants, who now intend to effectively participate in the daily management of power” (We Didn’t Import Our Revolution). The two statements, one from the first months and one from long afterwards, describe different things: a claim about political awakening and an assessment of what changed on the ground.

The emancipation of women#

The revolution’s central social claim was that “the revolution cannot triumph without the emancipation of women”. The most systematic statement is the speech of 8 March 1987, published in English by the official site from the Pathfinder collection (The revolution cannot triumph without the emancipation of women). It opens with a distinction between men and women: “The revolution’s promise is already a reality for men. But for women, it is still merely a rumor. And yet the authenticity and the future of our revolution depend on women.” It calls for a profound change in how men and women see themselves, because society was not only creating new social relations but overturning the authority of men over women.

The speech is explicitly Marxist. It credits dialectical materialism, the Marxist account of nature, society and thought as driven by material contradiction, with shedding the greatest light on women’s condition. It follows Engels in dating women’s defeat to the rise of private property, quoting the line “Man, master of his slaves and of the land, became in addition the woman’s master. This was the historic defeat of the female sex.” It insists, though, that women’s oppression is not the same as class oppression: in addition to the class exploitation they share with men, women face relations of conflict and violence that use physical difference as their pretext. It attacks revolutionary men who will not let their wives be politically active, or allow it only by day, or beat them for attending meetings (8 March 1987 speech).

The French text asks whether the community can still accept the levirate and the dowry. Pouvons-nous admettre le lévirat et la dot réduisant nos soeurs et nos mères au statut de biens vulgaires qui font l’objet de tractations ? translates as “Can we admit the levirate and the dowry which reduce our sisters and mothers to the status of common goods that are the object of bargaining?” The levirate is the practice by which a widow marries her husband’s brother (French text). The speech ends with demands for equal pay, a new labour code and the involvement of the ministries of culture and family with the Union des Femmes du Burkina. Pathfinder’s preface records that it was delivered to a rally of several thousand women in Ouagadougou, and quotes Dorotea Wilson of Nicaragua’s National Assembly calling it evidence of active solidarity with women’s struggles (English text).

Institutional change accompanied the speeches. The national conference for the promotion of women, held from 1 to 8 March 1985, gathered 3,000 delegates, and the Union des Femmes du Burkina was created in September 1985 (chronology). Women entered the armed forces and the government, and the land ordinance allocated land without distinction of sex or marital status (Governments of the CNR; ordonnance 84-050, art. 18). In the 1985 anniversary speech Sankara announced that wages would cease to be the sole property of the husband and become family property, paid to the wife through the salaire vital. He added, L’État ne contraint personne au mariage, mais exige que celui qui fonde un foyer assume ses responsabilités, meaning “the state forces no one to marry, but requires that whoever starts a household accept its responsibilities” (4 August 1985 speech).

A woman who took part in the mobilisation remembers both its scale and its cost. Sawadogo Antaille, who worked in information and mobilisation for the World March of Women and was an official in the primary teachers’ union SNEA-B, told an interviewer in 2010 that she and her husband were dismissed after the union criticised the 1983 coup. She worked as a cashier in a pharmacy while her husband found work elsewhere. “In all the history of the country we had never seen such mobilisation of women”, she said, describing buses sent to bring women from every district to demonstrations and the creation of women-only branch offices under the revolution. She also said she could not understand how expressing a point of view could cost someone their job, and noted that teachers were reinstated after Sankara’s fall (Témoignages sur la révolution, thomassankara.net, 2010, from Bendré number 615 of 16 October 2010). Her account, published by a movement site nearly a quarter of a century later, gives a woman’s assessment that is neither simple praise nor simple rejection. It supports the claim that women were mobilised on a new scale, and it exposes the limits of the revolution’s tolerance for independent organisation.

On dress, the source is the closing speech at the second national CDR conference at Dédougou on 3 April 1987, where Sankara made the wearing of Faso Dan Fani, locally woven cotton, a political act. Vous êtes habillé en tergal et moi je suis habillé en Faso Dan Fani, la différence est faite, he said: “You are dressed in tergal and I in Faso Dan Fani, the difference is made.” He went on that every village in Burkina Faso could grow cotton, every village had women who could spin it and men who could weave and sew it, so he had no worries (Dédougou speech). The same speech ties the consumption boycott to women: la meilleure arme, ce sont les femmes. Si les femmes ne sont pas d’accord, si les femmes sont pro-impérialistes, les repas seront pro-impérialistes! In English, “the best weapon is the women. If the women do not agree, if the women are pro-imperialist, the meals will be pro-imperialist” (Dédougou speech). Women’s emancipation was not a separate campaign in this thinking. It was a condition of the economic and cultural programme as a whole.

Health, education and culture#

The chronology records the launch on 25 November 1984 of a “vaccination commando” across the country, lasting fifteen days and reaching about 2.56 million children against measles, meningitis and yellow fever. It adds that the World Health Organization cited the operation as an example (chronology). Sankara gave the same figures in Paris in February 1986, speaking of children from Burkina Faso and neighbouring countries, and in July 1986 he stressed that vaccination crossed borders without regard to the political regime of the children’s countries, “in the name of love for the people” (Paris speech, 5 February 1986; Radio Havana interview, 19 July 1986).

The doctrine was primary health care. In his UN speech Sankara said Burkina Faso supported and applied the principles of the Declaration of Alma-Ata and had adopted UNICEF’s GOBI FFF strategy as state policy, meaning a package of growth monitoring, oral rehydration, breast feeding, immunisation, female education, family spacing and food supplementation. He spoke for mothers whose children died of malaria and diarrhoea when simple methods existed but were not offered by the multinationals (UN speech). The July 1986 interview claimed thousands of clinics, “one health clinic per village”, while the movement’s policy summary says communities chose their own health agent and auxiliary midwife, that hospitalisation was free with a certificate of indigence, that traditional healers were consulted in hospital, and that drug prices were unified (Radio Havana interview; policy summary).

The educational baseline was severe. Sankara put illiteracy at 98 per cent in 1984 if literacy meant reading, writing and speaking a language, with 16 per cent of children in school and one doctor for 50,000 people (UN speech). The DOP gave 92 per cent illiteracy and 16.4 per cent enrolment (DOP). The results claimed in the movement’s texts are that literacy rose from 16 to 22 per cent in under three years, that primary enrolment rose from 16.5 per cent in 1983 to 20.9 per cent in 1985 and 24 per cent in 1986 according to UNICEF, and that an “Alphabétisation commando” taught about 35,000 adults in national languages between February and April 1986 (4 August 1986 speech; policy summary; chronology). School fees were cut in September 1984 and the capitation tax abolished in October (chronology). Culturally, the revolution made the Semaine Nationale de la Culture and the FESPACO film festival central institutions, and used music and theatre for what it called conscientisation, or raising political awareness (Harlem speech; cultural policy).

Soumaila Ouédraogo, known as Ila, a resident of Ouahigouya who served as a CDR delegate for defence and security in the north for six years, remembered the practical side of this mobilisation in a 2010 interview. He described residents digging a water point by hand so market gardeners could work in the dry season, and people building the “Cités du 4 Août” housing after a directive from the CDR secretariat. “The revolution taught the population a great deal of civic spirit and love of work”, he said, adding that patrols and controls had reduced petty banditry and corruption (Témoignages sur la révolution). Like the other recollections, this was recorded long afterwards and published by a movement site. It tells us what participation meant to one delegate, not how widely his experience was shared.

Austerity, debt and the refusal of IMF and World Bank adjustment#

The revolution’s economic line is stated most directly in Sankara’s answer to the debt question in 1985 and in the anniversary speeches of 1985 and 1986. The movement’s policy summary quotes him on the international financial institutions: Nous avons refusé les prêts de la BM pour alimenter des projets que nous n’avons pas choisis… Nous avons dit au FMI : ce que vous nous demandez, nous l’avons déjà fait. Nous avons réduit les salaires, assaini l’économie, vous n’avez rien à nous enseigner. In English, “We refused World Bank loans to feed projects we had not chosen. We told the IMF: what you ask of us, we have already done. We cut salaries, we cleaned up the economy, you have nothing to teach us.” He adds that every time they spoke to the IMF they had to provide fresh guarantees, and that what the institution sought went beyond management control to political control (policy summary).

At the UN in 1984 he rejected the models offered by the aid industry: “There can be no salvation for our peoples unless we turn our backs completely on all the models that all the charlatans of that type have tried to sell us for 20 years. There can be no development without that break” (UN speech). The 1986 anniversary speech developed the alternative of “consume what you produce”. Ce sera un geste hautement patriotique et révolutionnaire, que de consommer burkinabè, he said: “It will be a highly patriotic and revolutionary gesture to consume Burkinabè.” He called for credit chosen on the basis of building an independent economy, “really freed from foreign domination”, and for a new Burkina Faso built to the measure of the country’s own means, “without pessimism but without the optimism of a lunatic” (4 August 1986 speech). The OAU speech of 1987 gave the idea its best known English form: “produce in Africa, transform in Africa, consume in Africa. Let’s produce what we need and let’s consume what we produce instead of importing.” Sankara pointed to his own delegation’s clothes, woven and sewn in Burkina Faso, with “not a single thread coming from Europe or America” (A United Front Against Debt).

Austerity was presented as an internally decided sacrifice. The chronology records cuts of 25 to 50 per cent in officials’ allowances in January 1984, the abolition of special mission envelopes in March, rent reductions, the “Cités du 4 Août” housing programme, a budget conference that ran from half past eight at night to eight in the morning and adopted salary deductions, and a levy of 5 to 12 per cent on salaries in January 1986 for the Popular Investment Effort, or EPI (chronology). In the 1985 interview Sankara said the budget had been purged of privilege before any appeal to the masses: Nous avons supprimé la plupart des avantages. Nous ne achetons plus rien, ce n’est pas un budget d’équipement, that is, “we have abolished most of the advantages. We buy nothing more, it is not a budget for equipment” (interview of 20 September 1985). The movement’s summary gives the budget rising from 58 billion CFA francs in 1983, with 12 billion for debt service, to 93 billion in 1987, and moving from a deficit in 1983 to a surplus in 1985 (policy summary). These are the movement’s own figures, and they should be read as claims about policy rather than as an outside assessment of the budget.

The “moral example” was central to the revolution’s self-image. In Dédougou in 1987 Sankara turned it into an argument about salary: Les salaires que nous payons chaque année s’en vont à l’étranger. Et rien ne reste au Burkina Faso. “The salaries we pay every year go abroad. And nothing stays in Burkina Faso.” He asked how the people could go on being squeezed to pay a minority whose spending was mostly overseas (Dédougou speech). He denounced “men, chameleons who have managed to keep a deceptive cloak while stealing the goods of the people, the privileges and the status of the people, in order to terrorise and exploit the people”, and demanded their expulsion from the ranks (4 August 1986 speech). The sale of the government’s luxury cars and Sankara’s use of a small Renault are recorded in the movement’s biographical material, which also notes the simple office (UNIR MPS declaration).

Production, industrialisation and the environment#

The Five-Year Popular Development Plan for 1986 to 1990, launched on 4 August 1986, is the economic programmatic document. The official site says it “reflects the will of the National Council of the Revolution to build a national economy through the development and support of its own productive sectors”, under the watchword comptons sur nos propres forces, “let us count on our own strength”, with an important role for the CDRs and “vigilant management of external debt” (Five-Year Plan). The major works listed in the chronology include the Sourou valley irrigation scheme, targeting 16,000 irrigated hectares, the “Bataille du Rail” to extend the Abidjan to Ouagadougou line northwards with popular participation, the Kompienga dam, and collective fields (chronology).

Industrialisation was treated as a question of mastering technology rather than importing finished factories. At Dédougou in 1987 Sankara made the point in French: C’est la petite bourgeoisie qui nous a amené les usines clés en mains. Toutes les usines clés en mains ont échoué parce que nous ne maîtrisons pas l’outil. “It is the petty bourgeoisie that brought us turnkey factories. All the turnkey factories failed because we did not master the tool.” Such factories bring technical assistance that never leaves, he said, because debt forces new loans to pay old ones (Dédougou speech). The Poura gold mine, inaugurated on 18 October 1984, was the emblem of the policy, and the official site reproduces Sankara’s speech “For whom does the gold of Poura shine?” (Poura speech). Food self-sufficiency was treated as a binding objective in both the UN speech and the land ordinance’s preamble (UN speech; ordonnance).

The environmental programme is well documented because Sankara’s Paris speech of 5 February 1986 survives in English. “Since 1973 they have seen the environment deteriorate, trees die, and the desert invade with giant strides”, he said, estimating that the Sahel desert advanced at seven kilometres a year (Paris speech). The measures he reported were concrete. Villages planted trees at public occasions such as marriages, baptisms, award presentations and visits by prominent people. Schoolchildren in Ouagadougou built 3,500 improved cookstoves for their mothers in early 1986, and women built 80,000 over two years. Tree planting was a condition of occupying public housing, enforced by the CDRs with evictions for non-compliance. The capital’s twenty districts without drinking water received 150 wells. Ten million trees were planted under a fifteen-month programme, with the rule that each family in the villages and river valleys plant a hundred trees a year. The firewood trade was regulated with merchant cards, designated cutting zones and compulsory replanting. Arson cases went before the Popular Courts of Conciliation, with tree planting as a sanction. Between February and March 1986, 35,000 peasants and cooperative officials were trained in economic management and environmental organisation, and a “Popular Harvest of Forest Seeds” supplied 7,000 village nurseries (Paris speech).

The “three struggles”, launched on 2 April 1985, were against destructive woodcutting, bush fires and the roaming of animals, or in the original, lutte contre la coupe abusive du bois, contre les feux de brousse et contre la divagation des animaux (chronology). The 1985 anniversary speech called for a “green Burkina”, a grove in every village, kitchen gardens for every household and whitewashed towns (4 August 1985 speech). Sankara’s theoretical claim was the most radical part: “This struggle to defend the trees and Forests is above all a struggle against imperialism. Because imperialism is the arsonist setting fire to our forests and savannas.” He compared the bee, the architect and the revolutionary architect, arguing that people should not merely collect what nature offers but deliberately remake their conditions (Paris speech). Guindé Bonzi’s Souvenirs de la Révolution, a memoir by a schoolboy during the period, recalls that CDRs sometimes killed roaming animals for food rather than impounding them, a reminder that environmental rules could be applied harshly at local level (review of Bonzi’s memoir).

War with Mali and foreign policy#

The chronology records a border clash with Mali in December 1974 over a strip of land, and the outbreak of the “Christmas War” on 25 December 1985, a ceasefire on 30 December, a peace agreement in January 1986 and an International Court of Justice ruling in December 1986 that both sides accepted (chronology). A September 1983 entry records an earlier attempt to submit the dispute to the court. The movement’s policy summary reads the 1985 war as an attempt to divert popular discontent by stirring nationalism against Malians, “without success”, and records Sankara’s subsequent release of detained trade unionists (policy summary). Pathfinder’s collection lists a solidarity rally of 3 January 1986 under the title “Malian soldiers are no longer prisoners, they are our brothers”, and the chronology records the release of all Malian prisoners of war in February (Pathfinder table of contents; chronology).

Sankara’s relations with France were confrontational but framed as friendship. In January 1984 he said that “every form of neocolonialism, imperialism, and paternalism is excluded from this type of relationship” and that “our dignity must be respected, as well as our sovereignty”. He criticised Mitterrand’s France for maintaining relations with apartheid South Africa and sending troops to Chad, while calling the criticism “an act of friendship, in all honesty” (interview). The chronology records the rejection of the France-Afrique summits, the blocking of transfers to France over the Kompienga financing and Mitterrand’s visit in November 1986 (chronology). In 1987 Sankara told interviewers that “we have to have the courage to condemn imperialism and to show in the clearest and simplest manner … that imperialism exists, that it is trying to exploit us, to manipulate us, to use us” (Reykjavik interview, 13 January 1987). Peterson’s biography treats this defiance as central to Sankara’s appeal and to the hostility he provoked, inside Burkina Faso and beyond (Indiana University Press).

Cuba signed scientific, economic and technical cooperation agreements in December 1983, and Sankara received the Order of José Martí in Havana in September 1984. The July 1986 Radio Havana interview praised “the clear-sightedness and the internationalist sense of Cuba’s commitment”, and shortly before his death he recorded Nous pouvons compter sur Cuba, “we can count on Cuba” (chronology; Radio Havana interview; Pathfinder contents). With Ghana there were joint military manoeuvres in 1983 and 1985, and at Harlem Sankara explained that they were meant to show imperialism what African countries could do together, displaying cloth printed “Ghana-Burkina Faso: same fight” (Harlem speech). Gaddafi visited in April 1984, although Sankara had disapproved of Gaddafi’s action in Chad in January (chronology).

Sankara used the UN, the OAU and the Non-Aligned Movement as platforms while refusing to pretend they were already revolutionary. His pan-African formula at the UN was “Africa for the Africans; Burkina Faso for the Burkinabè”, a rewriting of the Monroe doctrine, and he called for the veto to be abolished, South Africa expelled and Israel suspended (UN speech; policy summary). At Harare in 1986 he declared Selon nous, le monde est divisé en deux camps antagonistes: le camp des exploiteurs et le camp des exploités, “in our view the world is divided into two opposing camps, the camp of the exploiters and the camp of the exploited”, while defending non-alignment with the phrase On peut se faire assister sans se faire assujettir, “one can be helped without being subjugated” (Harare speech). At the Black Institute symposium in April 1986 he framed the task as a question: “What have Black people done, what can or must they still do, to take responsibility for their own history and in that way contribute to Universal civilization?” (Black Institute speech).

Debt and the Addis Ababa united front#

The speech to the OAU summit on 29 July 1987 is the high point of Sankara’s international politics, and the MIA preserves it in English (A United Front Against Debt). It calls debt the “second pillage” and argues that it is a continuation of colonialism by other means. “Debt’s origins come from colonialism’s origins. Those who lend us money are those who colonized us”, he said. “We had no connections with this debt. Therefore we cannot pay for it.” He played on the language of aid: “Debt is neo-colonialism, in which colonizers have transformed themselves into ‘technical assistants.’ We should rather say ‘technical assassins.’” And he set out the stark arithmetic: “if we don’t repay, lenders will not die. That is for sure. But if we repay, we are going to die. That is also for sure” (A United Front Against Debt).

The proposal was collective rather than unilateral, precisely to avoid the isolation of one refusing state. Sankara proposed an “Addis-Ababa Club” and a united front against debt, so that refusal would appear “not an aggressive move on our part, but a fraternal move to speak the truth”. He made his own position personal: “If Burkina Faso stands alone in refusing to pay, I will not be here for the next conference! But, with everyone’s support, which I need, with the support of everyone we would not have to pay” (A United Front Against Debt). The speech was also a disarmament speech: “each time an African country buys a weapon, it is against an African country”, and “I am a soldier and I carry a gun. But Mister President, I would want us to disarm.” He cited Castro, Mitterrand, Gro Harlem Brundtland and Félix Houphouët-Boigny as others who had said debt could not be repaid, a deliberate attempt to make the position non-sectarian (A United Front Against Debt).

15 October 1987 and its aftermath#

The MIA archive publishes a text Sankara prepared for a meeting of the OMR, the Revolutionary Military Organisation, on the evening of 15 October 1987. A note attached to it says the handwritten original exists, that the journalist Denis de Montgolfier found it, and that “this discourse disproves the Popular Front’s claim that Thomas Sankara was preparing to assassinate Blaise Compaoré and his companions” (The speech he would have given). The note is the archive’s claim, not an independent finding, but the text itself is an internal critique rather than a conspiracy theory. It diagnoses “a remarkable decline in enthusiasm for the revolution amongst activists”, “factionalism amongst our leadership”, and “the machinations of certain elements within our ranks” that had done more damage in months than struggles with the leftist opposition. It calls for open debate and criticism, for taking the divisions to the masses, for removing troublemakers, and for adopting at last the statutes and programme of the CNR and a Revolutionary Code of Ethics (speech prepared for 15 October 1987).

The official chronology’s last entry for the period is terse: “15/10/87. Assassinat de Thomas Sankara avec 7 autres collaborateurs,” meaning “15 October 1987. Assassination of Thomas Sankara with seven other colleagues.” (chronology). The movement’s site publishes a May 2015 dossier prepared in support of a request for a parliamentary inquiry, which documents the case being shuttled between civil and military jurisdictions before dismissal by the Supreme Court. On 18 June 2003 the UN Human Rights Committee declared the complaint admissible, and in April 2006 it found violations of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, calling on Burkina Faso to clarify the assassination, give the family impartial justice, correct the death certificate, disclose the place of burial, compensate the family and prevent a recurrence (2015 parliamentary dossier). The same dossier records letters from Burkinabè deputies to the French National Assembly in 2011 and 2012 and the family lawyer’s description of the reopening as resting on a single act, the order to prosecute (lawyer’s statement, March 2015). Bruno Jaffré’s chapter in the Murrey collection examines the killing as an unresolved political crime (Pluto Press).

After 27 years of rule, popular protests on 30 and 31 October 2014 drove Compaoré from power, as the 2015 dossier records. The transition authorities moved quickly. On 22 November 2014 transitional president Michel Kafando authorised examination of the presumed tomb with a view to DNA testing, and on 28 November Prime Minister Yacouba Isaac Zida announced that the file would be fully reopened and requested Compaoré’s extradition from Morocco (2015 dossier). Later invocations of Sankara are the words of later actors. The UNIR MPS declaration of December 2025 places its appeal for unity, civil military cohesion and sovereignty “in strict respect of the present institutional context”, and presents the revolution as a convergence of instinctive rejection of injustice, solid intellectual training, the decisive imprint of Madagascar, a tested sense of collective discipline and an unusual capacity to build civil military coherence (UNIR MPS declaration). Harsch’s retrospective essay asks why interest in Sankara has outlived the collapse of his revolution, and finds part of the answer in the memory of a leader who seemed to improve ordinary lives while speaking back to power (Review of African Political Economy, 2013).

The MIA archive lists twelve works by Sankara and reports on the assassination. Its English texts include the proclamation of 4 August 1983, the January 1984 interview, the Harlem and UN speeches of October 1984, the forest speech of February 1986, the Black Institute speech of April 1986, the Radio Havana interview of July 1986, the Reykjavik interview of January 1987, the OAU debt speech of July 1987 and the speech prepared for 15 October 1987 (archive index).
