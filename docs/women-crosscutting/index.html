Half the Sky? Women in the Socialist States and Movements

In 1920 Alexandra Kollontai, a Bolshevik and People's Commissar for Social Welfare, gave a name to a burden the revolution had promised to lift.

In 1920 Alexandra Kollontai, a Bolshevik and People’s Commissar for Social Welfare, gave a name to a burden the revolution had promised to lift. Capitalism, she wrote, had added wage work without removing the older loads: “Capitalism has placed a crushing burden on woman’s shoulders: it has made her a wage-worker without having reduced her cares as housekeeper or mother. Woman staggers beneath the weight of this triple load.” (Alexandra Kollontai, “Communism and the Family”, 1920; translated by Alix Holt). The “triple load” meant paid employment, housework and child-rearing. She proposed communal kitchens, laundries, creches and collective child-rearing, and pointed to the 1917 divorce decree as a gain for working women. This was a programme by a senior official with a polemical purpose, not a report on what had been built; the English is a 1977 translation.

Looking back in 1926, Kollontai recalled being the only woman in the first Bolshevik cabinet and the isolation she felt when she first pressed the party on the woman question. “I am also the first woman ever to have been appointed ambassadress, a post which I occupied for three years and from which I resigned of my own free will.” (Alexandra Kollontai, autobiography written 1926; translated by Salvator Attansio). She also remembered the six-week strike of women laundry workers in 1917. The autobiography was written nine years later and shaped as self-justification; its published text restores passages she crossed out in proof under what the prefatory note calls the gradually sharpening Stalinist control. The claim to be the first woman in a cabinet is her own.

The woman question before the party#

The woman question entered revolutionary writing before Marxism did. In 1919, working as a young journalist in Changsha, Mao Zedong treated a young woman’s suicide as a social fact. She had been trapped by three “iron nets”: Chinese society, her own family and her betrothed’s family. “It happened because of the shameful system of arranged marriages, because of the darkness of the social system, the negation of the individual will, and the absence of the freedom to choose one’s own mate.” (Mao Zedong, “Miss Chao’s Suicide,” 1919; translation, translator not credited on the page). Mao did not witness the suicide, gave no original venue, and used the event as an argument.

In Paris in 1922 the Vietnamese anti-colonial journalist Ho Chi Minh joined the question to empire: “Colonization is in itself an act of violence of the stronger against the weaker. This violence becomes still more odious when it is exercised upon women and children.” (Ho Chi Minh, “Annamese Women and French Domination,” 1 August 1922; translation, translator not credited on the page). He said he was recounting facts seen and described by witnesses, so he did not claim to have seen the atrocity he quoted. The article is polemical and speaks about Vietnamese women rather than recording their own organising. That difference between speaking for and speaking with runs through the record.

Labour as liberation#

In his 1927 investigation of the peasant movement in Hunan, Mao observed rural women forming associations and the “authority of the husband” weakening: “With the rise of the peasant movement, the women in many places have now begun to organize rural women’s associations; the opportunity has come for them to lift up their heads, and the authority of the husband is getting shakier every day.” (Mao Zedong, Hunan report, March 1927; translated in Selected Works). His explanation was material: poor peasant women had more say in family decisions because economic necessity forced them into manual labour. The report is advocacy as well as investigation, and this is an excerpt selected for the Little Red Book.

The productivist core was stated in 1955: “China’s women are a vast reserve of labour power. This reserve should be tapped in the struggle to build a great socialist country.” (Mao Zedong, introductory note, 1955; translated). A 1949 inscription for Women of New China urged women to take part in production and political activity, and the policy carried equal pay for equal work. These are notes and a magazine inscription, detached from their contexts: intention, not outcome.

M. Pichugina, identified on the title page as a member of the Supreme Soviet and chairman of the Tagansk District Soviet in Moscow, set out the Soviet case in 1939. Her booklet gave figures for employment, education and representation and told of her rise from collective farm and factory work. “The collective farm system has completely emancipated woman, in the full sense of the word.” (M. Pichugina, Women in the U.S.S.R., 1939; English translation, translator not credited in the text examined, OCR transcription). It presented the 1936 abortion ban as protection for mothers, with no trace of women who experienced it otherwise. This is a state publication with an overt propaganda purpose; the figures are unchecked and the autobiographical passage is too damaged in the scan to quote.

Promises and concessions#

In Cuba the claim was that women’s mass entry into production was central to the revolution. At a May Day parade in 1966 Fidel Castro said: “The women of our country are not only entering production work en masse, but are turning out to be workers of high efficiency” (Fidel Castro, May Day speech, 1 May 1966; English translation, OCR transcription). He also named what had to exist first: day nurseries, school dining halls and laundry services. At Santa Clara that December he gave the claim its form: “This Revolution has really been two revolutions for women; it has meant a double liberation as part of the exploited sector of the country, and, second, as women” (Fidel Castro, Santa Clara speech, 9 December 1966; English translation, OCR transcription). He conceded that he and other revolutionaries had been prejudiced and that a target of one million women in production by 1970 would not be met. Both are official self-assessments, translated from a scan with visible errors.

Linda Jenness, a US socialist who visited Cuba in early 1969, reported nurseries taking children from 45 days old, a hiring preference for women, and continuing male chauvinism in personal relations. Her sharpest observation concerned power: “while there is only one woman on the central committee of the Cuban Communist Party, over half the members of the Union of Young Communists (LUC) are women!” (Linda Jenness, reportage published 21 March 1969; OCR transcription, “(LUC)” as printed). Jenness wrote as an activist favourable to the revolution; her figures are unsourced, some claims are second-hand, and she said she was given two accounts of abortion eligibility and could not check the explanation.

A decade later the same tension appeared inside an official Soviet publication. Ada Baskina, a journalist writing for Novosti, opened her 1979 book with a question: “How to cope with her job as well as household tasks? How to see to the children’s upbringing and be a good worker?” (Ada Baskina, The Soviet Way of Life, 1979; English translation, translator not named in the text examined, OCR transcription). A caption conceded that married women still spent more time on housework than men, and one life story tells of a husband forbidding his wife to attend evening classes. Her four subjects were a deputy prime minister of Daghestan, a milkmaid turned scientist, a Tajik weaver and a Moldavian physicist; she reported that a third of Supreme Soviet deputies were women. Participation and power were not the same thing, and these women were chosen for a book about success. Novosti was an official foreign-propaganda publisher, and the interviews are mediated.

How historians have read it#

  • Barbara Evans Clements, Bolshevik Women (Cambridge University Press, 1997). Draws on a database of more than five hundred women and on biographical research to argue that women were important at the lower levels of the party before 1921; agrees with Kollontai on the significance of women’s organising and tests her claims against lives rather than programme texts. Full text borrow-restricted: y before 1921; agrees with Kollontai on the significance of women’s organising and tests her claims
  • Arlene Eisen, Women and Revolution in Viet Nam (Zed Books, 1984). Treats women as a distinct revolutionary constituency and asks why Vietnamese leaders mobilised them; the study needed to set beside the Ho Chi Minh article, since it supplies Vietnamese women’s voices. Full text borrow-restricted: ks why Vietnamese leaders mobilised them; the study needed to set beside the Ho Chi Minh article
  • Hilda Scott, Women and Socialism: Experiences from Eastern Europe (Alison & Busby, 1976; first published 1974 as Does Socialism Liberate Women?). Written from the author’s years in Czechoslovakia and research on employment, family and politics in the region; the standard starting point for the Eastern European dimension. Full text borrow-restricted: Czechoslovakia and research on employment
  • Yuhui Li, “Women’s Movement and Change of Women’s Status in China,” Journal of International Women’s Studies 1, no. 1 (2000). Uses income, occupation and education data to argue that change in women’s status over fifty years was “unstable and inconsistent”: real gains alongside persistent lower status. Broadly agrees with the productivist reading of Mao’s texts while qualifying claims of full equality. Source: vc.bridgew.edu
  • Carole Boyce Davies, Left of Karl Marx: The Political Life of Black Communist Claudia Jones (Duke University Press, 2008). Reads Jones’s journalism, political writing and prison poetry against her FBI file and argues that she expanded Marxism-Leninism to include gender and race; the bridge to Jones’s “triple exploitation” argument, which belongs to the same family of claims as Kollontai’s “triple load” but arises from a different political setting. Source: dukeupress.edu

Broad scholarly agreement holds that the state socialist regimes expanded women’s education, employment and legal rights, and that this was tied to labour mobilisation rather than to an independent feminist programme. Live disagreement concerns how far those gains altered household power and political representation, and whether the double burden was a transitional problem or a structural feature.

Limits of the record#

There is no first-hand account from an Eastern European woman in this material. Hilda Scott’s catalogue record confirms her book’s subject and method, but the full text is borrow-restricted, so nothing is quoted from it. The same applies to Arlene Eisen’s Vietnamese study. Two of the regions named at the start of this inquiry, Vietnam and Eastern Europe, are represented only by a man’s anti-colonial article and by historiography. The phrase “Women hold up half the sky” is widely attributed to Mao in 1968, but its exact wording, date and document could not be verified from a primary text; only the 1919, 1927, 1949 and 1955 texts are used here.

Language is a barrier. Chinese, Vietnamese, Russian and Spanish sources would need translation, and the English texts used here are themselves translations of uncertain provenance. Several survive only as scans with visible errors, and quotations were checked against digital text rather than the physical page. The testimony is elite and politically shaped: leaders, officials, sanctioned journalists and a sympathetic foreign visitor, with the ordinary woman appearing mainly through curated interviews. Comparative outcomes cannot be established from a 1919 Chinese newspaper article set beside a 1920 Soviet pamphlet, 1966 Cuban speeches and a 1979 Soviet interview collection. Proper treatment would require archive work in the University of Pittsburgh ephemera collection, the Novosti and Foreign Languages Publishing House imprints, the Vietnam Women’s Union archives, and Czechoslovak or East German women’s organisation records, along with oral histories that are not freely available online.
