The ABC Strikes and the Workers' Party: Brazil's Labour Road to the Presidency

On 18 April 1980 the metalworkers’ newspaper Movimento described the industrial town of São Bernardo do Campo in the middle of a general strike: “a cidade se transforma numa verdadeira praça de guerra, com quase 4 horas de escaramuças onde os operários, sem direção nenhuma, reagem à violência policial com pedradas.” (the city becomes a veritable square of war, with nearly four hours of skirmishes in which the workers, leaderless, answer police violence with stones, my rendering). The line appeared in Movimento no. 255 of 19–25 May 1980 and is reproduced in the Fundação Perseu Abramo’s chronology of the strike. Brazil was then under military rule, which banned strikes and supervised the unions. The action had begun on 1 April after weeks of mass meetings at Vila Euclides, the football stadium where workers from the ABC belt gathered. The ABC is the cluster of industrial municipalities around São Bernardo do Campo and São Paulo where the car and metal plants were concentrated. On 14 April a regional labour court declared the strike illegal, on 17 April the government intervened in the union and removed forty-two of its directors, and on 19 April police arrested the union president, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, and twelve other leaders without a judicial warrant. A march on 1 May was reported at about 100,000 people; the strike ended on 12 May and the prisoners were released on 20 May. The crowd figures come from the organizers and the police, not from independent measurement.

That ban on strikes is why the dispute reached beyond wages. Through the 1970s the military government held down real wages, a squeeze known as the arrocho, while an official union structure kept independent organizing out of the plants. In the ABC a current of organizers known as the new unionism worked around that structure through factory assemblies and a popular newspaper, ABCD Jornal, founded in 1975. Its surviving 1977 cover tells workers not to work overtime, not to accept shift changes and not to trust the bulletin of the intervenor, the official appointed to run the union. The strike wave began with a stoppage at the Scania truck plant on 12 May 1978 and grew through 1979 into the mass meetings at Vila Euclides. A photographic exhibition drawn from those years records Lula speaking from tables in the rain while the crowd repeated his words back through the stadium, a caption written decades later rather than a contemporary report. The historian John D. French argues, from police reports, oral histories and newspaper reporting, that Lula’s authority was made in those assemblies rather than brought to them, rejecting both the great-man account and older Marxist accounts that treated him as merely an expression of the ranks. Attendance at Vila Euclides was estimated by the police and the union, not counted.

The step from strike to party was stated plainly by Lélia Abramo, an actor and trade unionist who was among the first to sign the founding documents. Recalling the strikes some twenty years later, she said: “No decorrer das memoráveis greves de 1978-80, todos os que participavam do movimento começaram a perceber a necessidade de sair das lutas puramente reivindicatórias e estendê-las para o campo político.” (during the memorable strikes of 1978–80, all who took part came to see the need to leave purely wage demands and extend the struggle into politics, my rendering). This is retrospective testimony by a committed activist, published 24 April 2006 (Fundação Perseu Abramo). On 10 February 1980, at the Colégio Sion school in São Paulo, supporters gathered the 101 signatures required by law to form a party, with delegates from seventeen states. Raul Pont, Paulo Skromov and Paul Singer, interviewed in 1994, described how the Movimento Pró-PT added a letter to become the Partido dos Trabalhadores, the Workers’ Party, known as the PT. Singer recalled Mário Pedrosa telling the meeting: “Vocês vão deixar todas as suas bíblias fora. Vocês vão entrar no PT sem bíblia, sem dogmas. Nós vamos todos aprender juntos um novo bê-a-bá.” (leave your bibles outside, enter the PT without bibles or dogmas, and we will all learn a new alphabet together, my rendering). The words are Singer’s recollection of a speech, not a transcript (Mário Pedrosa at Colégio Sion, 10 February 1980, recalled by Paul Singer in a 1994 interview; Fundação Perseu Abramo).

The manifesto approved that day begins with the claim that the party answered a demand from below: “O Partido dos Trabalhadores surge da necessidade sentida por milhões de brasileiros de intervir na vida social e política do país para transformá-la.” (the Workers’ Party arises from the felt need of millions of Brazilians to intervene in the social and political life of the country in order to transform it, my rendering). It adds: “Somos um Partido dos Trabalhadores, não um partido para iludir os trabalhadores.” (we are a party of workers, not a party to deceive workers, my rendering). Both are from the founding manifesto, approved 10 February 1980 (Fundação Perseu Abramo). A programmatic text records what the founders said they wanted. It ties the party to the strike wave and insists that unions remain independent of political parties as well as of the state. At the founding Lula made the same point in his own words: “Começamos a construir um partido que defende a autonomia dos sindicatos, associações profissionais, movimentos populares, entidades representativas de setores da sociedade independentes do próprio partido e nos sentimos satisfeitos por este acontecimento.” (we begin to build a party that defends the autonomy of unions, professional associations, popular movements and representative bodies independent of the party itself, my rendering). The archive quotes this from the handwritten founding act of 1980, which has not been published in transcription; it dates the act to 1 June and says it replaced the act of 10 February (Lula, speech recorded in the founding act, 1980; Fundação Perseu Abramo).

Four years later Lula was speaking to a much larger crowd. On 16 April 1984 the Diretas Já campaign, which demanded direct presidential elections, held a rally in São Paulo’s Anhangabaú valley. A Folha de S.Paulo reporter who was there described the singing of the national anthem, the campaign’s yellow symbols, a large cardboard figure of Senator Teotônio Vilela and fireworks writing out “Diretas, já”. Lula was the most applauded speaker, and his answer to General Rubem Ludwig was one line: “Baderna é o Colégio Eleitoral.” (the Electoral College is a mob). “Colégio Eleitoral” means the indirect college that chose the president under the dictatorship (Lula at the Diretas Já rally, reported by Folha de S.Paulo, 17 April 1984; Folha archive). That single newspaper report gives five estimates of the crowd, from 800,000 to nearly two million, a disagreement that is itself part of the record. When the PT reached government, the language shifted. In a letter signed for the 2002 election Lula promised to respect contracts, keep the fiscal surplus and control inflation while calling for growth, jobs and agrarian reform: “Ninguém precisa me ensinar a importância do controle da inflação. Iniciei minha vida sindical indignado com o processo de corrosão do poder de comprar dos salários dos trabalhadores.” (nobody needs to teach me the importance of controlling inflation; I began my union life angry at the erosion of workers’ purchasing power, my rendering) (Lula, “Carta ao Povo Brasileiro”, 22 June 2002; Fundação Perseu Abramo). His inaugural address of 1 January 2003 put hunger and land reform at the centre of government and described the route there: “Quando olho a minha própria vida de retirante nordestino, de menino que vendia amendoim e laranja no cais de Santos, que se tornou torneiro mecânico e líder sindical, que um dia fundou o Partido dos Trabalhadores” (when I look at my own life as a migrant from the northeast, a boy selling peanuts and oranges on the docks of Santos, who became a lathe operator and union leader and one day founded the Workers’ Party, my rendering) (Lula, inaugural address, 1 January 2003; Fundação Perseu Abramo). A speech of this kind states a programme, not its results. The manifesto of 1980 and the assurances of 2002 both stand in the record, which does not decide whether the second was a conversion, a tactic or a consistent strategy. Governing also brought a corruption scandal, the Mensalão. José Dirceu, a former chief of staff convicted in the case, wrote after the verdict: “Fui prejulgado e linchado. Não tive, em meu benefício, a presunção de inocência.” (I was prejudged and lynched; I was not granted the presumption of innocence, my rendering) (José Dirceu, letter of 9 October 2012; Fundação Perseu Abramo). That is a defendant’s account rather than a finding of the court, and no court record is reproduced among these sources.

The movement that pressed Lula from outside the party had a different centre of gravity. On the night of 29 October 1985, families linked to the Movimento dos Trabalhadores Rurais Sem Terra (MST), the landless workers’ movement, occupied the Annoni estate. Isaías Vedovatto, one of the settlers, recalled two years of preparation, organization by municipality, a meeting at one in the morning and a convoy at two, encirclement by military police at dawn, shortages of water and a system of passwords. He begins with the act that made him responsible: “Fui eu que cortei a cerca da entrada da fazenda naquela noite.” (I was the one who cut the entrance fence that night, my rendering) (Isaías Vedovatto, testimony published 29 October 2019 about the occupation of 29 October 1985; MST). The account was recorded more than thirty years afterwards by the movement itself, and its later claims about the settlement’s output are not checked here. The same movement’s memory includes the killing of nineteen rural workers at Eldorado dos Carajás in 1996; statements gathered fifteen years later recorded that no one had been punished. In 2018, with Lula in prison, the MST national leadership wrote: “Também reafirmamos nossa convicção na inocência do Presidente Lula, defendemos seu direito de concorrer às eleições presidenciais e, diante desta prisão política resultado de um processo ilegal e ilegítimo, exigimos sua liberdade!” (we reaffirm our belief in President Lula’s innocence, defend his right to stand in the presidential elections and, in the face of this political imprisonment resulting from an illegal and illegitimate process, demand his freedom, my rendering) (MST national leadership, 12 May 2018; Fundação Perseu Abramo). The assertion that the imprisonment was illegal is the movement’s position, not something this document establishes.

How historians have read it#

  • John D. French, The Brazilian Workers’ ABC: Class Conflict and Alliances in Modern São Paulo (1992). A grass-roots social history of the ABC from 1900 to 1953 that challenges the view of Brazilian populism as simply demobilizing workers; it supplies the long background to the 1978–80 strikes. Source: openlibrary.org
  • Margaret E. Keck, The Workers’ Party and Democratization in Brazil (1992; paperback 1995). The first major study of the PT, based on written sources and extensive interviews, treating the party as both a product and a driver of democratization and stressing its links to the combative union movement and its unusually democratic internal structure. Source: openlibrary.org; broadly agrees with French on the labour roots of the party.
  • John D. French, “Charisma’s Birth from the Bottom Up: Lula, ABC’s Metalworkers’ Strikes and the Social History of Brazilian Politics” (2022). Uses DOPS reports, oral histories and newspaper reportage to argue that Lula’s charisma was created collectively at Vila Euclides and should not be conflated with populism; expressly revises earlier accounts, including those of Rainho and Bargas, Antunes and Durand Ponte, for minimizing the leader phenomenon. Cambridge University Press: E7E1D10F63BD31C81F190FD917D56267
  • John D. French, Lula and His Politics of Cunning (2020; Portuguese translation 2022). A political biography that insists on the first forty years of Lula’s life as the key to his later popularity; agrees with the article above on the importance of the strikes but develops the argument at book length. Source: openlibrary.org and Source: fpabramo.org.br
  • Venício A. de Lima, “O vocabulário do ‘mensalão’” (2013). Argues that media repetition built a selective vocabulary that fixed the Mensalão as a label for PT corruption; useful for the politics of representation, but it is a committed critique rather than a judicial finding, and should be read against the court record. Source: fpabramo.org.br

There is broad agreement on the PT’s origin in the new unionism and in democratization. Disagreement continues over how much Lula’s leadership explains the strikes, how to read the economic turn of 2002, and whether the Mensalão was a criminal case, a media construction or both.

Limits of the record#

Provenance is the main limitation. Most of the primary material survives through two movement institutions, the Fundação Perseu Abramo and the MST. Both preserve documents that would otherwise be hard to reach, but the first is the PT’s own foundation and the second is the movement whose cause its statements defend. Their summaries are not the documents themselves. The editions used here do not include the handwritten founding act in full, the separate party statute, the police records, the official publication of the manifesto of 21 October 1980, or the original issues of the newspapers and magazines quoted. The film ABC da Greve is not part of this evidence. The sources are in Portuguese, and every English rendering above is mine and is marked as such. The surviving voices are also uneven: founders, union officers and national leaders dominate, while the rank-and-file metalworkers, the families who ran the strike fund and the women who led the march of 8 May 1980 appear mainly through other people’s accounts. For the Mensalão there is a post-conviction defence but no court file, and for the presidency there are Lula’s own programme texts but no measure of what the policies achieved or of how beneficiaries experienced them. Anniversary statements and testimonies recorded decades later reshape memory, and their claims about later outcomes would need independent checks.
