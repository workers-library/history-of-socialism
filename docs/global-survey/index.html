Global Survey of the Socialist Camp

The Comintern, the Eastern Bloc, Yugoslavia, Albania, national liberation movements, and a table of socialist states past and present.

The communist movement that emerged from the First World War was never a single settled bloc. Its history can be read in the resolutions of its congresses and in the disputes that repeatedly divided it, from the founding of the Communist International in 1919 to the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. Three threads run through the record. The first is the attempt to build an international organisation capable of directing revolutionary strategy. The second is the argument about what socialism meant and how it could be reached. The third is the encounter between that argument and actually existing states, including the liberation movements that drew on the movement’s documents while adapting them to local conditions.

The Communist International#

The Communist International, also called the Comintern or Third International, was constituted in Moscow at its First Congress on 2 to 6 March 1919. Three events formed its background: the October Revolution of 1917, the revolutionary wave that swept Central Europe in 1918 and 1919, and the collapse of the Second International over the war credits of 1914. Its founding manifesto was signed by Christian Rakovsky, V. I. Lenin, G. E. Zinoviev, L. D. Trotsky and Fritz Platten. The manifesto defined the new body’s task as “to sum up the practical revolutionary experience of the working class, to cleanse the movement of its admixtures of opportunism and social patriotism, and to unite the forces of all the true revolutionary proletarian parties in order to further and hasten the complete victory of the communist revolution” (Manifesto of the First Congress of the Comintern, March 1919). The same document set out the internationalist premise that the national state had “become too narrow for the development of the productive forces”, that the proletariat must “transform the whole world into one cooperative commonwealth”, and that soviets, or workers’ councils, were the form of proletarian state power (loc. cit.).

The Second Congress of July to August 1920 is usually treated as the effective founding congress because it settled the organisational and colonial questions. Its Conditions of Admission to the Communist International, known as the Twenty-One Conditions, required that “every party that wishes to belong to the Communist International must bear the name Communist Party of this or that country (Section of the Communist International)”. Member parties had to expel “notorious opportunists” and support the Soviet republics through “legal or illegal propaganda” among troops sent against them (Minutes of the Second Congress, Seventh Session, 30 July 1920). Lenin’s Preliminary Draft Theses on National and Colonial Questions and the Congress of the Peoples of the East held at Baku in September 1920 established the proposition that the colonial and national question was a component part of the world proletarian revolution. Both the Chinese Communist Party and the Chinese Red Army later appealed to that thesis in their polemics (Comintern archive, Second Congress and Baku Congress).

The later world congresses recorded the shifting line of the International. The Third Congress of 22 June to 12 July 1921 adopted a “new course” of united-front tactics and partial retreat under the conditions of the New Economic Policy (report on the Third Congress). The Fourth Congress of November to December 1922 developed the idea of a united front “from below and from above” and addressed the German question and the fight against fascism (resolutions of the Fourth Congress). The Fifth Congress of June to July 1924 pushed the “Bolshevization” of member parties and the campaign against Trotskyism (Theses and Resolutions, Fifth Congress). The Sixth Congress of July to September 1928 adopted the programme associated with the “Third Period” and the colonial theses, and sharpened the polemic against “social fascism” (programme of the Sixth Congress; colonial theses). The Seventh Congress of 25 July to 20 August 1935 heard Georgi Dimitrov’s report on fascism and working-class unity and turned the International toward the popular front (archive of the Seventh Congress).

Historians have disagreed about how much freedom those congresses actually possessed. Kevin McDermott and Jeremy Agnew, in their history of the Comintern, argue that “strategy was defined in Moscow, but tactics, to a certain extent, could be elaborated on the ground by the parties themselves”, and they caution against describing the early years simply as a Russian dictatorship, noting both a drift toward bureaucratisation and genuine “communist pluralism” at the early congresses (McDermott and Agnew, The Comintern: A History of International Communism from Lenin to Stalin, Macmillan, 1996). Other historians, among them Jürgen Rojahn, treat the changing relationship between the Soviet party and the rest of the International as the crucial issue in its history (loc. cit.). The documents themselves record both central directives and minority statements, and later movements chose different congresses as the point at which the International had either “degenerated” or “correctly adapted” to new conditions.

Internal debate was part of the movement’s constitution rather than an accident. Trotsky’s The Third International After Lenin of 1928 was a book-length critique of the Sixth Congress draft programme. It argued that the “social fascism” line and the theory of “socialism in one country” departed from Lenin’s perspectives (Trotsky, The Draft Program of the Communist International: A Criticism of Fundamentals). The Italian delegation around Amadeo Bordiga and the “Italian Left” opposed the united-front and Bolshevization turns from a different standpoint, and the archive preserves both the majority resolutions and the minority statements (Comintern documents, 1923 to 1928, volume 2).

The Comintern was dissolved in May and June 1943. The Presidium of the Executive Committee proposed dissolution on 15 May 1943, “freeing the sections of the Communist International from their obligations arising from the statutes and resolutions of the congresses of the Communist International”, on the grounds that the “general national upsurge and mobilization of the people” could be “best of all and most fruitfully carried out by the vanguard of the working-class movement of each separate country, working within the framework of its own country”. The statement of 8 June, effective 10 June, recorded that “as from the 10th of June, 1943, the Executive Committee of the Communist International, the Presidium and Secretariat of the Executive Committee, as well as the International Control Commission, have been dissolved” (The Communist International, 1919 to 1943: Documents, volume 3).

The official explanation was that the Allied war against fascism and the strategy of national united fronts made a central international unnecessary. The Fourth International’s manifesto of July 1943 denounced the procedure as bureaucratic. It said “the so-called Central Committees hand-picked by Stalin and his Presidium” had approved the change, while “the membership was not even consulted” (Fourth International, Manifesto on the Dissolution of the Comintern, July 1943). That division of interpretation, between adaptation to a new situation and the liquidation of world revolutionary independence, lasted for the rest of the century. The complaint about the membership also matters for social history: the dispute was conducted by tiny committees, and ordinary party members were not asked to decide it.

The postwar movement and the Cominform#

After 1943 coordination passed to informal Soviet leadership, bilateral party relations, periodic international meetings and the Communist Information Bureau, or Cominform, established in 1947. Its founding declaration set the task of defending “peace and socialism” against the emerging Western bloc. Its first major act was the resolution of 28 June 1948, “On the Situation in the Communist Party of Yugoslavia”, which expelled the Communist Party of Yugoslavia from the brotherhood of communist parties and charged its leadership with “revisionism”, nationalism and deviation from the “general line”. The resolution of 1949, “The Communist Party of Yugoslavia in the Power of Murderers and Spies”, escalated the charge into an indictment of a police state and espionage (Cominform resolutions reproduced in The Workers’ Advocate, Vol. 7 No. 6, 1 November 1977).

The Yugoslav Central Committee answered the 1948 resolution. The International Secretariat of the Fourth International recorded that reply and observed that the Yugoslav party’s case “has been judged by the leaderships of the various Communist parties and the Cominform on the basis of unilateral accusations brought against it by the Russian Communist Party” (Fourth International, International Secretariat circular, 30 June 1948). This was a contemporary judgement from a rival revolutionary current, not a neutral one, but it is evidence for how the split was argued at the time.

De-Stalinization and the Sino-Soviet split#

The Twentieth Congress of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union in February 1956 and Nikita Khrushchev’s report on the “cult of personality” opened the second great division (Khrushchev’s report to the Twentieth Congress, 24 February 1956). The Chinese leadership answered with On the Historical Experience of the Dictatorship of the Proletariat in April 1956 and More on the Historical Experience of the Dictatorship of the Proletariat in December 1956. These texts defended Stalin’s historic role while criticising “certain mistakes” and, crucially, objecting to the way the Soviet leadership imposed a reassessment on fraternal parties (Sino-Soviet Split document archive). Enver Hoxha’s Albanian Party of Labour went further. It rejected “the revisionist theses of the XX Congress” at the plenum of its Central Committee in September 1960 and at the Moscow meeting itself (Hoxha, “Reject the Revisionist Theses of the XX Congress”, November 1960).

Two collective documents mark the high point of postwar unity. The Declaration of the Twelve Communist and Workers’ Parties of the Socialist Countries was issued in Moscow in 1957, and the Statement of the 81 Communist and Workers’ Parties followed in Moscow in November 1960 (1957 Declaration; 1960 Statement). The 1960 Statement defined the era as “a time of struggle between the two opposing social systems, a time of socialist revolutions and national-liberation revolutions”. It described “the world socialist system” as “becoming the decisive factor in the development of society” and insisted that “world war can be prevented by the joint efforts of the world socialist camp, the international working class, the national-liberation movement, all the countries opposing war and all peace-loving forces”. The same document asserted that “the restoration of capitalism has been made socially and economically impossible” in the socialist countries (loc. cit.). Later events turned that claim into the central object of Marxist contention.

By 1960 to 1963 the unity of those documents had broken down. The Chinese Communist Party’s open letter of 14 June 1963, A Proposal Concerning the General Line of the International Communist Movement, argued that “a number of major differences of principle now exist in the international communist movement”. It proposed the line: “Workers of all countries, unite; workers of the world, unite with the oppressed peoples and oppressed nations; oppose imperialism and reaction in all countries; strive for world peace, national liberation, people’s democracy and socialism; consolidate and expand the socialist camp; bring the proletarian world revolution step by step to complete victory” (CPC Proposal, 14 June 1963). The proposal rejected the reduction of the movement’s line to “peaceful coexistence, peaceful competition, and peaceful transition” (peaceful coexistence being the doctrine that states with different social systems should avoid war and compete rather than confront each other) as a violation of “the revolutionary principles of the 1957 Declaration and the 1960 Statement”. It conceded that “Communists would always prefer to bring about the transition to socialism by peaceful means”, but held that making peaceful transition “a new world-wide strategic principle” was impermissible because “there is no historical precedent for peaceful transition from capitalism to socialism” (loc. cit.).

The Soviet Central Committee’s Open Letter of 14 July 1963 denied the charge. It said “the controversy centres on issues affecting the vital interests of the peoples”, including “war and peace”, “the question of the role and development of the world socialist system”, “the struggle against the ideology and practice of the personality cult”, and “the strategy and tactics of the world labour movement and the national-liberation struggle”. It defended the Twentieth Congress conclusion that “in our times there is no fatal inevitability of war between states” and described the Chinese position on thermonuclear war as “adventuristic” and “fundamentally contrary to the ideas of Marxism-Leninism” (CPSU Open Letter, 14 July 1963).

The Chinese side replied with a series of “Comments”, including On the Question of Stalin of 13 September 1963 and Is Yugoslavia a Socialist Country? of 26 September 1963, and the Soviet side answered with Reply to Peking and Pravda editorials (Sino-Soviet Split document index). By 1969 the dispute had moved from polemics to armed border clashes. The archive records how the Chinese came to call the Soviet leadership “revisionists” and “social-imperialists”, “socialist in words, imperialists in deeds”, and how the Soviet side called the Chinese “splittists” and “left-wing adventurists” (loc. cit.).

Other parties took their own positions. The Romanian Workers’ Party issued a Statement on the Sino-Soviet Dispute in April 1964 defending the right of each party to autonomy (Romanian statement of 22 April 1964). The Korean Workers’ Party called for a policy of “Thwart the Manoeuvres to Split the International Communist Movement” in April 1964 (Korean Workers’ Party statement). A section of the Belgian party passed “Marxists-Leninists, Unite!” in 1963 (Brussels Federal Committee resolution). Albania aligned with the Chinese Communist Party against Khrushchev and then broke with the Chinese after Mao’s death. Each side claimed to be defending “the unity of the international communist movement” on the basis of a different definition of its principles.

Historians have not treated these claims as equally well supported. Lorenz Lüthi, using archival and documentary evidence from three continents, argues that disputes over Marxist-Leninist ideology played the primary role in the split, while showing how those disputes were linked to Chinese domestic politics and to Mao’s struggles with internal rivals (Lüthi, The Sino-Soviet Split: Cold War in the Communist World, Princeton University Press, 2008; Wilson Center discussion of the book). Other historians, such as Donald Zagoria in an earlier study, combined ideological with historical, personal, economic and political causes; later scholars have variously stressed security concerns and national interest (loc. cit.). The party polemics remain evidence of what each leadership said. They are not, by themselves, a settled account of why the split happened.

Eurocommunism#

A third current developed in the Western European parties, with roots in Palmiro Togliatti’s idea of an “Italian road”. In 1956 Togliatti wrote that “parliament, in the past the instrument for the organisation and consolidation of the capitalist regime, can today become an effective instrument in the hands of the parties which aim at a socialist transformation of society” (Togliatti, “Parliament and the Struggle for Socialism”, 1956). His article Diversity and Unity in the International Proletarian Communist Movement of 1961 and his Memorandum of 1964 argued for “polycentrism”, meaning that the movement had more than one centre and that each party should find its own road while retaining common aims (Togliatti archive; Togliatti Memorandum, 1964).

The Chinese polemic The Differences Between Comrade Togliatti and Us of 31 December 1962 treated this as a form of “modern revisionism” (CPC editorial). Hoxha devoted a book, Eurocommunism is Anti-Communism, to the same thesis in 1980 (Hoxha, Eurocommunism is Anti-Communism). In Imperialism and the Revolution of 1978 he described Enrico Berlinguer’s Italian Communist Party as “an utterly degenerate party ideologically, with a completely reformist, parliamentarist, social-democratic program”, and attacked “the Spanish revisionist party of Ibarruri-Carrillo” for playing a “fire brigade role” in defence of the bourgeois order after Franco (Hoxha, Imperialism and the Revolution, Part One, chapter III). These texts set out one side of an argument whose other side is found in the documents of the Italian, French and Spanish parties in the 1970s.

Comecon and the Warsaw Pact#

The term “socialist camp” designated the community of states ruled by communist and workers’ parties. The Chinese proposal of 1963 enumerated thirteen such states at that date: “Albania, Bulgaria, China, Cuba, Czechoslovakia, the German Democratic Republic, Hungary, the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea, Mongolia, Poland, Rumania, the Soviet Union and the Democratic Republic of Vietnam”. It defined proletarian internationalism, under the new conditions of a camp, as “whether or not it resolutely defends the whole of the socialist camp, whether or not it defends the unity of all the countries in the camp on the basis of Marxism-Leninism” (CPC Proposal, 1963). The 1960 Statement described the camp as “a social, economic and political community of free and sovereign peoples united by the close bonds of international socialist solidarity”. Its economic life required “the economic laws of socialism”, coordinated national plans and an “international division of labor” based on “voluntary participation, mutual benefit” (1960 Statement).

Two institutions gave the camp a formal expression. Comecon, the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, also known by its Russian initials CMEA, was founded in Moscow in January 1949 by the Soviet Union, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Poland and Romania. The German Democratic Republic, Mongolia, Cuba and Vietnam joined later. Its purpose was the coordination of national economic plans, specialisation and cooperation in production, and what the movement called the “socialist international division of labour”. The 1960 Statement described this as “continuous improvement of the system of international division of labor through the co-ordination of national economic plans, specialization and co-operation in production within the world socialist system on the basis of voluntary participation, mutual benefit” (Statement of 81 Parties, 1960). From the Albanian standpoint, Comecon became one of the “economic, political and military blocs” whose internal “contradictions, frictions, contests and quarrels” were “manifesting themselves ever more openly” (Hoxha, Imperialism and the Revolution).

The Warsaw Treaty Organisation was established by the Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Mutual Assistance signed at Warsaw on 14 May 1955 by Albania, Bulgaria, Hungary, the German Democratic Republic, Poland, Romania, the Soviet Union and Czechoslovakia. Its preamble gave the socialist states’ own rationale: the “ratification of the Paris Agreements, which provide for the constitution of a new military group in the form of a ‘West European Union’, with the participation of a remilitarized West Germany and its inclusion in the North Atlantic bloc, thereby increasing the danger of a new war”. Article 4 provided for mutual assistance “in the exercise of the right of individual or collective self-defence, in accordance with Article 51 of the United Nations Charter”. Article 5 established a “Unified Command”. Article 9 permitted the accession of “other States, irrespective of their social and political structure”, and Article 11 fixed the treaty’s duration at twenty years (treaty text reproduced by the CVCE academic archives portal). The 1960 Statement described NATO, CENTO and SEATO as “military blocs” “spearheaded first and foremost against the socialist countries” (1960 Statement). The Warsaw organisation was dissolved in 1991.

The Cold War setting is visible in the documents themselves, in the Warsaw Pact preamble, in the 1960 Statement’s description of the Western alliances, and in the repeated claim that “the principal characteristic of our time” was the existence of a world socialist system (loc. cit.). McDermott and Agnew’s history warns against reading the whole period as simple Soviet domination; their account allows both for central control of strategy and for room for manoeuvre by individual parties, while recording the drift toward bureaucratisation that the Fourth International had already denounced in 1943 (McDermott and Agnew, 1996).

Four Marxist explanations of the Soviet collapse#

The years 1989 to 1991 saw the unraveling of the bloc. The Warsaw Pact was dissolved in 1991 and the Soviet Union itself ceased to exist in December 1991. Marxists explained the collapse through the same divisions that had preceded it. Four readings can be distinguished, but they do not have equal support, and the difference matters.

The anti-revisionist reading, associated with Hoxha’s Albanian party and its allies, held that capitalism had been restored through revisionism before the state finally broke. Hoxha’s analysis of 1978 already described the Soviet and East European countries as “all … suffering from the ills of capitalism, from the quarrels and conflicts over power and privileges in the ranks of the leaders and the upper strata”, and characterised Soviet foreign policy as “social-imperialism” (Hoxha, Imperialism and the Revolution). On this view the Twentieth Congress, Khrushchev’s “peaceful coexistence” and the weakening of the dictatorship of the proletariat prepared the restoration, and the events of 1989 to 1991 made explicit what had already occurred in social relations. Hoxha’s The Khruschevites of 1980 narrates this as a staged rupture, with chapters titled “The Official Proclamation of Revisionism”, “Designing the Empire” and “The Final Act” (contents of The Khruschevites). This remains a partisan, party-defined interpretation rather than a position with broad scholarly acceptance.

The Chinese post-Mao reading identified the Soviet Union as “social-imperialism” and the United States as the other superpower, and subordinated class analysis to an international front against Soviet expansion. The “three worlds” thesis associated with Mao and set out by Deng Xiaoping at the United Nations on 10 April 1974 divided the world into the two nuclear superpowers, the developed industrial countries, and the exploited countries of Asia, Africa and Latin America (New York Times, “Excerpts From Chinese Address to U.N. Session on Raw Materials”, 12 April 1974). Hoxha’s polemic against “The Theory of the ‘Three Worlds’: A Counter-revolutionary Chauvinist Theory” reported that “the Chinese revisionists say that there is only one contradiction in the world today, the contradiction between the two superpowers”, but that is the Albanian critique, not a neutral statement of the theory (Hoxha, Imperialism and the Revolution, Part Two, chapter I). Like the first reading, this was a state-influenced interpretation, not a body of independent scholarship.

The Trotskyist reading rests on a longer intellectual tradition. Trotsky’s The Revolution Betrayed of 1936 asked “What is the Soviet Union and Where is it Going?” and analysed a “dual character of the workers’ state”, “the Soviet Thermidor” and “the growth of inequality and social antagonisms”. It considered the claim of “state capitalism” and concluded that “the question of the character of the Soviet Union is not yet decided by history” (Trotsky, The Revolution Betrayed). The Fourth International’s “state capitalist” current developed a more thoroughgoing thesis in 1951 (Fourth International, “State Capitalism in the USSR”, 1951). On these readings, 1991 confirmed the need for political revolution against a bureaucratic stratum rather than the defence of the existing state. This is a genuine theoretical current with an extensive literature, but it remains a minority position among historians of the Soviet Union.

The reformist and Eurocommunist reading treated the collapse as the failure of an over-centralised, militarised, administratively planned model. Togliatti’s Memorandum of 1964 argued that “the concrete forms and conditions for the advance and victory of Socialism will today and in the immediate future be very different from those of the past”, and that “the unity one ought to establish and maintain lies in the diversity and full autonomy of the individual countries” (Togliatti Memorandum). It read 1989 to 1991 as an argument for a democratic, pluralist socialism and against the model that had collapsed. This interpretation has the broadest support among academic historians, though they arrive at it from different starting points. Vladislav Zubok’s Collapse: The Fall of the Soviet Union of 2021 places economic factors at the centre and shows how Russian political leaders themselves dismantled the union; Serhii Plokhy’s The Last Empire: The Final Days of the Soviet Union of 2014 traces the political and national disintegration through the final year (National Security Archive, “The End of Soviet Union 1991”, Briefing Book No. 783, 21 December 2021).

The documentary record supports that emphasis on political and economic breakdown rather than on either Marxist polemic. The attempted coup of August 1991 humiliated Gorbachev, discredited the security organs and made Boris Yeltsin a hero of resistance; after it failed, republican leaders had tasted sovereignty and were unwilling to return to a strong centre. Gorbachev’s proposed Union Treaty, which would have created a decentralised federation with a common economic space, was never signed. Yeltsin’s unilateral economic programme of 28 October 1991 and the Ukrainian referendum of 1 December, in which 92.26 percent of valid votes were cast for independence, destroyed what remained. On 8 December the leaders of Russia, Ukraine and Belarus signed the Belovezhie agreement dissolving the Soviet Union (loc. cit.). These were decisions by named political actors, taken in a crisis, and ordinary citizens took part too: in the photographs of Yeltsin standing on a tank in Moscow, in the crowds that resisted the coup, and in the electorate that voted on independence. That is documented political action, not a retrospective legend about inevitable decline.

The Eastern European socialist states#

The states of the Soviet bloc in Eastern Europe shared a common pattern: the destruction of the old ruling classes, nationalisation of the commanding heights of the economy, collectivisation of agriculture, one-party rule by a communist or workers’ party, planned economies, and membership in Comecon and the Warsaw Pact. Each also had a distinct party history and road to power. The 1960 Statement listed Albania, Bulgaria, Hungary, the German Democratic Republic, Poland, Romania and Czechoslovakia alongside the Soviet Union and the Asian socialist states as the “socialist camp” (1960 Statement).

In the German Democratic Republic the Socialist Unity Party of Germany, known by its German initials SED, was formed in April 1946 through the forced merger of the Communist and Social Democratic parties in the Soviet occupation zone. Wilhelm Pieck, a veteran of the Comintern, was a central figure, and his texts of 1948 belong to the founding documentation of the state (Pieck archive). Walter Ulbricht, who had worked in Moscow during the war and returned with the “ultra-left” communist group, led the SED from 1950, first as General Secretary and later as First Secretary, and became Chairman of the Council of State in 1960 (Ulbricht archive). The GDR was proclaimed on 7 October 1949. Soviet forces crushed the workers’ uprising of 17 June 1953, the East German government erected the Berlin Wall in August 1961, Erich Honecker replaced Ulbricht as party leader in 1971, and a new constitution was adopted in 1974. SED rule ended in the autumn of 1989 and German reunification followed on 3 October 1990. The 81 parties had called the GDR “the outpost of socialism in Western Europe and the true expression of the peace aspirations of the German nation” (1960 Statement). That official judgement should be set beside the 1953 uprising and the exodus through Berlin, since both are part of the same history.

In Poland the Polish Workers’ Party, or PPR, was formed in 1942 and led the partisan and underground struggle. Its postwar leader Władysław Gomułka initially held the line of a specifically Polish road before being displaced by Bolesław Bierut. In December 1948 the PPR merged with the Polish Socialist Party to form the Polish United Workers’ Party, or PZPR, which ruled until 1989. The “Polish October” of 1956 brought Gomułka back with a promise of “the Polish road to socialism” and some autonomy from Moscow, part of the same crisis of Soviet authority addressed in the Chinese and Albanian polemics (Hoxha, The Khruschevites, especially chapter 4, “The Touch-Stone”). Edward Gierek succeeded Gomułka in 1970. The rise of Solidarity in 1980 and the martial-law period under Wojciech Jaruzelski led to the Round Table agreements and the PZPR’s electoral defeat in June 1989, which made Poland the first communist government in the bloc to be replaced through elections. Solidarity was a mass movement of workers and their families, not only a party manoeuvre, and its shipyard activists and local organisers gave the crisis a social base that the documents of the parties alone cannot show.

In Czechoslovakia the Communist Party, known by its Czech initials KSČ, was founded in 1921 and represented at the Comintern by Klement Gottwald, who delivered closing remarks in the discussion on economic struggles at the Twelfth Plenum in 1932 (Comintern archive). After the February events of 1948 the party monopolised power. Gottwald was president from 1948 to 1953, succeeded by Antonín Zápotocký and Antonín Novotný, and a Chinese delegation addressed the party’s Twelfth Congress in 1962 (CPC statement to the Twelfth KSČ Congress, 8 December 1962). In 1968 Alexander Dubček led the “Prague Spring”, a programme of “socialism with a human face” that Hoxha listed among the “bourgeois and revisionist theories” that had “been shaken to their foundations” (Hoxha, Imperialism and the Revolution). The Warsaw Pact invasion of 21 August 1968 was justified by the Brezhnev Doctrine of limited sovereignty, under which the Soviet Union claimed the right to intervene in any socialist country where it judged the party’s rule threatened. “Normalization” under Gustáv Husák followed until the Velvet Revolution of 1989.

In Hungary the Communist Party, or MKP, was founded in 1918 and briefly held power in the Hungarian Soviet Republic of March to August 1919, one of the earliest attempts at proletarian state power outside Russia. After 1945 the party, led by Mátyás Rákosi, consolidated power through the Hungarian Working People’s Party. The revolution of 23 October to 4 November 1956 was the most dramatic internal crisis of the bloc before 1968. Soviet intervention and the installation of János Kádár’s Hungarian Socialist Workers’ Party restored bloc discipline. The anti-revisionist press of the 1970s accused Tito’s Yugoslavia of having been “the main organizer of the 1956 counter-revolution in Hungary”, a charge that shows how deeply the Hungarian events were read through the Tito split (The Workers’ Advocate, 1977). Kádár’s later “goulash communism” combined planning with market incentives and consumer concessions, and the Hungarian Socialist Workers’ Party dissolved itself in 1989. The 1956 revolution involved students, soldiers and workers fighting in Budapest and in the provinces, and the numbers of dead and imprisoned remain part of the argument about what Soviet intervention meant.

In Romania the Communist Party, or PCR, founded in 1921, grew under Gheorghe Gheorghiu-Dej during the war and afterwards. The Romanian People’s Republic was proclaimed on 30 December 1947. Romania’s distinctive feature was a relatively autonomous foreign policy. The Statement of the Romanian Workers’ Party on the Sino-Soviet Dispute of 22 April 1964 asserted the right of each party to its own line, refused to take sides in the Sino-Soviet polemic, and is a primary document of that independence (RWP Statement). Under Nicolae Ceaușescu, who succeeded Dej in 1965, Romania condemned the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia, developed relations with both China and the West, and pursued a highly centralised “systematization” programme that involved the demolition and rebuilding of villages and urban districts. The regime was overthrown in the revolution of December 1989 and Ceaușescu was executed on 25 December.

In Bulgaria the Communist Party, or BCP, founded in 1921, was led during its Comintern years by Georgi Dimitrov, whose 1935 report to the Seventh Congress on fascism and working-class unity was one of the International’s defining texts (Dimitrov at the Seventh Congress). After the Soviet advance of September 1944 the party took power, the monarchy was abolished and a people’s republic proclaimed on 15 September 1946, with Dimitrov as prime minister until his death in 1949. Valko Chervenkov followed, and Todor Zhivkov led party and state from 1954 until 10 November 1989. Bulgaria was among the most consistently pro-Soviet members of the bloc. A 1971 constitution declared it a “socialist state” and the BCP the “leading force of society”, and Zhivkov’s removal opened the transition to multiparty politics.

Yugoslavia and Albania#

Yugoslavia and Albania both broke with the Soviet-led bloc, but from opposite directions. Yugoslavia moved toward workers’ self-management and non-alignment. Albania moved toward a stricter anti-revisionism. Their divergence shows how the movement split into rival definitions of principle.

The Communist Party of Yugoslavia, or KPJ, was founded in 1919 and reorganised underground under Josip Broz Tito, who became General Secretary in 1937. After the Axis invasion of 1941 it led the Partisan movement, and the Anti-Fascist Council for the National Liberation of Yugoslavia proclaimed a federal structure in November 1943. The Federal People’s Republic of Yugoslavia was established in 1945 and renamed the Socialist Federal Republic in 1963. The Cominform resolution of 28 June 1948 accused the party leadership of “revisionism”, “nationalism” and deviations from the “general line” and expelled it; the resolution of 1949 escalated to the language of “murderers and spies” and an “anti-communist police state” (Cominform resolutions). Inside Yugoslavia the “Cominformist” opposition was suppressed and party cadres who had sided with Moscow were expelled or imprisoned. Tito’s independent course, accepting Western credits and military aid while retaining one-party rule and social ownership of property, became the archetype of “revisionism” for the Soviet leadership after 1955 to 1956 and for the anti-revisionist current throughout. The Albanian study Yugoslav “Self-Administration”: Capitalist Theory and Practice of 1978 opens with “A Brief Excursion on the History of the Titoite Revisionists” (Hoxha, Yugoslav “Self-Administration”).

In 1950 Yugoslavia introduced the Law on Self-Management of Enterprises Through Workers Councils, which replaced direct state administration of enterprises with workers’ councils and management boards. Contemporary communist observers called it “the law concerning the workers’ councils introduced in Yugoslavia in March 1950” (Fourth International, “State Capitalism in the USSR”, 1951); Hoxha treated the same institutions as “the system of ‘Self-Administration’ in the Economy” (Hoxha, 1978). In 1952 the party renamed itself the League of Communists of Yugoslavia, or SKJ, and redefined its role as “ideological” rather than direct administrative command. Edvard Kardelj theorised “socialist self-management” as a distinct road, and Hoxha’s book engages his Directions of the Development of the Political System of Socialist Self-Administration chapter by chapter (contents of Hoxha’s study). The 1974 Constitution institutionalised the system through associated labour, delegates’ assemblies, rotation of office and a collective federal presidency with republican and provincial vetoes. It tried to reconcile workers’ self-management with national autonomy among six republics and two autonomous provinces. For anti-revisionists this was “self-administrative socialism”, an “opportunist ideological trend and an anti-socialist and anti-communist political practice” (Workers’ Advocate, 1977).

Yugoslavia was also a co-founder, with Egypt’s Gamal Abdel Nasser, India’s Jawaharlal Nehru, Ghana’s Kwame Nkrumah and Indonesia’s Sukarno, of the Non-Aligned Movement, whose first summit was held in Belgrade in September 1961. Non-alignment meant staying outside the two military blocs of the Cold War, and Tito presented it as a third road. The anti-revisionist critique was that it detached national liberation movements from the socialist camp and disguised an alliance with imperialism: “Tito put forward the hoax that these countries should be ‘non-aligned’ with both western imperialism and the socialist Soviet Union … In reality, this actually meant openly splitting with socialism while covertly allying with imperialism” (Workers’ Advocate, 1977). Revolutionary movements used the language differently. Amílcar Cabral’s address of 1964, National Liberation and Peace, Cornerstones of Non-alignment, treated non-alignment as a terrain of anti-imperialist struggle rather than a third road (Cabral archive). After Tito’s death in May 1980, the collective presidency, mounting foreign debt and intensifying national antagonisms led to the dissolution of the state in 1991 and 1992 and to wars whose interpretation remains contested.

The Communist Party of Albania was founded on 8 November 1941, with Enver Hoxha as its first secretary. It led the National Liberation Front, liberated the country in November 1944 and established the People’s Republic of Albania in January 1946. It was renamed the Party of Labour of Albania, or PLA, in 1948. Hoxha’s reports of 1944 and 1946 are the founding documents of the new state (Hoxha archive). Albania’s three ruptures define its international position. It sided with the Cominform resolution against Tito in 1948, despite the KPJ’s earlier influence over Albanian partisan politics, and the party consistently treated Titoism as “the first revisionist group to emerge in a socialist country” (Hoxha, Imperialism and the Revolution). It broke with the Soviet Union in 1960 to 1961, when Hoxha delivered his speech Reject the Revisionist Theses of the XX Congress of the CPSU and the Anti-Marxist Stand of Khrushchev’s Group! Uphold Marxism-Leninism! at the Moscow meeting of 81 parties (text of the November 1960 speech; September 1960 plenum speeches). Soviet advisers were withdrawn and relations severed, and Albania withdrew from the Warsaw Pact’s activities after the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia. The Khruschevites of 1980 is Hoxha’s full-length political memoir of that rupture (contents). It broke with China between 1972 and 1978, after first aligning with the Chinese Communist Party in the Sino-Soviet split. Reflections on China and Imperialism and the Revolution set out the case, the latter under headings including “The Theory of the ‘Three Worlds’: A Counter-revolutionary Chauvinist Theory”, “China’s Plan to Become a Superpower” and “‘Mao Tsetung Thought’: An Anti-Marxist Theory” (table of contents). Under the 1976 constitution Albania became the People’s Socialist Republic, the only state to describe itself in those terms. Hoxha died on 11 April 1985, Ramiz Alia succeeded him, multiparty elections were held in 1991 and the old order collapsed in 1992.

Revolutionary leaders and liberation movements#

The international movement also included revolutionaries and liberation movements that adapted its ideas to their own conditions. Their relation to the socialist camp was often practical rather than based on a shared doctrine. The Comintern’s national and colonial theses and the postwar documents supplied a theoretical basis. Hoxha’s exposition quoted Stalin’s formulation that “Leninism has proved … that the road to victory of the revolution in the West lies through the revolutionary alliance with the liberation movement of the colonies and dependent countries against imperialism. The national problem is a part of the general problem of the proletarian revolution, a part of the problem of the dictatorship of the proletariat” (Hoxha, “The Peoples’ Liberation Struggle: a Component Part of the World Revolution”). The 1960 Statement treated support for such movements as an “internationalist duty”, and the Chinese proposal of 1963 called the national democratic revolution in those areas “an important component of the contemporary proletarian world revolution” (CPC Proposal, section 8).

Ernesto “Che” Guevara, an Argentine-born doctor who became a commander in the Cuban Revolution, set out the theory of the foco, a small armed revolutionary nucleus intended to create the conditions for wider revolt, in his article “Guerrilla War, a Method” of September 1963. He wrote that “it is not always necessary to wait for all conditions favorable to revolution to be present; the insurrection itself can create them”, and that “in the underdeveloped parts of America, the battleground for armed struggle should in the main be the countryside” (Guevara, “Guerrilla War, a Method”). His Message to the Tricontinental of 16 April 1967 argued that “imperialism is a world system, the last stage of capitalism — and it must be defeated in a world confrontation” (Guevara, Message to the Tricontinental). He led a guerrilla campaign in Bolivia in 1966 and 1967 and was killed on 9 October 1967. The outcome of that campaign, and the assessment of his economic ideas, are treated in the chapter on Guevara.

In Angola the MPLA, the People’s Movement for the Liberation of Angola, was founded in 1956 under Agostinho Neto. Its early documents include the Declaration Addressed to the Portuguese Government of 1960, the Program of Immediate Action of 1962 and the Statutes and Programme of 1966 (MPLA archive). Armed struggle began in February 1961 and internal conflicts included the “Eastern Revolt” of 1974 and Daniel Chipenda’s split. After the Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974 in Portugal, the Alvor Agreement of January 1975 set a transition to independence, but civil war broke out between the MPLA, the FNLA and UNITA. With Cuban and Soviet support the MPLA took Luanda and proclaimed the People’s Republic of Angola on 11 November 1975 (Alvor Agreement and Neto’s speech). Neto died in September 1979 and was succeeded by José Eduardo dos Santos. The MPLA became the MPLA Party of Labour in 1977 as a Marxist-Leninist vanguard party, abandoned Marxism-Leninism at its 1990 congress and accepted multiparty elections in 1992.

In Mozambique FRELIMO, the Mozambique Liberation Front, was formed in June 1962 through the merger of MANU and UDENAMO under Eduardo Mondlane (FRELIMO archive). Armed struggle began on 25 September 1964. Mondlane was assassinated in February 1969 and Samora Machel took the leadership. In the liberated zones FRELIMO established popular courts, schools and cooperatives, so that the war involved ordinary people as teachers, local officials and producers as well as fighters. Independence was proclaimed on 25 June 1975. The Third Congress of 1977 declared the party Marxist-Leninist and set the transition to socialism as its task (Machel’s report to the Third Congress; congress assessment). War against the Rhodesian and South African backed RENAMO insurgency, economic crisis and the shifting regional balance led to the Fifth Congress of 1989, the abandonment of the Marxist-Leninist formulation and a new constitution in 1990.

In Guinea-Bissau and Cape Verde the PAIGC, the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde, was led by Amílcar Cabral. His essays The Weapon of Theory of 1966, National Liberation and Culture of 1970 and The Nation-Class of 1971 are among the most original bodies of Marxist theory of national liberation (Cabral archive). Cabral wrote that “there are two alternatives: either we admit that there really is a struggle against imperialism which interests everybody, or we deny it. If … imperialism exists and is trying simultaneously to dominate the working class in all the advanced countries and smother the national liberation movements in all the underdeveloped countries, then there is only one enemy against whom we are fighting” (loc. cit.). The party built schools, health posts and local administration in the liberated areas, and peasants who joined or supplied those structures were participants in the political project, not merely its beneficiaries. The PAIGC proclaimed the independence of Guinea-Bissau in 1973, recognised by Portugal in 1974 after the Carnation Revolution. Cabral was assassinated in January 1973.

In Ethiopia the Derg, the Provisional Military Administrative Council, seized power in September 1974, deposed Haile Selassie and declared “scientific socialism”. Mengistu Haile Mariam became chairman in 1977. His Address to the Nation of 1977, the Fourth Anniversary speech of 1978 and the announcement of the Commission for Organising the Party of the Working People of Ethiopia in 1979 are primary sources (Mengistu archive). With Soviet and Cuban assistance the Derg defeated the Somali invasion in the Ogaden in 1977 and 1978. The Commission was formed in 1979 and the Workers’ Party of Ethiopia in September 1984. The regime fell in May 1991.

The same archives document appeals by these movements to the Organisation of African Unity and the United Nations, the Afro-Asian Peoples’ Solidarity Organisation, Cuban Tricontinental solidarity and Soviet and Eastern bloc material support (Africa subject archive). They also include documents of the African National Congress, the South African Communist Party, SWAPO, ZAPU, ZANU, the Algerian revolution and Soviet writings on the “non-capitalist road of development” (loc. cit.). In Asia the Korean Workers’ Party, the Japanese Communist Party, the Indonesian PKI, the Indian CPI and CPI(M) and the Indochinese parties all figured in the Comintern and postwar movements (Comintern sections).

In the advanced capitalist countries the parties developed distinct strategies that became part of the international argument. In Italy Palmiro Togliatti led the party from 1927, in exile and under the name Ercoli, returned in 1944 and led the “Salerno Turn” into a government of national unity, then developed the “Italian road to socialism” centred on parliament and mass alliances (Togliatti archive). In France Maurice Thorez’s line was addressed in the Chinese polemic Whence the Differences? A Reply to Thorez and Other Comrades of 27 February 1963 (CPC editorial). In Spain Dolores Ibárruri and Santiago Carrillo led the Communist Party, which was legalised after Franco’s death and played a major role in the transition. In Britain the Communist Party had a substantial theoretical press including The Communist Review, Labour Monthly and Marxism Today (Comintern British section). In the United States Earl Browder’s dissolution of the party into the Communist Political Association in 1944 became, in anti-revisionist writing, the first great instance of “modern revisionism” in a ruling-class country (The Workers’ Advocate, 1977). The new communist movements of the 1960s and 1970s rejected both the older parties and Trotskyism, and their press is a source for how the splits were received at the base (Revolution magazine).

The socialist states after 1991#

Four states with ruling communist parties remained into the twenty-first century: China, the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea, Vietnam and Cuba. Each is treated elsewhere. Laos deserves notice here because its constitutional settlement shows how a surviving party state reconciled socialist language with a market economy. The Lao People’s Revolutionary Party traces its lineage to the Indochinese Communist Party of the 1930s, was constituted as a separate party in 1955, and after the Indochina settlement of 1954 and the subsequent civil war defeated the Royal Lao government. The Lao People’s Democratic Republic was proclaimed on 2 December 1975, with Kaysone Phomvihane as the dominant leader. The preamble of the constitution records that “under the correct leadership of the former Indochinese Communist Party and the present Lao People’s Revolutionary Party, the multi-ethnic Lao people … managed to crush the yokes of domination and oppression of the colonial and feudal regimes, completely liberate the country and establish the Lao People’s Democratic Republic on 2 December 1975” (Constitution of the Lao PDR, 1991, revised 2003).

The constitution defines the political and economic order in terms comparable with the other surviving socialist states. Article 2 calls Laos “a people’s democratic state” in which “all powers belong to the people”, with workers, farmers and intelligentsia as key components. Article 3 states that the people’s rights are exercised “with the Lao People’s Revolutionary Party as its leading nucleus”. Article 5 requires state organisations to follow “the principle of democratic centralism”, meaning that decisions are made centrally and then carried out uniformly. Article 13 provides for “a stable multi-sectoral economy” in which “all types of enterprises are equal before the laws and operate according to the principle of the market economy, competing and cooperating with each other”, while being “regulated by the State in the direction of socialism”. Article 18 states that “the State manages the economy in accordance with the mechanism of the market economy regulated by the State” (loc. cit.). These clauses codify the “new economic mechanism” of enterprise autonomy, foreign investment and mixed ownership adopted from the mid-1980s and associated with the party’s Fourth Congress of 1986. Article 15 guarantees that “the lawful assets and capital of investors in the Lao People’s Democratic Republic shall not be confiscated, seized or nationalised by the State”, and Article 12 commits Laos to “peaceful coexistence; respect for each other’s independence, sovereignty and territorial integrity; noninterference in each other’s internal affairs; and equality and mutual benefit” (loc. cit.). As with Vietnam’s Đổi Mới and China’s reform and opening, the party presents these measures not as an abandonment of socialism but as a stage of “creating conditions to move towards socialism” (loc. cit.).
