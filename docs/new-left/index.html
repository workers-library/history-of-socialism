The New Left in the West: From 1956 to the Years of Lead

First-hand sources on The New Left in the West: From 1956 to the Years of Lead: first-hand documents, the evidence, scholarly interpretations and the limits of the record.

In the summer of 1957, E. P. Thompson, a historian and a communist who had broken with the party, put an open letter in the first issue of a small journal called The New Reasoner. His subject was what socialists should say about Stalinism, the system of rule and doctrine built under Stalin in the Soviet Union. He argued that it had to be understood as an ideology that could win assent, not merely as hypocrisy by men who knew better. “It is humanist because it places once again real men and women at the centre of socialist theory and aspiration” (E. P. Thompson, “Socialist Humanism: An Epistle to the Philistines”, The New Reasoner no. 1, Summer 1957. Marxists Internet Archive: sochum). The current he was naming became known as socialist humanism: socialism defined by actual people rather than by abstractions such as “Marxism-Leninism-Stalinism”.

The generation after 1956#

Two years later Thompson described the current he belonged to. “It is a generation which never looked upon the Soviet Union as a weak but heroic Workers’ State; but, rather, as the nation of the Great Purges and of Stalingrad” (E. P. Thompson, “The New Left”, 20 May 1959. Marxists Internet Archive: newleft). He wrote before New Left Review existed and admitted that in Britain this “New Left” was scarcely identifiable as an organisation. He proposed journals, clubs and education instead of a new party, and attacked what he called demonism, economism and parasitic factionalism among the older left. The name described a convergence of dissident communists, left socialists and the postwar generation, not a membership card.

The same break showed up in the campaign against nuclear weapons. The Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, or CND, pressed for Britain to give up its bombs unilaterally, whatever other states did. Thompson defended that position in the summer of 1958, linking it to a break with NATO, the Western military alliance, and to what he called active neutrality in Europe. “This is what the Aldermaston marchers thought they were marching about; not only against the Bomb but also against all crooked attitudes of power which permit the Bomb to be used as an instrument of policy.” (E. P. Thompson, “NATO, Neutralism and Survival”, Summer 1958. Marxists Internet Archive: nato) He reported that British Communist Party leaders regarded CND as a “pathetic illusion”. He conceded that some of his own assumptions could not be proved; his piece is an argument about what the marches meant, not a survey of the marchers.

In France the corresponding rupture came through the war in Algeria, France’s colonial war against the movement for Algerian independence. On 6 September 1960 (the press release was issued on 5 September), the day before the trial of the Jeanson network, 121 intellectuals and public figures, among them Simone de Beauvoir, Jean-Paul Sartre, André Breton and Henri Lefebvre, declared a right of insubordination. “The cause of the Algerian people, which contributes decisively to the ruin of the colonial system, is the cause of all free men and women.” (The Manifesto of the 121, 5 September 1960. Marxists Internet Archive: manifesto 121) A journal that printed the text was censored and left two blank pages. The manifesto broke with the official Communist Party’s cautious peace campaign. It was written by intellectuals, not by Algerians or soldiers, and the publisher stopped at 121 signatures because the number sounded nice.

May 1968 in Paris#

At Nanterre, a student group called the March 22 Movement issued a leaflet calling a demonstration for 10 May 1968. It rejected what it called a “dialogue” about decisions already taken and refused the role of “complicitous managers and beneficiaries of the exploitation of the workers”. “We don’t want to be capitalism’s watchdogs.” (March 22 Movement leaflet, “1000 Wounded 3 dead Combat Gas”, 10 May 1968. Marxists Internet Archive: 1000 wounded) The casualty figure in the heading was the movement’s own, not an independent count.

On the night of 10 to 11 May, students occupied the Latin Quarter and built roughly sixty barricades. Le Monde‘s reporters reconstructed the hours from the assemblies to the police assault at about 2:15 in the morning. The police prefect’s balance sheet gave 367 injured, 251 of them police, 461 arrests and 60 cars burned. Daniel Cohn-Bendit, after meeting the rector, said: “What’s happening this evening in the streets is that an entire youth is expressing itself against a certain society.” (Daniel Cohn-Bendit, quoted in Le Monde, 12–13 May 1968; translated by Mitchell Abidor. Marxists Internet Archive: night barricades) The reporters inferred that the barricades had not been part of the organisers’ plan.

The Sorbonne was occupied from 13 May. A committee there issued instructions on 16 May: “OCCUPY THE FACTORIES / POWER TO THE WORKERS COUNCILS / ABOLISH CLASS SOCIETY” (Occupation Committee of the People’s Free Sorbonne University, 16 May 1968; translated by Ken Knabb. Marxists Internet Archive: slogans), and proposed spreading them by leaflets, microphones, songs and graffiti. A Council for Maintaining the Occupations wrote on 19 May: “The occupation of the Sorbonne that began Monday, May 13, has opened a new period in the crisis of modern society.” (Council for Maintaining the Occupations, 19 May 1968; translated by Ken Knabb. Marxists Internet Archive: sorbonne) Factory occupations spread from Sud-Aviation at Nantes on 14 May to roughly a hundred factories three days later. The council’s report was a factional document written to justify a split, and its charges of manipulation are claims, not findings.

What the student documents could not supply was the voice of the workers. An unnamed libertarian communist at Renault-Boulogne-Billancourt on 13 May described leaflets in French and Spanish, about 10,000 workers in the Île Seguin shed, speakers from the CGT, the CFDT and FO, and the singing of the Internationale. “A friend nearby assures me that in 20 years this is the first time he has heard the song sung inside Renault.” (An eyewitness account by a Libertarian Communist, 13 May 1968. Marxists Internet Archive: libertarian communist account) He called CGT officials “Stalinist” and said their stewards tried to keep students away from the Champ de Mars. A Maoist leaflet went further: “THE BANNER OF THE STRUGGLE HAS PASSED INTO THE HANDS OF THE PROLETARIAT” (leaflet “The Red Flag of the Workers Floats over Renault”, late May 1968; translated by Mitchell Abidor. Marxists Internet Archive: red flag), and denounced the PCF and CGT leaderships for trying to impose “petty-bourgeois university watchwords”. These are three partisan accounts of who spoke for the strike. The eyewitness admitted he could not count the crowd.

The armed minority in West Germany#

Some students drew the opposite lesson. On 1 April 1971 the Red Army Faction, an underground group in West Germany, published “The Urban Guerilla Concept”. An urban guerrilla is a small armed group operating in cities; the RAF claimed the name as an answer to the Emergency Laws and to the state’s capacity for repression. “The urban guerilla is the consequence of the long since complete negation of parliamentary democracy by the elected representatives themselves.” (Red Army Faction, “The Urban Guerilla Concept”, 1 April 1971; English translation from the Smith/Moncourt documentary history, individual translator not named. Source: germanguerilla.com) The manifesto placed the RAF in the lineage of the student revolt while rejecting that revolt’s “antiauthoritarianism” as a political form. It cited Mao, Lenin, Stokely Carmichael’s Black Power and the Black Panthers, and gave the group’s version of the May 1970 prison break and shooting. Its denials about who fired first and its claim that informers betrayed it are self-defences by people wanted for violence. The documentary history that makes the text available in English argues against reducing the group to a “pathological” celebrity; that is an editorial position, not a neutral description.

Italy: the constitutional road and its critics#

In Italy the dispute took a parliamentary form. Enrico Berlinguer, general secretary of the Italian Communist Party, wrote in the party journal Rinascita on 12 October 1973, in the weeks after the coup that overthrew Salvador Allende in Chile: “Ecco perché noi parliamo non di una «alternativa di sinistra» ma di una «alternativa democratica»” (“That is why we speak not of a ‘left alternative’ but of a ‘democratic alternative’”) (Enrico Berlinguer, Rinascita, 12 October 1973; translated from the Italian. Source: enricoberlinguer.it). This was the launch of the “historic compromise”: an alliance of communists, socialists and Catholic popular forces rather than a left bloc alone. Berlinguer argued that the democratic road was neither straight nor painless, that a 51 per cent left majority would not by itself protect a government, and he invoked Lenin on Brest-Litovsk and the New Economic Policy to defend tactical retreat. Six years later he defended the strategy under the heading “Né meschine transazioni né espedienti tattici” (“Neither shabby transactions nor tactical expedients”) (Enrico Berlinguer, Rinascita, 22 August 1979; translated from the Italian. Source: enricoberlinguer.it), linking it to austerity and to workers’ control of part of investment. That later defence comes from the strategy’s author and cannot serve as a neutral verdict.

The hardest test came in 1978. The Red Brigades kidnapped Aldo Moro, president of the Christian Democrats, on 16 March and killed him on 9 May. From captivity he wrote to his own party. To Benigno Zaccagnini around 19 April: “Io lo dico chiaro: per parte mia non assolverò e non giustificherò nessuno.” (“I say it plainly: for my part I will absolve and justify no one.”) (Aldo Moro to Benigno Zaccagnini, about 19 April 1978; translated from the Italian. Source: it.wikisource.org) To the party on 27 April: “Ma tra le Brigate Rosse e me non c’è la minima comunanza di vedute.” (“But between the Red Brigades and me there is not the slightest community of views.”) (Aldo Moro to the DC party, concluded 27 April 1978; translated from the Italian. Source: it.wikisource.org) He argued that “reason of state” could not justify abandoning him, urged an exchange of prisoners and warned that refusal would split party and country. He also insisted he was lucid, unsedated and writing in his own hand. That is his claim under captivity, not independent verification. The scholarly edition records several versions of some letters, and their authenticity was disputed at the time.

How historians have read it#

  • Kristin Ross, May ’68 and Its Afterlives (University of Chicago Press, 2002). Internet Archive: may68itsafterliv0000ross Studies how the event was remembered and absorbed; supports reading leaflets and graffiti as claims about everyday life and flags the later commodification of the slogans.
  • Mitchell Abidor, May Made Me: An Oral History of the May 1968 Uprising in France (AK Press, 2018), with a supplement hosted by MIA. Marxists Internet Archive: index Collects participants’ recollections. These are retrospective, so they are a test of how memory differs from the contemporary leaflets.
  • J. Smith and André Moncourt, eds, The Red Army Faction: A Documentary History, Vol. 1: Projectiles for the People (Kersplebedeb / PM Press, 2009). Source: germanguerilla.com An explicitly committed documentary edition that makes the RAF’s manifestos available in English and argues against the “pathological” interpretation; its editorial standpoint should be declared when it is used.
  • David Cunningham, There’s Something Happening Here: The New Left, the Klan, and FBI Counterintelligence (University of California Press, 2004; paperback 2005). Source: ucpress.edu Uses more than 12,000 FOIA documents to study repression of both the New Left and the Klan; it supplies the state’s side of the story that the first-hand sources here largely lack.
  • Michele Di Sivo, ed., Le lettere di Aldo Moro dalla prigionia alla storia (Mura, Rome, 2013). Source: it.wikisource.org Establishes delivery dates, manuscript features and variant versions, and is the necessary companion to any use of the Moro letters.

Historians broadly treat 1956 as the rupture that made a non-orthodox Western left possible, treat May 1968 as a student revolt that became a mass strike, and treat the RAF and the Red Brigades as minority breaks with the student movements rather than their natural continuation. They disagree about how far 1968 changed French society, about how the RAF should be explained, and about the authenticity and meaning of the Moro letters. The existence of the documents is certain; their interpretation is not.

Limits of the record#

The French documents reached readers through translations of leaflets and reports scanned at Simon Fraser University; the scans themselves are not reproduced here, so original wording and page references remain unchecked. The famous slogan “Sous les pavés, la plage” appears in none of these sources, and accounts of where and when it was written are disputed. No speech by de Gaulle, no text of the Grenelle accords and no founding editorial of New Left Review is available here. For West Germany the only first-hand text is the RAF’s own manifesto. No speech or diary of Rudi Dutschke has been verified, and the surviving German source is not a 68er but the group that broke with the student movement. The Italian material gives the PCI leader and the kidnapped premier, but no Red Brigades communiqué and no rank-and-file Catholic or trade-union voice. The Italian quotations are translations; the Berlinguer archive warns that some documents are incomplete. The Moro letters are a transcription of a 2013 scholarly edition, and several versions exist. The RAF translation names no individual translator. Across all four countries the voices that survive are those of leaders, manifesto writers and journalists. Workers, women and ordinary members appear mainly as subjects of other people’s accounts.
