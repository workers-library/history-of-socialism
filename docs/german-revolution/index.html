The Revolution That Stopped: Germany, 1918–1919

The German revolution began with a refusal.

MarxismPart V: Revolutions and Movements in Europe and Asia

The German revolution began with a refusal. In the last weeks of the war the naval command ordered the fleet at Kiel to put to sea for a final battle against Britain, and sailors refused to go. They and the workers of the port seized the city, released prisoners and formed a soldiers’ council, an elected body of sailors and soldiers. On 4 November 1918 the council issued fourteen demands, including freedom of speech and press, an end to mail censorship, the prevention of another fleet sortie, and the election of officers by their units. The leaflet ended with a claim of authority: “These demands are orders of the Soldiers’ Council and are binding for every military person.” (Soldiers’ Council of Kiel, 4 November 1918; translated by Klaus Kuhl and GHDI staff, germanhistorydocs.org). A demand list is not a record of what was carried out, but it shows how the revolution understood itself at the start. It was a military revolt with a political programme, not a party conspiracy.

Two republics and an argument#

Workers’ and soldiers’ councils spread through the cities in the days that followed. On 9 November, Philipp Scheidemann of the Social Democratic Party, the largest socialist party, declared a German republic from a window of the Reichstag; Karl Liebknecht proclaimed a socialist republic later the same day. In the weeks before, the shop steward Richard Müller had chaired the executive body of the councils in Berlin. Writing in 1921, he placed the origins of the council movement in wartime strikes and factory committees of 1916 to 1918, not in the Russian example of the previous year: “In November 1918, the revolutionary socialists recognised that the realisation of formal democracy, of political equality, the convening of the National Assembly, was tantamount to the re-establishment of the shaken class rule of the bourgeoisie” (Richard Müller, “The Council System in Germany”, 1921; translated by Ryan Breeden, historicalmaterialism.org). Müller was a defeated participant writing an argument as well as a memory, and his claim that the revolution was not made sits awkwardly beside his own account of preparing an insurrection.

A provisional government, the Council of People’s Deputies, brought together three ministers of the Social Democratic Party and three of the Independent Social Democratic Party, a breakaway formed over the war. Its photograph postcard, made for collectors, showed all six with a red flag. Friedrich Ebert, the Social Democratic leader who co-chaired it, welcomed nine returning divisions to Berlin on 10 December. He told them that no enemy had overcome them and that the new state would be a “polity of labor”: “The socialist republic that welcomes you through me will be a polity of labor.” (Friedrich Ebert, address to homecoming troops, 10 December 1918; translated by Pam Selwyn, germanhistorydocs.org). His words joined recognition of the soldiers’ sacrifice to a demand that they now lay down their arms. The claim that the army had not been defeated fed the story, current after the war, that it had been betrayed at home.

The other half of the arrangement emerged later. Wilhelm Groener, who led the army high command from October 1918, described secret telephone agreements with Ebert, a plan to bring divisions into Berlin, and a threat to resign if the councils’ congress was allowed to reorganise the army on its own terms. “All that remained was to muster a volunteer army, which alone could take up the battle against the urban masses of workers.” (Wilhelm Groener, memoirs published 1957; translated by Pam Selwyn, germanhistorydocs.org). The arrangement was denied until 1925, and Groener wrote after his death with an interest in presenting the army as the restorer of order. His pages are a memory of conversations, not the pact itself.

The argument among socialists was concrete and organisational: whether power belonged in the councils or in a National Assembly, and who would control the army. Rosa Luxemburg, a founder of the Spartacus League, the revolutionary group around her and Liebknecht, wrote in Die Rote Fahne on 17 December that the choice lay between an assembly and “all power to the Workers’ and Soldiers’ Councils”. She also wrote that “The proletarian revolution requires no terror for its aims; it hates and despises killing.” (Rosa Luxemburg, “What Does the Spartacus League Want?”, Die Rote Fahne, 14 December 1918; translated by Martin Nicolaus, marxists.org). That sentence was attacked at the founding congress of the Communist Party of Germany, or KPD, as a veiled criticism of the Bolsheviks, which shows how contested the text was on the left. At that congress, on 30 December, Luxemburg argued the opposite tactic to her own article of thirteen days earlier: enter the National Assembly elections, use them to reach workers, and “blow up this bulwark from within”. “You have nothing behind you but the miserable half-revolution of November 9th.” (Rosa Luxemburg, speech at the KPD founding congress, 30 December 1918; GHDI translation, germanhistorydocs.org). The congress rejected her view and boycotted the elections. In her programme speech she told delegates to return to the foundation laid by Marx and Engels in 1848 and insisted that power must be won “not from above but from below”: “Our motto is: In the beginning was the act.” (Rosa Luxemburg, “Our Program and the Political Situation”, 30 December 1918; translated by Dick Howard, germanhistorydocs.org). The two December texts must be read apart, not merged into a single position.

The view from the street#

Käthe Kollwitz, a Berlin artist and socialist, kept a diary through these weeks. She recorded the abdication leaflets at the Brandenburg Gate, shooting near the Reichstag, Liebknecht speaking to front-line soldiers, the Christmas fighting at the palace, women voting for the first time, and the March battles. She was drawn to the council left and afraid of civil war, and she wrote on 8 December: “if I had to choose between the dictatorship of Ebert and the dictatorship of Liebknecht, I would certainly choose Ebert.” (Käthe Kollwitz, diary, 8 December 1918; GHDI translation, germanhistorydocs.org). She was a middle-class artist, not a factory worker or a soldier, and she noted her own difficulty reaching an independent judgement. Betty Scholem, wife of a Berlin printer, wrote to her son on 13 January as machine-gun fire sounded outside: “This past week has been incredible, bizarre beyond belief. It now seems that the Spartacans have been all but driven out.” (Betty Scholem, letter, 13 January 1919; translated by Anthony David Skinner, germanhistorydocs.org). Her letters report what she saw on walks and heard at home, and are openly hostile to the insurgents. A government newsreel of the same fighting listed the Brandenburg Gate, government troops arriving at Pariser Platz and the recapture of the Vorwärts newspaper building. In Munich, Kurt Eisner, the journalist who led the Bavarian revolution, argued that a socialist state should leave the artist “complete freedom of artistic action”; he was assassinated in February 1919, before the Council Republic he might have shaped.

The killings and the court#

On 15 January 1919, Luxemburg and Liebknecht were seized, taken to the headquarters of the Guard Rifle Corps at the Eden Hotel, beaten and shot. Liebknecht was delivered to a morgue as an unknown man; Luxemburg’s body was thrown into the Landwehr Canal and recovered months later. Kollwitz wrote on 16 January: “Outrageous and despicable murder of Liebknecht and Luxemburg.” (Käthe Kollwitz, diary, 16 January 1919; GHDI translation, germanhistorydocs.org). The military court set up by the same division acquitted almost everyone; two officers received short sentences for lesser offences. Waldemar Pabst, the Freikorps chief of staff who ordered the killings, wrote in 1969: “Daß ich die Aktion ohne Noskes Zustimmung garnicht durchführen konnte (mit Ebert im Hintergrund) und auch meine Offiziere schützen mußte, ist klar” (Waldemar Pabst, letter of 26 June 1969, original German, Bundesarchiv), which translates as “That I could not carry out the action without Noske’s consent (with Ebert in the background), and had to protect my officers too, is clear.” Pabst was writing decades later, had never been prosecuted, and shifted between naming different men as the shooters. The court file proves that he ordered the killings and that his officers were protected; it does not prove what the defence minister said by telephone. Whether Gustav Noske authorised the murders is disputed, and Pabst first named Hermann Souchon, and the Bundesarchiv now regards Souchon as the proven killer of Luxemburg; a different name, Kurt Vogel, came from the 1919 court-martial file. Luxemburg’s own attitude to the January uprising remains unclear in the surviving record.

How historians have read it#

  • Sebastian Haffner, Failure of a Revolution: Germany 1918–19 (German 1969; English 1973). A short, polemical narrative that places the failure on Ebert’s deference to traditional authority and fear of disorder; reviewers found it readable but over-personalised (Encyclopedia.com).
  • Mark Jones, Founding Weimar: Violence and the German Revolution of 1918–1919 (2016). A study of incidents of violence arguing that fear and rumour led the new state to use “pedagogical violence” to demonstrate its right to rule (Cambridge University Press).
  • Ralf Hoffrogge, Working-Class Politics in the German Revolution: Richard Müller, the Revolutionary Shop Stewards and the Origins of the Council Movement (2014). Recovers the shop stewards as a distinct leadership and argues that their lack of a coherent political vision paralysed the revolution; John Rose’s review disputes the treatment of Luxemburg (review).
  • Klaus Gietinger, Eine Leiche im Landwehrkanal (1995) and Der Konterrevolutionär: Waldemar Pabst** (2008). Uses the Pabst estate to reconstruct the murders and the cover-up; the Federal Archives recommends both while noting that Pabst’s claim of Social Democratic complicity is unproven (Bundesarchiv).
  • Ben Fowkes, ed. and trans., The German Left and the Weimar Republic: A Selection of Documents (2013). Makes available the agreements and polemics of the period, including the accord between employers and unions (review).
  • Anthony McElligott, Rethinking the Weimar Republic: Authority and Authoritarianism, 1916–1936 (2014). Frames 1918–19 as part of a longer argument about authority rather than a clean constitutional break (germanhistorydocs.org).

There is broad agreement that the monarchy collapsed because of military defeat and the Kiel mutiny, that councils spread rapidly, that the Social Democratic leadership chose a National Assembly and an alliance with the army, and that the murders and the court-martial breached the new state’s own law. How much responsibility Ebert personally bears, whether Luxemburg’s tactics helped or harmed the councils, and whether the revolution was betrayed from above or defeated by the left’s own organisational weaknesses remain open arguments.

Limits of the record#

The English-language record is uneven, weighted toward Berlin and toward people who wrote. There is no first-hand account here from a rank-and-file participant in the January fighting, from a woman in the councils’ movement, or from a soldier in the ranks of a Freikorps. The Freikorps appears mainly through its commander’s late memoirs, a few facsimiles and the findings of its own court, all of which need to be read as self-justification. The Pabst estate and the court files are catalogued and partly digitised but have not been read document by document. A finding aid in the Prussian Secret State Archives lists an autopsy report, statements by witnesses at the Eden Hotel and Otto Runge’s memorandum of 1932; none of those texts was opened. The Bavarian Council Republic is thinly documented in English: Eisner’s essay on art and a photograph of troops entering Munich are all that is available here. Ernst Toller’s prison memoir and Erich Mühsam’s diary would fill part of the gap, and Richard Müller’s three-volume history has not been translated. Several quotations are translations, identified where they appear. The violence of March 1919 and of Munich in May 1919 lies largely outside this record, and a fuller account would need separate sources.
