The Dirty War: Argentina, 1973–1983

On 24 March 1977, the first anniversary of the military coup, Rodolfo Walsh sat down to write an open letter to the junta.

On 24 March 1977, the first anniversary of the military coup, Rodolfo Walsh sat down to write an open letter to the junta. Walsh was a writer and journalist who had worked for the CGT news service, had later joined the Montoneros, and had lost a daughter in combat. He was writing from inside Argentina as a man who expected to be arrested, and he circulated the letter clandestinely rather than submitting it to the censors. He opened with a count: “Quince mil desaparecidos, diez mil presos, cuatro mil muertos, decenas de miles de desterrados son la cifra desnuda de ese terror.” Fifteen thousand disappeared, ten thousand prisoners, four thousand dead, tens of thousands exiled: that was the bare figure of the terror, as he saw it in the regime’s first year (Rodolfo Walsh, Carta abierta de un escritor a la Junta Militar, 24 March 1977). He knew the letter might cost him his life: “sin esperanza de ser escuchado, con la certeza de ser perseguido, pero fiel al compromiso que asumí hace mucho tiempo de dar testimonio en momentos difíciles.” He was declaring himself without hope of being heard, certain of being hunted, but faithful to a pledge to bear witness in hard times (Walsh, same letter). The desaparecidos, the disappeared, were people seized by the security forces and never acknowledged to be in custody. Their families could not prove they were alive or dead. The Argentine state called what it was doing a war against subversion and described itself as anti-communist and “occidental y cristiano,” Western and Christian.

The armed turn#

To understand why Walsh wrote as he did, it is necessary to go back to the movements that preceded the dictatorship. Juan Perón was overthrown by a coup in 1955, and Peronism, the political movement built around him, was banned. A generation of activists concluded that constitutional politics had been closed to them. Among them was the Montoneros. On 1 June 1970 the organisation issued a communiqué of four sentences. “The leadership of the MONTONEROS communicates that at 7:00 today Pedro Eugenio Aramburu was executed.” (Montoneros Communiqué No. 4, 1 June 1970; translated by Mitchell Abidor). Aramburu was a former president; the 1955 coup against Perón had been led by Lonardi, whom Aramburu replaced on 13 November 1955. The terse notice, addressed over the heads of the government to “the people of the nation,” set the movement’s early public tone: ritualised, formal, and insistent that it spoke for the country.

Later that year the Montoneros defined themselves more fully. “Only by fighting will we succeed in recovering what is ours. The Montoneros call for armed resistance for a Free, Just and Sovereign Fatherland.” (Montoneros, “The Montoneros: Armed Wing of Peronism,” September 1970; translated by Mitchell Abidor). The claim to be the armed wing of Peronism was contested inside Peronism and later by Perón himself; the document records the claim, not its acceptance. A second current, the ERP, or Ejército Revolucionario del Pueblo, the People’s Revolutionary Army, was the armed wing of the Revolutionary Workers’ Party, a Trotskyist party influenced by Che Guevara. Its newspaper El Combatiente announced on 28 July 1976 that Mario Roberto Santucho, known as Comandante Carlos, had been killed with Domingo Menna and Captain Benito Urteaga in a firefight at a flat in Villa Martelli after their ammunition ran out (PRT-ERP, “Comandante Carlos ¡Hasta la victoria siempre!,” El Combatiente No. 226, 28 July 1976; text signed by Luis Mattini). This was a eulogy by the surviving leadership, not an independent account of how the security forces found the flat. On 12 October 1973, the day Perón resumed the presidency, the Montoneros and the FAR, the Fuerzas Armadas Revolucionarias, recorded their merger: “A partir de la fecha ambas organizaciones se fusionan pasando a constituir una sola y quedando unificadas definitivamente todas sus estructuras y mandos;” (FAR–Montoneros, Acta de Unidad, 12 October 1973). The act committed the merged organisation to “Socialismo Nacional,” a socialist programme understood as national liberation under Peronist leadership. What these documents establish is what the armed groups said about themselves. They do not establish how much support either had.

The state answers#

The machinery of internal war was authorised before the coup, by an elected government. On 5 February 1975 President María Estela Martínez de Perón signed Secret Decree 261/75. Its first article read: “El Comando General del Ejército procederá a ejecutar las operaciones militares que sean necesarias a efectos de neutralizar y/o aniquilar el accionar de los elementos subversivos que actúan en la Provincia de TUCUMAN.” (Secret Decree 261/75, Article 1, 5 February 1975; published in the Boletín Oficial only on 9 April 2013). The army was to “neutralise and/or annihilate” the action of subversives in Tucumán. Police were placed under army operational control, the air force and navy given a support role, and forty million pesos budgeted. The decree was secret at the time, so contemporaries could not read the order that governed them. Esteban Pontoriero has used decrees, military directives and congressional debates to argue that the constitutional Peronist governments militarised repression before March 1976, and he warns that the later account of the September 1975 decision given by General Videla, the future junta leader, serves to shift blame away from the military. The decree is a command, not a report on what the army did, and its wording targets the “action” of subversives rather than persons. Later orders changed the formulation.

On 24 March 1976 the junta converted the seizure of power into a legal order. The three service chiefs, Videla, Massera and Agosti, issued a statute for what they called the National Reorganisation Process. “La Junta Militar, integrada por los comandantes generales del Ejército, la Armada y la Fuerza Aérea, órgano supremo de la Nación, velará por el normal funcionamiento de los demás poderes del Estado.” (Estatuto para el Proceso de Reorganización Nacional, Article 1, 24 March 1976; Boletín Oficial, 31 March 1976). A junta is a committee of military officers holding supreme power. The statute made the three chiefs the supreme organ of the nation, let them remove the president and the Supreme Court, gave the president legislative power subject to a nine-officer advisory commission, and subordinated the provinces to junta instructions. Both documents speak the language of law and administration. Neither mentions torture or disappearance, which existed outside the legal framework they described.

The invisible system#

Contemporary witnesses established what the official documents concealed. Walsh’s letter named censorship, the seizure of his home, murdered friends, the disappearance of prisoners, torture methods drawn from survivor testimony, and what he called miseria planificada, planned misery: cuts to real wages, scarcity of food and medicine, and the suppression of unions. He challenged the regime’s casualty arithmetic by comparing military communiqués with clandestine press reports. His figures were a militant’s estimates from his first year under the junta, not a verified count. In September 1979 a delegation of the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights, a body of the Organization of American States, visited Argentina for two weeks. It received 5,580 denunciations, interviewed Videla, the junta, ministers, judges, governors and police chiefs, and toured official prisons including Devoto, Caseros, Rawson and Unidad 9. Its conclusions found that “persons belonging to or connected with government security agencies have killed numerous men and women subsequent to their being placed in detention”. Torture was systematic and habeas corpus, the legal writ requiring a detainee to be brought before a court, was ineffective (OEA/CIDH, report on the human rights situation in Argentina, conclusions, 14 December 1979; English version). The delegation could not enter the clandestine centres whose existence it knew from testimony. It also recorded that denunciations declined after its visit, a finding that describes what was reported to it, not the fate of people already taken.

The National Commission on the Disappearance of Persons, known by its Spanish initials CONADEP, was created by decree in December 1983 and reported in September 1984, chaired by the writer Ernesto Sábato. It compiled 7,380 case files and inspected detention sites. It found that disappearance as a method began before the coup but became generalised afterwards, that roughly 340 clandestine detention centres existed, and that 1,300 people were provisionally listed as seen alive in them before disappearing. It recorded 8,960 people still disappeared at the date of the report while stating plainly that the figure was not definitive. Its judgment on the nature of the system was direct: “Tales atrocidades fueron práctica común y extendida y eran los actos normales y corrientes efectuados a diario por la represión.” (CONADEP, Nunca Más, conclusions, September 1984). Such atrocities were common and widespread practice, the ordinary and routine acts of repression carried out daily. The commission had no power to assign guilt and the armed forces destroyed records before the transition. Its chapter on trade unionists shows that 30.2 per cent of complainants were workers and records the targeting of delegates at the Astarsa shipyard and the Ford plant, people with no guerrilla affiliation.

The Mothers and the long return to court#

Relatives made the silence itself a public issue. The Mothers of Plaza de Mayo began meeting in April 1977 after months of individual searches. When police ordered them to “circular” in the square, they began the walking rounds around the May Pyramid that became their signature. The archive of the Mothers’ founding line lists a secret organising instruction of October 1977, a newspaper petition that year asking for the freedom of the mothers who searched for their children, a declaration of principles in 1979, and banners commemorating disappeared women (Memoria Abierta, documents of the Madres de Plaza de Mayo, 1977 to 1986; page published 23 March 2020). Three of the founders, Esther Ballestrino de Careaga, María Eugenia Ponce de Bianco and Azucena Villaflor, were themselves kidnapped and disappeared in December 1977. Hebe de Bonafini, president of the Asociación Madres de Plaza de Mayo, later described how the public identity was made by the loss: “Hasta que se llevaron a mi hijo mayor yo era Kika Pastor. En el mismo momento en que desaparece me convierto en Hebe de Bonafini.” (Hebe de Bonafini, quoted by Celeste del Bianco, elDiarioAR, 20 November 2022). Her two sons Jorge and Raúl and her daughter-in-law María Elena were seized in 1977 and never returned. Her words are a leader’s memory of her own conversion, recorded decades later, not a contemporary record of the events.

Survivors’ testimony became the basis of the later settlement. The museum at the former Navy Mechanics School, ESMA, describes more than seven hundred testimonies homologated before the courts, drawn from CONADEP in 1984, the 1985 Trial of the Juntas, and trials since cases were reopened in 2004 (Museo Sitio de Memoria ESMA, “Los Testimonios”). The catalogue of Memoria Abierta names witnesses such as Iris Avellaneda, mother of the disappeared Floreal Avellaneda, who testified on 28 May 1985, and the ESMA survivors Ricardo Coquet and Carlos Loza in 2010 (Memoria Abierta, “Testimonios en juicios”). Those accounts identified the names and layouts of the clandestine centres, named perpetrators, and described killings in “death flights,” a practice in which captives were thrown from aircraft. A truth commission without power to punish was followed by a trial that convicted commanders, and then by further rounds of prosecutions decades later. The Falklands war, which Argentina calls the Malvinas war, brought the dictatorship a defeat in 1982. Marcelo Pérez Schneider, a conscript who served as a gunner in the 101st Air Defence Artillery Group, remembered the confusion of the surrender: “El día de la rendición (14 de junio, a las 7 a.m.), nosotros, a las 11 de la mañana, seguíamos en nuestros puestos de combate cuando nos avisaron de que Argentina se había rendido.” (Marcelo Pérez Schneider, interview, Track to Hell, date not stated on the page). He said ordinary people did not blame him on his return and that fellow veterans remained a lasting community. His account records how one soldier understood the war; it says nothing about the dictatorship’s crimes, and the two should not be equated.

How historians have read it#

  1. Esteban Damián Pontoriero, “Represión y ‘aniquilamiento de la subversión’ durante los gobiernos peronistas de la década del setenta,” Sociohistórica 49 (2022). Uses decrees, military directives and congressional debates to argue that the constitutional Peronist governments militarised internal repression before the coup. Source: scielo.org.ar
  2. Julieta Pacheco, “Una aproximación a los antecedentes programáticos de la organización político-militar Montoneros,” Revista SAAP 8(1) (2014). Reconstructs Montoneros’ programme from party documents and oral interviews and concludes it was a reformist national-liberation programme inherited from the MLN, disputing accounts that derive it from the Catholic nationalism of individual militants. Source: scielo.org.ar
  3. Marina Franco, Un enemigo para la nación. Orden interno, violencia y “subversión” (Fondo de Cultura Económica, 2012). Argues that the “two demons” interpretation of the violence, which treated left and right as equally guilty, was constructed before Alfonsín’s government and that civil as well as military actors drove the exception regime.
  4. Richard Gillespie, Soldados de Perón: Los Montoneros (Grijalbo, 1987; cited edition 1998). The older study that treats the organisation’s Catholic-nationalist origins as decisive, a reading Pacheco disputes as a confusion of militants’ backgrounds with the organisation’s programme.
  5. Marcelo Novaro and Vicente Palermo, La dictadura militar 1976–1983: del golpe de Estado a la restauración democrática (Paidós, 2006). A standard account of the dictatorship and the transition, used here for the 1975 repressive turn and the destruction of the ERP’s Tucumán company.

Broad agreement covers the existence of clandestine detention, systematic torture and the institutionalised character of state terror. Live disagreement concerns the weight to give the elected Peronist governments before 1976, the interpretation of Montoneros’ politics, and how far “two demons” thinking shaped the 1984 truth-telling settlement. Those positions are not equally supported: the decree record settles the legal authorisation question, the organisation’s own documents settle what it claimed, and the programme question remains argued.

Limits of the record#

No physical archive was visited. The documents were read online, and PDF editions, audiovisual testimony and scanned collections could not be rendered into readable text, so several important records are described rather than quoted. The disappeared cannot testify. What survives consists of relatives’ accounts, released prisoners’ accounts, and institutional summaries, all shaped by later legal and political needs. The leadership documents of Montoneros, the ERP and the junta tell us what commanders said; they do not reveal what rank-and-file militants, conscripts, torturers or bystanders believed. No source used here gives a verified chain of command for an individual disappearance, a complete list of the dead, or the fate of children born in captivity. The archive is uneven: almost everything is in Spanish, much of it from Buenos Aires and leadership circles, while rural, indigenous and working-class women’s voices appear mainly as fragments inside CONADEP’s edited case files. Much of the most vivid testimony was recorded in 1984, 1985 or later, when memory, legal strategy and political loyalty could shape it. The commission’s case files are edited summaries rather than verbatim transcripts, and the records most likely to identify the fate of the disappeared were destroyed before the transition. Figures are contested and must not be treated as interchangeable: Walsh’s 1977 estimate, CONADEP’s 8,960 complaints, and later activist counts are different kinds of measurement. Timerman’s memoir, the junta’s Acta and Proclama of 24 March, and the trial recordings catalogued by Memoria Abierta were not opened, and no declassified British or American record was consulted. The Malvinas interview must not be used to suggest a moral equivalence between the dictatorship’s crimes and the conduct of the war; the veteran’s own account does not support such a claim.
