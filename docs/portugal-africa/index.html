The Carnation Revolution and the End of Portuguese Africa, 1974–1988

On 27 April 1974, two days after a coup by middle-ranking officers ended Portugal’s dictatorship, the central committee of the Mozambique Liberation Front, known as FRELIMO, issued a statement from the war. The leaders had learned of the events in Lisbon from radio broadcasts. They hailed the Portuguese democratic forces but refused any settlement short of independence, and insisted that the colonial system, not the Portuguese people, was the enemy. “We are not fighting to become Portuguese with black skins. We are fighting to affirm ourselves as Mozambicans” (FRELIMO Central Committee, “The Coup d’Etat of April 25,” 27 April 1974, English translation, translator not named). The claim that the liberation wars had forced the coup was an argument, not a demonstrated finding, but it fixed the movements’ reading of events before any negotiation began.

The coup and the movements’ reply#

Agostinho Neto, president of the Movement for the Liberation of Angola, or MPLA, answered from Lusaka on 1 May. He listed the colonial wars, inflation, emigration and military desertion as the crisis behind the coup. He rejected the federation proposed by General Spínola, the Portuguese officer who hoped to keep a common system, because Portugal would have the final say. His statement “does not agree to a federation as proposed by General Spinola, that is a federation in which Portugal would have the last word” (Agostinho Neto, statement on the Portuguese coup, 1 May 1974, MPLA translation). Neto insisted on complete independence as the only basis for cooperation, while declaring that the MPLA was prepared to negotiate. This was a public position from the movement’s own information department, not a record of its internal debate.

British residents in Lisbon and Angola remembered the same weeks differently. In reminiscences compiled by the British Historical Society of Portugal, witnesses recalled the end of censorship, armed young men stopping cars in Lisbon, the congestion at Luanda’s port as expatriates left, the RAF airlift, and the arrival of roughly a million retornados in Lisbon, some housed in former prisons and one in the Ritz. The compiler warns that these accounts were written more than thirty years afterwards and came from a narrow group of businesspeople and landowners. They are memories of decolonisation from the settler side, not contemporary documents. The MFA, the Armed Forces Movement that made the coup, appears here only as the liberators named by the movements.

Alvor and the two ceremonies#

Nine months after Neto’s statement, the Portuguese state and the three Angolan movements, the MPLA, the FNLA and UNITA, met at Alvor in the Algarve and signed an agreement on 15 January 1975. It recognised the three as “the only and the legitimate representatives of the people of Angola”, fixed a date for independence and set up a transitional government under a High Commissioner and a presidential college of three members, one from each movement. “The independence and the complete sovereignty of Angola shall be solemnly proclaimed on the 11th of November 1975” (Alvor Agreement, Article 4, signed 15 January 1975, English translation). Article 6 recorded a ceasefire already observed in practice, Article 15 made coordination rather than majority rule the college’s task, and Article 17 required two-thirds majorities. Each movement’s forces stayed where they were stationed. The settlement was built to manage rivalry rather than end it.

Neto spoke at the signing for all three movements, and his words caught the brief optimism of the moment. He called the talks an attempt to take decisions about ending colonial rule, credited the Armed Forces Movement with freeing Portugal from fascism, and thanked the Portuguese security police and armed forces for keeping calm at the meeting. “It was the system which was wrong, and now that it has been put right, Mr. President of the Republic, in Angola fraternisation is a fact between the vast majority of the Portuguese military forces and Angolan guerrillas” (Agostinho Neto, speech at the signing of the Alvor Agreement, 15 January 1975, translation). Fighting began within weeks, so the claim that there were no irremediable antagonisms has to be read against what followed.

Mozambique’s transition ran on an earlier timetable. The Lusaka agreement of 7 September 1974 had fixed 25 June 1975 for independence and created a transitional government with a Portuguese High Commissioner, a FRELIMO prime minister and nine ministers, six of them from FRELIMO. Samora Machel, FRELIMO’s president, defined its task at the investiture on 20 September as extending people’s power to areas still under colonial rule, and warned against governing by decree. “Power belongs to the people. It has been won by the people and it must be exercised and defended by the people” (Samora Machel, “Frelimo President’s Independence Message,” 20 September 1974). That was a standard for government, not evidence that it was met.

A correspondent for Sechaba, the African National Congress journal, was in the stadium at Machava for the flag ceremony. He described Portuguese marines lowering their flag, the FRELIMO flag rising, and the presence of Machel, Neto, Oliver Tambo and delegates from SWAPO and other movements. “At 0.22 hours, the date June the 25, 1975. An era of Portuguese colonialism had ended in Mozambique” (Sechaba report on the Mozambique independence celebrations, August–September 1975). The writer judged that this was a celebration by the people rather than the elite, but that was his judgement, printed in a sympathetic movement periodical. In Luanda on 11 November 1975, Neto proclaimed the People’s Republic in the First of May Square while, as the introduction to the published documents says, invading troops were no more than twenty miles from the capital. “Concretely realizing the aspirations of the great masses of our people, the People’s Republic of Angola will, under the guidance of MPLA, gradually advance toward a people’s democratic state” (Agostinho Neto, proclamation of the People’s Republic of Angola, 11 November 1975, in Road to Liberation, 1976). The transition was never a purely administrative handover.

After the flag#

The new institutions were funded and staffed by people whose names rarely appear in the leaders’ speeches. Ole Gjerstad, a journalist with the Liberation Support Movement, recorded a workers’ quarter in Luanda raising its own people’s commission and a metal shop at Soferros reopening as a cooperative after its owner fled. “When we started, a year ago, we had no money whatsoever. Our initial expenses were covered by random contributions from the population” (Elisio Napoleao of the Bairro Operario people’s commission, quoted by Ole Gjerstad, The People in Power, 1976). Gjerstad wrote from his own journal and from Angolans who let him share their experience, and he believed that people’s power had defeated the invading armies. The people he quoted were chosen by a sympathetic visitor, and many were movement activists.

In Tombali region in Guinea-Bissau, Gjerstad and Chantal Sarrazin spent three weeks with villagers who explained why they had taken up arms. “We worked hard but the Portuguese took all we had and treated us worse than animals. When the Party asked us to fight, we looked back and found we had nothing to lose” (villagers of Tchugue, quoted by Gjerstad and Sarrazin, 1975–76). They worked with political commissars and interpreters and were introduced at a public meeting, a setting that shaped what people said. Carmen Pereira, a member of the PAIGC’s Executive Committee of Struggle, described her own route from leaving Bissau in 1962, through nursing and political work on the South Front and election to the 1973 National Assembly, to peacetime work in the party secretariat. “After the fall of colonialism, I came here to Bissau. I am now working for the Secretariat-General as a member of the Party’s Executive Committee of Struggle” (Carmen Pereira, interview of June 1975). Her account of her responsibilities is self-presentation as well as evidence.

Cuito Cuanavale and its meaning#

In 1987 and 1988 the war in Angola reached a battle at Cuito Cuanavale whose outcome is still argued over. D. Kirkman, a conscript in the South African Defence Force, recalled being flown to Mavinga and driven towards what his unit called the Western Front. “Most of us couldn’t sleep, we had been briefed, we were going to the (Western Front), Cuito Cuanavale” (D. Kirkman, “7 SAI: Memories of my SADF Experiences,” posted by 2002). He wrote fourteen years later that his unit was told it had gone to stop a crossing by FAPLA, the Angolan national army, while FAPLA and the Cubans said South Africa wanted the town. Both sides removed rank insignia near the front. One memoir from one battalion cannot stand for the whole South African force.

The political meaning of the battle was made afterwards. Nelson Mandela, speaking in Cuba in 1991 while president of the African National Congress, credited the intervention with breaking the myth of white military invincibility and linked it to the unbanning of the African National Congress and to Namibian independence. “The decisive defeat of the aggressive apartheid forces destroyed the myth of the invincibility of the white oppressor” (Nelson Mandela, speech in Cuba, 26 July 1991). This was a speech of thanks to a host country, not a military assessment, and the causal chain it asserts is contested.

How historians have read it#

Edward George’s study of the Cuban intervention, built on interviews and archives from Cuba, Angola and South Africa, calls the claimed Cuban victory at Cuito Cuanavale spurious and treats the intervention as a gamble to avoid defeat. Piero Gleijeses, using American, South African and Cuban archives, reaches the opposite conclusion: that Cuba’s 1988 campaign forced Pretoria to free Namibia and helped break apartheid. The two books are the clearest opposed poles, and Mandela’s speech is a participant’s political claim rather than an arbitration between them. Gennady Shubin and Andrei Tokarev’s collection of Soviet soldiers’ accounts gives the advisers’ view instead of the generals’, and Gary Baines argues that the war is remembered through competing South African narratives. Peter Polack’s military history is useful for checking the sequence of operations against these partisan accounts. Broad agreement holds that the Lisbon coup made decolonisation unavoidable, that Alvor failed, and that Cuito Cuanavale was followed by negotiations that ended foreign military involvement in Angola and led to Namibian independence. The live disagreement is over whether the battle was decisive for South Africa’s transition, or whether other diplomatic and internal factors mattered more.

Limits of the record#

No MFA document from inside the Portuguese officer movement survives here, so the record cannot show how the decision to negotiate was taken or how Lisbon viewed Alvor. Almost every first-hand text comes from the liberation movements or their solidarity networks, with two counterweights: a retrospective club publication written largely by businesspeople and landowners, and one conscript’s memoir posted on a veterans’ website more than a decade later. There is no verified testimony here from FNLA or UNITA participants, from Portuguese conscripts in Africa, from the retornados in their own words, or from Cuban and Soviet fighters at Cuito Cuanavale. Several key PDFs are image-only scans, read as pictures of printed pages, and the Alvor text is an English translation of uncertain provenance with no Portuguese original compared. The Cuito Cuanavale material cannot establish casualties, territory held or who won; the speech, the monographs and the memoir answer different questions. The gaps would need the Arquivo 25 de Abril at Coimbra, the Arquivo Nacional Torre do Tombo, the LSM archive and Cuban and Soviet military records to close.
