José Carlos Mariátegui: Indigenous Socialism and Peruvian Reality

A research profile of the Peruvian Marxist's account of the Indian question, the land question and the socialist tradition he founded in Latin America.

José Carlos Mariátegui argued that the oppression of Peru’s Indigenous majority was not a racial problem or a shortage of education but a question of land and power. He founded a socialist party, edited two journals and wrote essays that made Peru central to Latin American Marxism. He also insisted that socialism in the Americas had to be made out of local reality rather than copied from Europe, a phrase later read both as a declaration of independence and as a move inside a specific polemic with Peruvian nationalists. This profile reports what he argued, what the research dossier records for it, and where historians and other socialists objected. Quotations from Spanish texts are given in the Spanish recorded in the dossier; where an English rendering appears, it is the dossier author’s translation, not a quotation from a published English edition.

Biography#

The institutional chronology gives José Carlos Mariátegui La Chira’s birth as 14 June 1894 in Moquegua, the son of María Amalia La Chira Ballejos and Francisco Javier Mariátegui Requejo, with two siblings, Guillermina and Julio César (Casa Museo chronology). This date is not settled by the opened sources. His own autobiographical letter of 10 January 1927 says “Nací el 95” (“I was born in ’95”), and the dossier records both dates rather than resolving the difference (Mariátegui, “Apuntes autobiográficos”). A school accident in 1902 led to a long convalescence in Lima and a permanently weak leg. In the 1927 letter he says only that in 1924 he “lost a leg” and was left in delicate health, without naming which leg; the chronology alone says the May 1924 amputation was of the right leg, after the 1902 accident had left the left one weak. No medical record was opened, so the two accounts are recorded side by side rather than reconciled (chronology; “Apuntes autobiográficos”).

He entered the newspaper trade as a copy boy at La Prensa in 1909 and moved through reporting and proof-reading posts at La Prensa, El Tiempo and La Razón, where he supported the university reform movement. In 1918 he helped found Nuestra Época, a socialist-oriented review, and was attacked by military officers after an article criticising the army; in 1919 La Razón was closed under pressure from Augusto Leguía’s government (chronology). In October 1919 the Leguía government sent him to Italy, in the chronology’s words “as a propaganda agent for Peru abroad, as a way of covering his deportation”. He spent more than two years there, married Anna Chiappe in 1921, attended the Livorno congress of the Italian Socialist Party as a correspondent, and travelled in France, Germany, Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia and Belgium. His own account compresses the period: “De fines de 1919 a mediados de 1923 viajé por Europa. Residí más de dos años en Italia, donde desposé una mujer y algunas ideas” — “From the end of 1919 to the middle of 1923 I travelled through Europe. I lived more than two years in Italy, where I married a woman and some ideas”, in the dossier’s translation (“Apuntes autobiográficos”). He returned to Peru in March 1923 and began lectures at the Universidad Popular, interviews, and what he called “the investigation of national reality according to the Marxist method” (Ibid.).

The chronology records the public career that followed. He contributed to Mundial in 1924; in October 1925 he founded Editorial Minerva and published his first book, La escena contemporánea; Amauta appeared in September 1926. A police and press campaign in 1927 led to his detention in the San Bartolomé military hospital and to the closure of Amauta and Minerva, with Amauta reappearing in December (chronology; Archivo, Amauta series record). In April 1928 he broke with Haya de la Torre, in September published “Aniversario y balance”, and on 8 October the Peruvian Socialist Party was formally constituted with him as general secretary. 7 ensayos and the fortnightly Labor appeared in November 1928. In 1929 he sent delegations to the Montevideo trade-union congress and the Buenos Aires Latin American Communist conference, and his house was raided during an alleged “Jewish plot”. He entered the Villarán clinic in March 1930 and died on 16 April 1930 (chronology). The exact publication details of the Livorno congress article, of “Elogio a Cervantes” (1916), of the short 1924 pieces and of most posthumous collected volumes were not individually verified; the dossier takes their dates from the MIA works index and the Archivo authority record (MIA works index; Archivo, Obras Completas record).

Intellectual tradition#

Mariátegui was a Marxist who argued inside, and often against, the communist movement of his time. In El problema de la tierra he called himself “a convinced and confessing Marxist”, and the 1928 party programme adopted Marxism-Leninism explicitly as the party’s method: “El marxismo-leninismo es el método revolucionario de la etapa del imperialismo y de los monopolios. El Partido Socialista del Perú, lo adopta como su método de lucha” (El problema de la tierra; “Principios programáticos”). At the same time he refused the role of system-builder. The “Advertencia” to 7 ensayos says the book is not an organic work, that none of the essays is finished, and that “Toda esta labor no es sino una contribución a la crítica socialista de los problemas y la historia del Perú” — “All this work is only a contribution to the socialist critique of the problems and history of Peru”, in the dossier’s translation. He added “Otra vez repito que no soy un crítico imparcial y objetivo”, repeating that he was not an impartial and objective critic (Mariátegui, “Advertencia”).

The familiar English contrast between an “orthodox” Comintern and a “heterodox” Mariátegui is now contested. Peter David Morgan argues that the binary hides a practical commitment: Mariátegui admired the Bolshevik party form, regarded the Third International as indispensable to anti-imperialist socialism, and became more concerned with party discipline towards the end of his life, while still disputing particular Comintern directives (Morgan 2026). Alberto Filippi reports a letter of 11 March 1930 in which Mariátegui described his work as conceived and practised far “de todo pedantismo doctrinal y de toda preocupación de ortodoxia”, free of doctrinal pedantry and concern for orthodoxy (Filippi 2017, quoting Mariátegui). The dossier’s description is therefore a Marxist who treated Marxism as a method for a semi-feudal, colonial country rather than as conclusions to be repeated, while still seeking organisational affiliation with the international communist movement. Here “semi-feudal” means what Mariátegui’s own terms record: the large landed estate and serf-like labour surviving inside a republic with no fully formed capitalist class and under imperialist economic control. He named the surviving feudal forms “latifundio and servitude”, and said the two could not be separated (El problema de la tierra).

The break with the APRA belongs to this context. The chronology dates the rupture to April 1928, after Mariátegui had worked in the APRA united front from 1926 (chronology). “Aniversario y balance”, published five months later, defines Amauta as a socialist journal and declares its “absoluta independencia frente a la idea de un Partido nacionalista, pequeño burgués y demagógico” (Mariátegui, “Aniversario y balance”). Morgan reads the editorial as an intervention in a split that had turned Haya de la Torre’s APRA into a left anti-Bolshevik force: Haya argued that Bolshevik Marxism was European and inapplicable to Latin America, and Mariátegui answered that he was “closer to reality and closer to Perú” than the apristas despite his supposed Europeanism (Morgan 2026). Aníbal Quijano’s introduction to the Biblioteca Ayacucho edition describes 1923–1928 as a period of broad coincidence between Mariátegui and the nationalist current led by Haya on the colonial question and the oligarchic character of Peruvian culture, followed by a “creciente depuración” that ended with the socialist definition of Amauta in September 1928 (Quijano, introduction).

Major contributions#

The central contribution was to recast what the dossier calls “the Indian question” as an economic and land question. The term means what Mariátegui argued it meant: not a racial, administrative, legal, moral, educational or ecclesiastical problem, but one rooted in the economy and the regime of land ownership. The second of the 7 ensayos opens “La cuestión indígena arranca de nuestra economía. Tiene sus raíces en el régimen de propiedad de la tierra” and closes its first section with the formula “El nuevo planteamiento consiste en buscar el problema indígena en el problema de la tierra” (Mariátegui, “El problema del indio”). He rejected the alternative explanations one by one and wrote that “El gamonalismo invalida inevitablemente toda ley u ordenanza de protección indígena. El hacendado, el latifundista, es un señor feudal” — gamonalismo, the local power of the landed bosses he identified as estate owners and feudal lords, defeated protective law (Ibid.). The third essay joined the two questions: “Las expresiones de la feudalidad sobreviviente son dos: latifundio y servidumbre”, and servitude could not end while the large estate remained (Mariátegui, “El problema de la tierra”). This was an argument about property and power, not a plea for charity; he treated the humanitarian and philanthropic approach as superseded. Becker records the same position in the 1929 Buenos Aires debate as the Peruvian thesis that Indian oppression was fundamentally class oppression rooted in land tenure (Becker 2006).

The second contribution was the claim that Andean communal traditions made Peru peculiarly apt for socialism without making socialism a restoration of the Inca past. In El problema de la tierra he wrote of “la supervivencia de la comunidad y de elementos de socialismo práctico en la agricultura y la vida indígenas” and described Inka economic organisation as “comunismo agrario”, citing César Ugarte’s account of collective ayllu landholding (El problema de la tierra). The Biblioteca Ayacucho text puts the claim more sharply: “El ayllu –la comunidad–, fue la célula del imperio. Los Inkas hicieron la unidad, inventaron el imperio; pero no crearon la célula”, and “Cuando la expropiación y el reparto parecen liquidar la ‘comunidad’, el socialismo indígena encuentra siempre el medio de rehacerla, mantenerla o subrogarla”. Against liberal individualisation of communal land he wrote that “La defensa de la ‘comunidad’ indígena no reposa en principios abstractos de justicia ni en sentimentales consideraciones tradicionalistas, sino en razones concretas y prácticas de orden económico y social” (Biblioteca Ayacucho edition). The October 1928 programme supplied the qualification: supporting the free resurgence of indigenous forces did “no significa en lo absoluto una romántica y anti-histórica tendencia de reconstrucción o resurrección del socialismo incaico”, because “El socialismo presupone la técnica, la ciencia, la etapa capitalistas” (“Principios programáticos”). Morgan argues that this reinterpretation of the Indigenous peasantry as a subject of socialist revolution was made in the polemic with the APRA and answered the objection that Peru’s proletariat was too small (Morgan 2026).

The third contribution is the phrase and argument known as “heroic Marxism”. The close of “Aniversario y balance” reads: “No queremos, ciertamente, que el socialismo sea en América calco y copia. Debe ser creación heróica. Tenemos que dar vida, con nuestra propia realidad, en nuestro propio lenguaje, al socialismo indoamericano. He aquí una misión digna de una generación nueva” (Mariátegui, “Aniversario y balance”). The same editorial contains the claim that “La más avanzada organización comunista, primitiva, que registra la historia, es la inkaica” (Ibid.). He returned to the theme in “Sentido heroico y creador del socialismo” of 1 February 1929, a polemic against Henri de Man’s ethical socialism: “La energía revolucionaria del socialismo no se alimenta ni de compasión ni de envidia”, and “El marxismo es totalmente extraño y contrario a estas mediocres especulaciones altruistas y filantrópicas” (Archivo transcript). Morgan reads the famous phrase not simply as a declaration of autonomy but as a tactical concession to the nationalist premise that revolutionary politics in Peru had to be Indo-American; he calls it “a significant compromise with the aprista insistence that politics in Perú be ‘Indo-American’” (Morgan 2026). The debate is whether the phrase marks a heterodox, nationally rooted Marxism or a rhetorical move inside a 1928 polemic; the opened sources support both readings, with Morgan’s historicised account the most recent.

The fourth contribution, less often stated, is his theory of myth and will. “Pesimismo de la realidad y optimismo del ideal” of 21 August 1925 adopts José Vasconcelos’s formula and argues “Creemos que puede y debe ser mejor” — that things can and should be better (Archivo typescript). A note to “El problema del indio” says: “No es la civilización, no es el alfabeto del blanco, lo que levanta el alma del indio. Es el mito, es la idea de la revolución socialista. La esperanza indígena es absolutamente revolucionaria” (Mariátegui, “El problema del indio”, note 1). Jeffery R. Webber reads this as a “romantic Marxist” thread, a utopian-revolutionary dialectic between the pre-capitalist past and the socialist future that answered the evolutionist and economistic orthodoxies of the period. Only Webber’s abstract and endnotes were opened; the full article was behind a paywall (Webber 2015, abstract and notes only).

Analysis of capitalism#

Mariátegui treated Peruvian capitalism as weak, dependent and entangled with surviving feudal relations rather than as a fully formed industrial system. In El problema de la tierra he stated that “en el Perú no hemos tenido en cien años de república, una verdadera clase burguesa, una verdadera clase capitalista”, and that the old feudal class, “camuflada o disfrazada de burguesía republicana”, had kept its positions (El problema de la tierra). The colonial economy had been organised around mining and forced labour rather than agriculture, destroying the human capital on which an agrarian economy depended; the republic left the great estates intact while formally abolishing communal property, so that liberal law served the latifundio rather than the peasantry (Ibid.). His comparison of coast and sierra mattered here: coastal agriculture had moved towards capitalist technique while the highland latifundio remained feudal and technically stagnant. In “Sumaria revisión histórica”, appended to the second essay from the 1928 Labor text and the January 1929 The Nation article, he described mining under American companies as a wage regime with low pay, negligible safety and the enganche system of debt bondage (Mariátegui, “El problema del indio”).

At the level of the world economy, his 1928 programme described contemporary economics as international and capitalism as having reached the stage of monopoly, finance capital and imperialist wars over markets and raw materials. Imperialism, he wrote, did not allow semi-colonial countries an independent programme of nationalisation and industrialisation; it forced them into specialisation and monoculture — “Petróleo, cobre, azúcar, algodón, en el Perú” — producing crises because national production was determined by the world capitalist market (“Principios programáticos”). La escena contemporánea (1925) applied the same frame to contemporary Europe; its seven sections cover the “Biología del fascismo”, the crisis of democracy, the Russian Revolution, the crisis of socialism, revolution and the intelligentsia, the “Mensaje de Oriente”, and antisemitism (MIA contents). In “Aniversario y balance” he attacked the aprista and arielista tendency to explain imperialism as a clash of cultures. Morgan translates his argument as the claim that “Oppositions of language, race and spirit have no decisive meaning” and that Latin America’s place in the capitalist order was that of simple colonies (Morgan 2026, translating and discussing the passage; “Aniversario y balance”). The Spanish wording of that sentence was not confirmed in the MIA text opened here, so it is available only in Morgan’s translation. He did not deny capitalism’s creative force: in the 1929 polemic he wrote that Marx’s work carried “cierto acento de admiración de la obra capitalista” and that the proletariat succeeded the bourgeoisie in the civilising task (Archivo, “Sentido heroico”).

Analysis of socialism#

Mariátegui wrote no blueprint of a socialist society and said so. The 7 ensayos were a “contribution to the socialist critique”, provisional essays he expected to revise for as long as he lived (Mariátegui, “Advertencia”). What he left was a programme and a direction. The nine “Principios programáticos” of the Peruvian Socialist Party affirm the international character of the economy and of the proletarian movement, adapt party practice to national circumstances while subordinating them to world history, identify monopoly capitalism as the stage whose revolutionary method is Marxism-Leninism, and state that Peru’s pre-capitalist economy cannot be liberated under a bourgeois regime tied to imperialism and the gamonal-clerical feudal remnant. They say that socialism finds elements of a solution to the agrarian question both in surviving communities and in large agricultural enterprises, with collective management extended where that form already prevails and individual management tolerated where yanaconaje or smallholding makes it appropriate. The document ends with the party as “la vanguardia del proletariado” (“Principios programáticos”).

The immediate demands give the programme its concrete content: freedom of working-class association, meeting and press; the right to strike; abolition of the road conscription known as conscripción vial; social insurance and state assistance; enforcement of accident, women’s and children’s labour and eight-hour agricultural day laws; a seven-hour day in mines and unhealthy work; wage increases tied to the cost of living; effective abolition of forced or unpaid labour and of the semi-slavery regime in the eastern mountains; granting of latifundio land to communities for distribution among their members; expropriation without compensation of convent and religious-congregation estates in favour of communities; and free education at all levels (Ibid.). This is a platform of transition and class power, not a description of a finished socialist order.

His positive ideal is named in “Aniversario y balance” as “socialismo indoamericano”, to be made “con nuestra propia realidad, en nuestro propio lenguaje” rather than copied from Europe (Mariátegui, “Aniversario y balance”). Morgan cautions that the same text insists socialism is a global movement from which no country in the orbit of Western civilisation can withdraw, so the national and global formulations sit together in an unresolved but purposeful tension (Morgan 2026). As an ethical theory his socialism was deliberately anti-humanitarian in the narrow sense. “Socialismo ético, pseudocristiano, humanitario” was, he wrote, at best a lyrical exercise of a tired bourgeoisie; the revolutionary energy of socialism came from class struggle and a “moral de productores”, not compassion or envy (Archivo, “Sentido heroico”).

Several bibliographic points remain unsettled. Both 7 ensayos de interpretación de la realidad peruana and Siete ensayos de interpretación de la realidad peruana appear in the opened editions, and no publisher record was opened that establishes which form was used on the first-edition title page (MIA index; Biblioteca Ayacucho edition). The first-edition date is 1928 in the MIA edition note and November 1928 in the Casa Museo chronology; a different web edition encountered in search results printed “Primera Edición: Biblioteca Amauta, Lima (Perú), 1918”, plainly inconsistent with the other records and not used as evidence. A claim that exactly four of the seven essays form a recognised group could not be tied to any publisher, archive or scholarly source opened here; the seven verified titles are “Esquema de la evolución económica”, “El problema del indio”, “El problema de la tierra”, “El proceso de la instrucción pública”, “El factor religioso”, “Regionalismo y centralismo” and “El proceso de la literatura”. Finally, “Definición del socialismo” could not be verified as a work by Mariátegui: no such title appears in the Archivo’s list of Obras Completas volumes or in the MIA works index. The verified related title is “Sentido heroico y creador del socialismo” (Mundial, 1 February 1929) (Archivo).

Recommended reading#

These selections follow the dossier’s own list; each line restates what the work contributes to Mariátegui’s argument.

  • 7 ensayos de interpretación de la realidad peruana (1928) — The essential work: seven linked essays showing his method applied to economy, land, Indigenous life, education, religion, regionalism and literature.
  • “El problema del indio” (essay II of 7 ensayos, 1928) — The clearest statement of his claim that the Indigenous question is a land and power question, with his own evidence on gamonalismo.
  • “El problema de la tierra” (essay III of 7 ensayos, 1928) — Where the ayllu and the “comunismo agrario” argument are worked out in detail, including his rejection of a romantic restoration of Inka socialism.
  • “Aniversario y balance” (Amauta 17, September 1928) — The short text containing “creación heroica” and the Inka-communism claim, best read alongside Morgan’s historicised account.
  • “Principios programáticos del Partido Socialista” (October 1928) — The nearest thing he wrote to a socialist programme, with concrete immediate demands and a clear statement of method.

Selected lectures and interviews#

No sound or film recording of Mariátegui was found in the opened sources, so no audiovisual item can be listed here. What exists is a documented lecture series and written transcripts. The chronology records that he began a course at the Universidad Popular in June 1923 under the title “Historia de la Vida Mundial” and concluded it in January 1924 (chronology). The archive holds typescripts of individual lectures: “La crisis mundial y el proletariado peruano”, dated 15 June 1923; “La revolución rusa”, 13 July 1923; and “La revolución alemana”, 20 July 1923, each catalogued in the Fondo José Carlos Mariátegui (Archivo subject results). His autobiographical note confirms that after returning in 1923 he spoke at the Federación de Estudiantes and the Universidad Popular as part of his Marxist study of national reality (Mariátegui, “Apuntes autobiográficos”). These descriptions come from catalogue records and typescripts, not from recordings.

The chronology also records that in March 1923 he was interviewed by Ángela Ramos for the review Variedades, but the interview text was not opened here, so no content is claimed for it (chronology). The “Apuntes autobiográficos” is a letter written in response to a request from Enrique Espinoza, not a spoken interview; it is the nearest thing to an authoritative self-description that was opened (MIA).

Intellectual influences#

The primary evidence for influences is Mariátegui’s own citation practice. In the “Advertencia” to 7 ensayos he placed his method under Nietzsche’s patronage, saying his thought and life formed one process and that he wanted to “meter toda mi sangre en mis ideas” (Mariátegui, “Advertencia”). In the same book he quotes and argues with González Prada, José A. Encinas, Dora Mayer, César Ugarte, Luis E. Valcárcel, José Vasconcelos and Georges Sorel; he attributes to Sorel a study of monasteries as an early form of capitalist enterprise, and to Valcárcel the image of the land as common mother in the Tawantinsuyu (Mariátegui, “El problema del indio”; “El problema de la tierra”). In “Pesimismo de la realidad y optimismo del ideal” he engages Vasconcelos directly and also Ortega y Gasset, Hans Vaihinger and Giuseppe Rensi (Archivo transcript). In “Sentido heroico” he argues against Henri de Man’s ethical socialism and quotes Adriano Tilgher on Marx’s discovery of the proletariat (Archivo transcript). Marx, Engels and Lenin are present throughout, with Lenin’s Imperialism and the Comintern’s national and colonial theses forming the framework of the 1928–1929 party documents (“Principios programáticos”; Becker 2006). Filippi adds that Mariátegui read the Italian Marxist and liberal tradition — Sorel, Gobetti, Croce — alongside Lenin and Spengler, but this is an interpreter’s summary rather than a reading list in his own hand (Filippi 2017).

Influence and legacy#

The archive records the institutional afterlife of the journals. Amauta ran to 32 numbers between 1926 and 1930 and has been digitised; Labor ran to ten issues and a bulletin in 1928–1929 (Archivo, Amauta; Archivo, Labor). A letter of 6 May 1929 from Abraham Valdez in La Paz thanks Mariátegui for sending “7 Ensayos y colección de Labor”, showing that the books and periodicals circulated among socialist militants beyond Peru while he was alive (Archivo correspondence).

Scholarly accounts of his legacy divide along the lines of the historiography Morgan describes. The older “Latin American Marxism” paradigm treated him as the thinker who made Marxism congruent with Latin American reality; a newer current emphasises his cosmopolitan and global framing, and Morgan proposes reading both as responses to anti-Bolshevism (Morgan 2026). Filippi places him in a wider debate among Latin American socialists about the Second and Third Internationals and about the name “Socialist” versus “Communist” in 1928 (Filippi 2017). Quijano’s introduction credits Mariátegui’s insistence on the place of the Indigenous community in any socialist revolution with a lasting subversive effect on Eurocentric social thought, while noting that later “indigenista” and decolonial currents developed different answers (Quijano, introduction). Webber argues that Mariátegui’s romantic Marxism remains useful for Indigenous and left critics of extractive capitalism in the Andes, and reads the Aymara activist Felipe Quispe as a contemporary instance of that combined class and Indigenous politics (Webber 2015, abstract and notes only). These are interpretations of influence, not measurements of it; no circulation figures for 7 ensayos were verified from a publisher record opened here.

Criticisms and controversies#

The strongest contemporary orthodox Marxist criticism is recorded by Quijano. He reports that V. Miroshevsky published in 1942 a piece originally issued in Moscow and reproduced in Dialéctica, the journal of the Communist Party of Cuba, no. 1 of May–June 1942, accusing Mariátegui of being a “populista”, a “representante de la democracia revolucionaria”, a “propagandista del socialismo pequeño burgués” and an advocate of a “revolución campesina socialista”, because he treated the Indigenous community as a possible starting point for socialist reorganisation of agrarian structure (Quijano, introduction, reporting Miroshevsky). Quijano rejects that reading as a “banal tergiversación” and argues that Mariátegui’s position was close to Lenin’s on transition in societies with large pre-capitalist sectors, but he concedes that Mariátegui “estaba limitado por el horizonte del conocimiento científico de su tiempo”, sharing with Haya de la Torre, Castro Pozo and Valcárcel the five-modes-of-production schema and the thesis of Inka “primitive communism” (Ibid.). The Miroshevsky article itself was not opened, so the accusation is reported through Quijano, not quoted from the original. The full text of Quijano’s Reencuentro y debate (1981) was also not opened.

A second line of criticism came from the Comintern at the time. Becker shows that at the June 1929 Buenos Aires conference the Peruvian delegation was criticised by Victorio Codovilla and other delegates for “deviance” on the Indian question and for insisting that Peru had a national reality different from Argentina or Mexico. Codovilla rejected the Peruvian thesis that Indian oppression was fundamentally class oppression rooted in land tenure, and the Peruvians’ application for Comintern membership was turned down because of their divergent positions (Becker 2006). Becker also documents the reciprocal criticism: Mariátegui and his collaborators regarded Codovilla’s handling as arrogant and insensitive to an Indian and agrarian society (Ibid.). Morgan qualifies the usual story by arguing that Mariátegui’s differences with Comintern officials should not be inflated into a rejection of the International itself (Morgan 2026).

The criticism from an Indigenous standpoint is structural rather than personal. Here indigenismo means the current of writing and politics about Indigenous people conducted by non-Indigenous intellectuals; Becker’s question makes the distance explicit, asking whether Mariátegui, “as an indigenista intellectual … was not an Indian but spoke on behalf of Indians”, and concludes that in the Buenos Aires debate “Missing from this equation, however, are the desires and goals of the Indigenous peoples themselves” (Becker 2006). He notes that Mariátegui echoed Valcárcel’s phrase that “the Indigenous proletariat awaits its Lenin”, which places agency in an outside, urban, mestizo leadership, and that neither the Comintern nor the Peruvian socialists consulted Indigenous people about the proposed Indian Republic (Ibid.). Becker records the Shuar intellectual Ampam Karakras’s later insistence that Indigenous peoples in Ecuador were nationalities because of their distinct identities, cultures, histories, languages and spiritual practices — a position that indicates how the terms of the debate shifted after Mariátegui’s death (Ibid., reporting Karakras). Webber’s defence runs the other way: he argues that Mariátegui’s “romantic” reading of the pre-capitalist communal past was a productive heresy against economistic Marxism and can help interpret contemporary Indigenous resistance to extractivism (Webber 2015, abstract only). The dossier records the disagreement rather than resolving it.

A further dispute concerns his own self-presentation. Morgan notes that “Aniversario y balance” is “a complex and sometimes contradictory text” in which Mariátegui both de-territorialised Marxism as a universal method and then conceded that legitimacy in Peru depended on Indigenous and national origin; the “heroic creation” phrase is for Morgan evidence of that concession, not a simple manifesto of independence (Morgan 2026). Filippi’s account of Mariátegui as free of “orthodoxia” and Quijano’s account of him as limited by his epoch are compatible only if one distinguishes method from historical knowledge; that distinction is not explicit in all the literature.

Related thinkers#

The dossier records these relationships; it does not establish links to other profiles in this collection, so none are made here.

  • Antonio Gramsci — a structural comparison made by Filippi and Masek rather than by Mariátegui himself; Filippi refuses to call either the anticipator of the other. Masek’s article page was blocked by an anti-bot check and only its search-result abstract was seen, so it is not cited as evidence (Filippi 2017).
  • Georges Sorel, José Vasconcelos, Friedrich Nietzsche and Henri de Man — the relationship runs from them to Mariátegui, who quotes or argues with them in the texts opened here.
  • González Prada, Luis E. Valcárcel and César Ugarte — Peruvian precursors whose evidence and vocabulary he adopted while correcting what he saw as their moralism or literary idealism.
  • Víctor Raúl Haya de la Torre — first collaboration in a united front, then a polemic over whether Marxism could be legitimate in Latin America (chronology; Morgan 2026).
  • Victorio Codovilla and the Comintern — affiliation sought and terms contested (Becker 2006).
  • Aníbal Quijano, Michael Löwy and Jeffery R. Webber — interpretive links: each later wrote about Mariátegui, and none can be treated as his intellectual predecessor.
