Alec Nove: Feasible Socialism and the Limits of Planning

A research profile of the economic historian's account of how actually existing socialist economies worked and what a feasible alternative would require.

Alec Nove (1915–1994) spent his career asking what a socialism that could actually work would look like. His term for it was “feasible socialism”: in his words, “a state of affairs which could exist in some major part of the developed world within the lifetime of a child already conceived, without our having to make or accept implausible or far-fetched assumptions about society, human beings and the economy”, which also required “the dominance of social ownership in the economy, together with political and economic democracy” (Nove, 1991). By central planning he meant the direction of output and investment from a centre rather than by enterprises deciding what to make and buy for themselves. He defined it from the Soviet system he had studied, where the planning body Gosplan depended on information held by ministries. This profile sets out what he argued, what his texts support, and where economists and socialists objected.

Biography#

The existing chapter on planning, incentives and shortage covers how plans were made, how workers and managers answered them, and why goods remained scarce; this profile adds Nove’s economic explanation of why that pattern recurred and the alternative he sketched in response.

Alexander Novakovsky was born into a Russian Jewish family in Petrograd on 24 November 1915 and died in hospital in Voss, Norway, on 15 May 1994, after a heart attack while on holiday (University of Glasgow, World Changing; Brown and Cairncross, 1997; Wallace, The Independent, 20 May 1994). His father, Jacob, had been a Menshevik, was imprisoned under the tsars and again by the Bolsheviks, and left Russia in 1922; his mother, a doctor, brought Alec to Britain to join him the following year. The family adopted the name Nove, already used by relatives in Manchester. He went to school at King Alfred School in London, graduated from the London School of Economics in 1936 with an upper second in the B.Sc. (Econ.), joined the Territorials in 1939 and served in the Royal Signals and then intelligence until 1946, finishing as a major.

He joined the Civil Service in 1947, working on price control and export targets at the Board of Trade. An attachment from 1952 to 1954 to Glasgow’s Department of Soviet Studies turned him towards full-time scholarship; he returned to Whitehall, moved to the Treasury’s Economic Section in 1956, and in 1958 became Reader in Russian Social and Economic Studies at the LSE. In 1963 he took the James Bonar Chair of Economics at Glasgow and the directorship of the Institute of Soviet and East European Studies. He retired from those duties in 1982 but remained an honorary senior research fellow until his death. He was elected FBA in 1978, FRSE and an honorary fellow of the LSE in 1982, and received honorary doctorates, including one at Stockholm days before he died; a chair in Russian and East European Studies was named for him at Glasgow in 1996 (Glasgow biography; Brown and Cairncross).

Biography matters here because Nove drew method from these institutions. He said that his Board of Trade years taught him what governments could and could not do, and the British Academy memoir links his later scepticism about central control directly to that experience (Brown and Cairncross; Nove, 1991). His family history gave him Russian without making him a communist. In his own preface he wrote: “Brought up in a social-democratic environment, son of a Menshevik who was arrested by the Bolsheviks, I inherited a somewhat critical view of Soviet reality: if this really was socialism, I would prefer to be elsewhere. (Luckily, I was elsewhere!)” (Nove, 1991).

Intellectual tradition#

Nove worked in the political-economy tradition of Adam Smith and the British civil-service economics of the postwar years, not in Marxism. The British Academy memoir places him in “a tradition which is now in danger of being squeezed out of university departments” and records that “his own political beliefs were firmly in the social democratic (or democratic socialist) tradition. He was a Labour voter” (Brown and Cairncross, 1997). He spent much of his career rebutting both Marxists and dogmatic free-market economists. In the preface to The Economics of Feasible Socialism Revisited he said he was “ill-disposed towards those latter-day Marxists who airily ascribe all the world’s evils to ‘capitalism’”, and equally disinclined to excuse “the Chicago school, whose belief in ‘free enterprise’ seems quite unaffected by the growth of giant bureaucratic corporations” (Nove, 1991).

He did not accept the label of socialist advocate without qualification. The 1983 addendum says: “My object was not to advocate socialism, but rather to act as an advocate for socialism in the legal sense of the word ‘advocate’: that is to say, I put the best case I can for my client, without necessarily being committed to all my own arguments.” In the 1991 preface he repeated the image, adding that the best he could hope for was the Scottish verdict of “not proven” (Nove, 1991). A 1972 article has him writing, “for want of a better phrase, I would call [my standpoint] ‘feasible socialist economics’”; this wording comes from a passage reproduced with citation in a compiled PDF, and the original article was not opened (compiled quotations). Other writers called his position “market socialism”; Nove answered in New Left Review that he was “not a believer in laissez-faire” and that the line between plan and market was not the line between bespoke and ready-made tailoring (Nove, “Markets and Socialism”, 1987).

Major contributions#

His first contribution was to describe how the Soviet economy actually worked rather than how its constitution said it worked. The Soviet Economy (Praeger, 1961) was written, its preface says, to introduce the non-specialist to “those features of the Soviet economy which particularly distinguish it from that of western countries” (Nove, 1961; Routledge Revivals record). It was radically rewritten as The Soviet Economic System (1977; second edition 1980, third 1986), and the British Academy memoir identifies it as the book that explained “how the Soviet economy actually worked” (Brown and Cairncross, 1997). Nove showed that Gosplan depended on information held by the ministries, so that “in practice the sheer volume of work and of decisions in Gosplan places very considerable powers in the hands of the ministries”, and he coined “centralized pluralism” for the tug-of-war between ministries and the planning committee. The Academy memoir judges that this “was stretching the concept of pluralism too far” but that it captured something the totalitarian paradigm missed (Brown and Cairncross, quoting Nove’s primary text).

The second contribution was the analysis of plan-fulfilment incentives and their consequences. In The Economics of Feasible Socialism Revisited Nove cited a Soviet calculation of between 2,700 and 3,600 million plan indicators set at all levels in a year, 70 per cent of them concerning material allocation and supply. Managers, he wrote, are judged on “the success indicators of the plan”, “frequently to the detriment of quality and satisfying user needs”; unreliable supplies breed “self-supply” (wasteful duplication), informal networks, tolkachi or expediters, and corruption (Nove, 1991). These figures are Nove’s citations of Soviet sources, not independently verified measurements. This is the mechanism that the existing chapter documents from workers’ letters, party reports and the Stakhanov record; Nove supplies the economist’s account of why it recurs.

The third contribution was the concept of feasible socialism, defined in the 1991 book in the two sentences quoted at the opening of this profile: what could exist in a developed country within a child’s lifetime without far-fetched assumptions, and social ownership combined with political and economic democracy (Nove, 1991). The publisher calls the 1983 book “path-breaking” and notes translations into Chinese, Hungarian and the Soviet Union (Taylor & Francis, The Economics of Feasible Socialism).

The fourth was his account of where the line between plan and market falls. In New Left Review in 1987 he told Ernest Mandel: “Where Mandel goes wrong is in drawing the line of demarcation between plan and market, between ex ante and ex post.” Forward anticipation of demand occurs inside capitalist firms as well as in plans; “Surely the line between plan and market does not run between bespoke and ready-made tailoring!” The market exists “when goods are made for sale, for exchange and not for use”, whatever the degree of vertical integration inside a firm (Nove, 1987). In the 1991 appendix he conceded that “strains and contradictions would arise on the boundary-line between plan and market, between central and local decisions” and called them “quite unavoidable” (Nove, 1991). This is his own formulation. The word “Rubicon” was not found in any Nove text opened for the research record, so this profile does not attribute such a phrase to him.

The fifth contribution was a critique of the standard microeconomic case for public ownership. Efficiency Criteria for Nationalised Industries (1973) argued that marginal-cost pricing, drawn from a model of perfect competition, was ambiguous when quality, service and network effects mattered; Cairncross’s memoir recalls his railway examples, in which dropping every loss-making service can destroy the profitable system as a whole (Brown and Cairncross, 1997). Nove was, in other words, a critic of both command planning and the textbook market.

Analysis of capitalism#

Nove’s case against capitalism was empirical and moral rather than derived from the labour theory of value. He found “the present distribution of wealth offensive, especially as it seems to bear so little relationship to any real contribution to welfare in any recognisable sense”, while conceding that “it is not by any means obvious that the poor are poor because successful businessmen make a great deal of money” (Nove, 1991). He thought the “essential ideals of democratic socialism look positively virtuous if contrasted with the narrow selfishness on which the alternative ‘Chicago’ philosophy is based” and asked, “What is the difference between ‘greed’ and profit maximisation?” (Nove, 1991).

His objections were to particular mechanisms. Unregulated markets produce unemployment and wasteful bankruptcy; oligopoly and giant corporations escape market discipline; externalities go unpriced; financial markets divert talent into what he called, quoting Keynes, “the casino”; and deregulated credit can fuel inflation while productive investment stagnates (Nove, 1991). In the appendix he attacked the “abstract model-builders” of professional journals and preferred even Friedman, who “at least advocates action in the real world (even though I believe the action he advocates is wrong)”. Against “methodological individualism” he insisted that “Pace, Mrs Thatcher, there is such a thing as society” and that sub-units such as a city, an orchestra or a transport system are more than the sum of their members (Nove, 1991).

But he did not treat the market as simply an artefact of capitalism. Competition was “a consequence and pre-condition of choice” and a stimulus to laggards; “all practicable alternatives are worse” was his blunt summary in the debate with Boris Frankel (Radical Philosophy 39 debate, 1988). In 1984, according to the British Academy memoir, he agreed with Friedman about the weaknesses of Soviet planning but argued that Friedman’s market rested on unrealistic assumptions about externalities, oligopoly, unemployment, economies of scale and the relation of property to merit. Their deepest disagreement concerned what should be excluded from markets altogether; Nove concluded that a market economy might be “a necessary condition for human freedom, but it is certainly not a sufficient condition” (Brown and Cairncross, 1997).

Analysis of socialism#

Nove rejected both the Soviet model and the Marxist blueprint. In the preface he wrote that “the functional logic of centralised planning ‘fits’ far too easily into the practice of centralised despotism”, and that “‘Stalinist’ Soviet socialism (and indeed Leninism, too) gave too exclusive an emphasis on the so-called collective, and neglected the rights and interests of the individual” (Nove, 1991). His positive model was explicitly not a blueprint: “In no circumstances should the pages that follow be seen as some sort of blueprint… They are no more than a sketch, a try-out of a few ideas” (Nove, 1991). He also admitted a gap in the argument. Asked how to get from the existing world to his socialism, he wrote: “The reason is: I really have no idea how to bridge it!” (Nove, 1991).

The sketch assumed scarcity, the continued existence of the state, a division between governors and governed, and the possibility of abuse of power. He therefore wanted institutions that limited it, including multiparty democracy, a free press, separation of powers and “permanent vigilance, permanent reform” (Nove, 1991; Brown and Cairncross, 1997). For ownership and control he proposed five legal categories of producer: centrally controlled state corporations; publicly or socially owned enterprises with full autonomy and management responsible to the workforce; enterprises owned or administered by the workforce, such as cooperatives; private enterprise subject to limits; and individuals such as freelance journalists or plumbers. Electricity grids, rail networks, large steelworks and oil complexes would remain centrally directed because only the centre could see the whole system; the bulk of the economy would be decentralised, with enterprises contracting with one another, competing for customers and obtaining their own inputs (Nove, 1991; Nove, 1987). This division belongs to the argument surveyed in economic calculation and the price question: he thought prices and contracts necessary for most decisions, but not for the few interconnected systems that had to be planned as a whole.

Major investment would be decided centrally, with credit distributed through a state banking system alongside other sources of finance; “adjustment-to-demand” investment would be handled by management; and competition would be policed by “a species of socialist anti-trust legislation” (Nove, 1991). Health, education and infrastructure would not be left to the market. He rejected the “withering away of the state” as “inherently incorrect” and argued that the “associated producers” could not decide where every consignment went: “In no society can an elected assembly decide by 115 votes to 73 where to allocate ten tonnes of leather, or whether to produce another 100 tonnes of sulphuric acid” (Nove, 1991). He also rejected abundance and the “New Man” as assumptions that removed the very problems a working economy had to solve. The market’s weakness was that it was retrospective: “Markets provide (imperfect) information about what has already happened. Did not someone use as a parallel a driver who looks out of the rear window at the road behind him?” His answer was a compromise: “Total control is impossible… Total de-control is also unworkable, and certainly cannot be seen as a socialist solution” (Nove, 1991).

Recommended reading#

These selections are starting points rather than a complete account of his output. The annotations restate his case rather than add claims to it.

  • The Economics of Feasible Socialism (1983), revised as The Economics of Feasible Socialism Revisited (1991) — the central statement of what he thought a working socialism would require, and the source of his ex ante/ex post argument.
  • An Economic History of the USSR 1917–1991 (1992) — his mature account of how the Soviet economy developed and why he judged its conclusion a tragedy rather than a simple crime.
  • The Soviet Economic System (1977, revised 1986) — the institutional analysis of Gosplan, ministries, success indicators and shortage on which the later theoretical work rests.
  • “Markets and Socialism” (New Left Review, 1987) — the clearest short statement of his boundary between plan and market, in direct reply to Ernest Mandel.
  • Was Stalin Really Necessary? (1965) — the essay that made his qualification of the Stalinist industrialization record famous and shows his method of weighing constraints against choices.

Intellectual influences#

Nove’s own acknowledgments give the clearest evidence. In the 1991 preface he thanks Wlodzimierz Brus, Agota Dezsenyi-Gueullette, Michael Ellmann, Radoslav Selucky and Ljubo Sirc for reading parts of the book, and names Xavier Richet, Marie Lavigne, Jan Elster and seminar participants in Paris, Amsterdam and Oxford (Nove, 1991). In the model itself he credits André Gorz on diseconomies of scale and “small is beautiful” (derived from Schumacher), Selucky on social ownership and tripartite supervision of monopolies, Coase, Williamson and Richardson on the nature of the firm, Tinbergen on the necessary level of centralisation, Schumpeter on the limits of contract-based society, and Loasby on perfect foresight and freedom of choice. Popper supplies the appendix on contradiction; Bogdanov’s “tektologiya” is treated as a precursor of organisation theory (Nove, 1991).

Among economists he engaged, Barone and Mises are credited with seeing “the genuine difficulties” of planning clearly, while Oskar Lange, Abba Lerner, Maurice Dobb and Maurice Dickinson are discussed as producers of models that did not, in his view, solve the problem of a working socialist economy (Nove, 1991). Marx and Engels are the central negative influence: he argued that they supplied no operational account of allocation without prices, and that Kautsky and later Marxists compounded the error. His father’s Menshevism and his own social-democratic upbringing are named as influences on his political starting point (Nove, 1991; Brown and Cairncross, 1997).

Influence and legacy#

The most durable institutional legacy is the body of work around Soviet Studies at Glasgow. The Glasgow biography credits him with creating the first peer-reviewed journal devoted to the economic, political and social policy systems of the Soviet Union and with advising successive British ambassadors in the early 1980s (Glasgow biography). The British Academy memoir records the 1983 Chequers seminar, the 1989 embassy fellowship and his conversations with Russian economists from Leonid Abalkin to Yegor Gaidar; it quotes Gorbachev greeting him in 1989 with “So you are the man who knows more than anyone else about the Soviet economy!” (Brown and Cairncross, 1997).

Scholarly influence is documented in the debate the book provoked. The publisher says the 1983 book was “immensely influential in the West” and translated into China, Hungary and the Soviet Union (Taylor & Francis). Wlodzimierz Brus opened his 1985 New Left Review review by calling it “an important landmark in contemporary writing on socialism”, though those first lines were seen in a search preview rather than the full text (NLR record). Ernest Mandel, Chris Harman, Boris Frankel, Pat Devine and David McNally wrote replies or critiques, treated in the next section. Ian D. Thatcher’s 1995 bibliographical tribute in Europe-Asia Studies 47(8), pp. 1383–1410, catalogues several hundred articles and reviews (Crossref record). Stephen White’s obituary reports that at the 1990 World Congress of Soviet and East European Studies he was introduced as “a legend” (Slavonica 1(1), 1994); the text was seen as a search-index preview only and the PDF endpoint returned HTML, so the word is reported as preview text rather than a checked quotation.

His later reputation also rests on his criticism of the post-Soviet transition. In a 1992 Harriman Institute Forum paper he borrowed Bukharin’s title “Economics of the Transition Period” and argued that Russia could not rely on a market mechanism “that has yet to be created” while decline accelerated; the state would have to intervene under emergency conditions (quoted in Brown and Cairncross, 1997; the original was not opened). Bill Wallace’s obituary records that Nove welcomed perestroika but came to regret “the unbridled rush to a chaotic semi-market that would enrich some, but impoverish others” (Wallace, 1994). Mark Harrison’s review of the final edition praises its “encyclopaedic” information base and “penetrating analysis”, while noting that it neglects the economic history of the regions and republics before 1987 (Harrison review).

Criticisms and controversies#

The Marxist objection was that Nove had conceded too much to the market. Ernest Mandel’s reply in New Left Review I/159 began by rejecting Nove’s claim that Marxism was irrelevant to building socialism and insisted that planning meant direct allocation ex ante, against market allocation ex post; both had existed historically, he wrote, and “Both are therefore quite ‘feasible’” (Mandel, 1986, first portion opened). Mandel argued that the Soviet distortions Nove described arose from backwardness, isolation and bureaucratic mismanagement rather than from planning as such, and that Nove ignored two centuries of market economies prone to mass unemployment (Mandel, “The Myth of Market Socialism”, 1988, first portion opened). He summarised their disagreement: “Alec Nove’s answer is a categorical ‘no’ to the first question, and a rather hesitant ‘no’ to the second one.” Nove replied in 1987 that “it would be foolish to ignore Soviet experience because of a prior decision to classify it as ‘not socialist’” (Nove, 1987).

Chris Harman, writing from International Socialism in 1989, argued that Nove selected arguments from incompatible traditions and relied on the neoclassical notion that price signals guide firms to meet needs. Harman accepted that Nove was “quite rightly criticis[ing] the proliferation of bureaucratic penpushers and form fillers in the Eastern European states” but said he should “be equally critical of the similar proliferation in the finance sectors of the ‘market’ economies” (Harman, 1989). He treated Nove’s own admission that today’s prices are a poor guide to scarcities six years ahead as “a devastating blow to his whole case”. Allin Cottrell and W. Paul Cockshott later argued that Nove’s critique, though offered in favour of market socialism rather than Austrian capitalism, was “mutually reinforcing” with the neo-Austrian case, and noted that by the early 1990s Mandel was almost the only writer still defending socialist planning (Cottrell and Cockshott, 1993).

The Radical Philosophy debate added a different Marxist criticism. Boris Frankel argued that “Nove’s ‘feasible socialism’ is almost as conservative as Crosland’s Fabian image of socialism which was put forward in the 1950s”, that Nove failed to explain why capitalist societies cannot survive on services, and that his proposals neglected unpaid labour, women’s work and welfare. Nove replied that “all practicable alternatives are worse” and called Frankel’s “conscious, deliberately planned exchanges” an unworkable fantasy. Chris Arthur, in the same debate, thought the argument about productive and unproductive labour was a distraction from the question of socialism. Michael Ratner, writing after 1991, broadly agreed with Nove’s diagnosis but argued that he and Devine underestimate the possibilities of computer-assisted democratic planning and that market relations would in any case persist after a socialist victory (Ratner).

From the right and from mainstream economics, the criticisms were different. Nove’s own appendix imagines the neoliberal case: that his mix of plan and market would be inconsistent with efficiency, destroy incentives and degenerate into serfdom; he answers that an age of giant corporations gives few people real enterprise, and that Hitler rose in a capitalist state (Nove, 1991). János Kornai’s The Road to a Free Economy (1990) argued that there was no third way between Soviet-type socialism and Western capitalism; Nove conceded “an element of what Kornai has called ‘naiveté’ in early models of so-called market socialism”, including his own, but rejected Kornai’s opposition to progressive taxation and his stress on private accumulation (Nove, 1991). Alec Cairncross judged The Economics of Feasible Socialism highly effective as a polemic against utopian socialism but “much more sketchy” as a picture of a workable system, with “no mention of banks or any discussion of a capital market”; he also thought “centralized pluralism” stretched the concept of pluralism too far (Brown and Cairncross, 1997). Nove’s 1991 edition did add discussion of alternative sources of credit, a capital market and the dangers of financial speculation, which Stephen Louw notes as a change from the earlier edition (Louw, Transformation 21, 1993). Martin Cave’s review in the Economic Journal 93(372), December 1983, pp. 937–939, and Nicolas Spulber’s in the Journal of Political Economy 92(1), 1984, pp. 170–174, are verified as records (Crossref record for Cave; DOI record for Spulber); their arguments were not opened and no claim is made about their contents. Harrison’s review criticises the final economic history for avoiding the methods and statistical products of postwar quantitative economic history (Harrison review).

A personal controversy arises from Nove’s own statements. The Stephanson interview has him saying he was “not so much advocating socialism, I was acting as an advocate for it in the legal sense of the word advocate”, and the 1983 addendum says he was “not necessarily committed to all my own arguments” and had no idea how to bridge the transition (Nove, 1991; compiled quotations, original interview not opened). Readers such as Mandel treated this as evasive; others, such as Louw, regarded the book as “the most intelligent and sophisticated contemporary defense of socialism” (Louw, 1993). Both readings rest on the same admissions.

Related thinkers#

  • Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels — the central theoretical opponents; Nove argued that their socialism was utopian and offered no working account of allocation without prices (Nove, 1991).
  • Vladimir Lenin, Leon Trotsky, Nikolai Bukharin and Karl Kautsky — treated as inheritors of the same lacuna; Nove quotes Trotsky on the limits of physical planning and Kautsky on the single gigantic industrial concern (Nove, 1991; Ratner).
  • Joseph Stalin — not treated as an accident or a betrayal but as a product of circumstances that did not allow an easy path; Nove still insisted that “no one should follow the trail blazed by Stalin” (Nove, 1992, p. 427).
  • Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek — Mises is credited with seeing the genuine difficulty of planning; Hayek and the “Chicago school” are criticised for exaggerating the virtues of markets (Nove, 1991).
  • Oskar Lange, Abba Lerner, Maurice Dobb, Maurice Dickinson and Enrico Barone — predecessors whose models Nove judged incomplete (Nove, 1991); a profile of Oskar Lange covers the calculation debate in which Lange’s model was one of the targets.
  • Wlodzimierz Brus and Radoslav Selucky — acknowledged influences and correspondents; Brus welcomed the 1983 book while Selucky’s ideas shaped Nove’s ownership categories (Nove, 1991; NLR record).
  • Ernest Mandel, Chris Harman and Boris Frankel — Marxist critics who argued that planning, not the market, was the socialist alternative (Mandel, 1986; Harman, 1989; Radical Philosophy 39).
  • János Kornai — whose “no third way” thesis and Economics of Shortage Nove engages directly in the 1991 appendix (Nove, 1991).
  • Pat Devine and David McNally — the former proposing “negotiated coordination” against market socialism, the latter declaring “socialism or the market” (Ratner).
  • Milton Friedman — an adversary whose analysis of Soviet planning Nove partly accepted while rejecting his market assumptions (Brown and Cairncross, 1997).
  • André Gorz, E. F. Schumacher, Oliver Williamson, R. H. Richardson and Jan Tinbergen — sources for his treatment of scale, the firm and the necessary level of centralisation (Nove, 1991).
  • Mikhail Gorbachev — whose reforms Nove welcomed and later judged incoherent; Nove published in Kommunist and advised the British embassy during perestroika (Brown and Cairncross, 1997).
