The DPRK, Vietnam and Cuba

Parties, ideologies, constitutions and socialist construction in North Korea, Vietnam and Cuba, including the 2026 congresses.

The constitutions of the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea, the Socialist Republic of Vietnam and the Republic of Cuba all place a single party at the centre of political life. All three describe the main means of production, meaning the factories, land, machinery and banks used to produce goods, as publicly owned. All three present socialism as the goal of the state. The language they use to justify that arrangement differs, and so does the history each tells about how it got there (DPRK 2019 constitution; Vietnam 2013 constitution; Cuba 2019 constitution).

These documents are evidence of what each government wanted its citizens and the world to believe. They are not a measurement of what happened. A constitution says that a party leads; it does not show how a party leads. An official history says that a revolution was won by the people; it does not tell us how people experienced it. This chapter sets the self descriptions of the three states beside the work of historians and the testimony of ordinary people, and marks the points where the two agree, differ or cannot be reconciled.

North Korea: the party’s account of its origins#

Official DPRK sources present Kim Il Sung (1912 to 1994) as the founder of the Juche idea and the leader of the Korean revolution. In a treatise published in 1982, his son Kim Jong Il wrote that Kim Il Sung first advanced the principles of Juche at a meeting of leading youth activists at Kalun in June 1930. The treatise says this answered a practical need, because “a revolution should be carried out not by anyone’s approval or instruction but by one’s own conviction and on one’s own responsibility” (Kim Jong Il, On the Juche Idea, 1982). In 1982 Kim Il Sung described Juche as “a man-centred world outlook and a revolutionary theory for realizing the independence of the masses of the people” (Kim Il Sung, Answers to Questions Raised by the Director of the RSS News Agency of Nepal, 22 June 1982). Juche, in plain terms, is the claim that each country’s revolution must be made by its own people, in a way that suits their own conditions, rather than by following instructions from abroad or copying another country’s model.

The Workers’ Party of Korea (WPK) was founded on 10 October 1945. A party exhibition published for its seventy fifth anniversary calls this “a solemn declaration to herald the birth of a new-type leadership political organization” and describes the party as “the revolutionary party of the Juche type” and “an integral whole” with the masses (Naenara, WPK anniversary exhibition). The same exhibition lists the stages the party assigns itself: founding the party and building a new country; leading the Fatherland Liberation War, which is the DPRK name for the Korean War of 1950 to 1953; establishing socialism and carrying out the Chollima campaign for rapid industrial production; building socialist industry and a self reliant defence capacity; carrying out ideological, technological and cultural change; and modelling the whole society on Juche.

The order of events in the official account is broadly supported by other records. Korea was liberated from Japanese rule in August 1945, the WPK was founded in October, and the DPRK was established in September 1948 with a constitution adopted on 8 September (1948 constitutional text, reproduced by the Committee for Human Rights in North Korea). Land reform, the nationalisation of major industry and the organisation of agricultural cooperatives followed. The party’s own history celebrates this as the “establishment of socialist system and the movement of Grand Chollima March” (Naenara, WPK exhibition). The historian Suzy Kim has shown what such a transformation looked like at village level. Using documents seized by American forces during the Korean War and now held in the United States National Archives, together with oral histories, she reconstructs land reform, elections, literacy schools and mass organisations in the years 1945 to 1950. She quotes a poor peasant named Sŏ Yŏng-jun, born in 1926, who wrote a short autobiography in September 1948 when he applied to join the Youth League. He described a childhood of tenant farming, of being unable to attend school and of caring for younger siblings from the age of twelve. After liberation he received about two hectares of land, learned to read at a Korean School from 1946, and joined the Youth League, the Peasant League and then, in January 1947, the Workers’ Party (Suzy Kim, Everyday Life in the North Korean Revolution, 1945 to 1950, Cornell University Press, 2013). Kim notes that the handwriting of newly literate writers, with its spelling errors and lack of punctuation, is visible in the documents themselves. That detail tells us something the official exhibition cannot: literacy and political participation were new and uneven experiences, not an accomplished fact (interview with Suzy Kim, Sino-NK, 2013).

After Kim Il Sung’s death in 1994 his son Kim Jong Il led the country until 2011. The official literature of those years returns repeatedly to the claim that socialism in Korea was a distinct path. Kim Jong Il’s 1991 text Our Socialism Centred On the Masses Shall Not Perish calls it “the embodiment of the Juche idea” and “a man-centred socialism under which man is the master of everything and everything serves him” (Kim Jong Il, 1991). His 1992 work The Historical Lesson in Building Socialism and the General Line of Our Party sets out why the Soviet and European experiences were thought to require correction (Kim Jong Il, 1992). These are statements of doctrine. They should be read as arguments inside a ruling party about what socialism should mean, not as descriptions of how those institutions actually worked.

Juche, military first and the nuclear line#

The 1982 treatise calls Juche a philosophy in which “man is the master of everything and decides everything”. It explains that this means human beings control their own destiny and play the decisive part in changing the world. It names “independence, creativity and consciousness” as the essential social qualities of human beings, and gives the guiding principles as “Juche in ideology, independence in politics, self-sufficiency in the economy, and self-reliance in defence” (Kim Jong Il, On the Juche Idea). The treatise insists that Kim Il Sung did not simply apply Marxism-Leninism to Korea but developed a new stage of revolutionary theory from a Juche standpoint. That is a claim about intellectual originality made by a loyal successor. How far Juche constitutes an advance in Marxist theory is a question historians and political scientists have answered in different ways, and it cannot be settled by the treatise itself.

Two later slogans qualified the same tradition. Songun means putting the military first. Kim Jong Il’s 2003 text The Songun-Based Revolutionary Line is a Great Revolutionary Line of Our Era and an Ever-Victorious Banner of Our Revolution presents the army as the main force of revolution and construction (Kim Jong Il, 2003). Byungjin, the policy of developing the economy and the nuclear weapons programme at the same time, became the party line under Kim Jong Un. The 2019 constitution calls the country “the socialist State of Juche” and, in its preamble, “a nuclear state” (2019 constitution, English text reproduced by the University of Hawai’i at Mānoa). Official reporting in June 2026 described the nuclear forces as “the core of the military sovereignty of the DPRK and the pivot of implementing the strategy for deterring or fighting a war”, and said the position of a nuclear weapons state was to be “thoroughly exercise[d]” (KCNA, 23 June 2026). Those are statements of intent published by the state news agency.

The plan, the famine and what happened in practice#

Official accounts describe an independent national economy built on public ownership and central planning. In 1982 Kim Il Sung said the DPRK had “built an excellent socialist independent national economy which is run with our own resources and efforts, diversified, and equipped with up-to-date technology”, and that it was “unaffected by world economic fluctuations” (Kim Il Sung, 1982). This was a claim made by the country’s leader to a foreign interviewer, and later events test it. In the mid 1990s the DPRK experienced one of the worst famines of the twentieth century. Stephan Haggard and Marcus Noland argue that the socialist food distribution system collapsed because of a state led push for self reliance, made worse by the government’s slow response and its obstruction of humanitarian relief. Food supplies had begun to shrink and mortality to rise before the floods of the mid 1990s that the government cited as the cause (Haggard and Noland, Famine in North Korea: Markets, Aid, and Reform, Columbia University Press, 2007; interview with the authors, Columbia University Press, 2016). Estimates of the death toll vary widely. Haggard and Noland suggest up to a million; Barbara Demick reported figures between six hundred thousand and two and a half million and noted that North Korean hospitals were prohibited from recording starvation as a cause of death (Demick, “The Good Cook”, The New Yorker, 26 October 2009). Such figures are estimates drawn from incomplete evidence, so no single number should be treated as settled.

Demick’s interviews give the collapse a human scale. Song Hee-suk, a former nursery worker at a clothing factory in Chongjin whose name Demick changed, described getting by on a shrinking ration in the late 1980s and early 1990s. She foraged for weeds and pine bark powder, tried to make tofu for sale, and later traded rice by train. Three members of her family, including her husband and son, died. She told Demick in Seoul in 2004 that for years she had blamed herself rather than the government. The account is retrospective, recorded years after the events and after defection, and one person’s experience cannot establish a national death rate. It does show how the public distribution system, the ration book and the work unit were tied together, and what happened when that chain broke (Demick, Nothing to Envy: Ordinary Lives in North Korea, Spiegel and Grau, 2009).

Scholars disagree about what to make of the years after the famine. Haggard and Noland see later reforms as reactive moves to regain control of an economy that had partly marketised from below. Hazel Smith argues that the marketisation itself changed the interests and expectations of almost every social group, including party members, soldiers, women and young people. She describes North Koreans as active agents rather than victims or bystanders, and concludes that daily life became increasingly distant from the government’s pronouncements (Hazel Smith, North Korea: Markets and Military Rule, Cambridge University Press, 2015). Both accounts accept that informal markets grew. They differ over how much weight to give state policy, popular adaptation and outside pressure.

What does this mean for the official claim about the “socialist enterprise”? In DPRK accounts such an enterprise is supposed to operate with more responsibility and initiative within the plan. That is a description of an intended institution. Independent evidence about how enterprises actually make decisions is thin, and what exists points to the importance of informal trading and household coping described above. The honest conclusion is that the official model is known in detail, while its day to day operation is much less well documented (Naenara, WPK exhibition).

Diplomacy and doctrine after 2018#

The DPRK’s constitution is called the Socialist Constitution. First adopted in 1948 and revised many times, it is published in English by the Foreign Languages Publishing House and reproduced in full by the University of Hawai’i at Mānoa (Naenara constitution entry; 2019 text). It calls the WPK the “General Staff of the Korean revolution” and gives the leading role to the party alongside what are called the people’s power organs, meaning the assemblies and committees through which the constitution says citizens exercise power. The Supreme People’s Assembly is formally the highest organ of state power; the State Affairs Commission is the leading state body; and cabinets and local people’s committees handle economic administration. Official reporting in June 2026 invoked Articles 90, 96 and 99 of the constitution in announcing a change in the chairmanship of the Supreme People’s Assembly Standing Committee (KCNA, 23 June 2026).

Between 2018 and 2019 the DPRK took part in inter Korean summits at Panmunjom and Pyongyang, and Kim Jong Un met the president of the United States in Singapore in June 2018. A second meeting in Hanoi in February 2019 ended without agreement. The constitutional revision that described the country as a nuclear state came in the same period. From 2023 official statements redefined the relationship with the Republic of Korea, which the DPRK calls the most hostile state. At a Central Committee plenum in June 2026, acting on decisions of the Ninth WPK Congress held in April 2026, Kim Jong Un reaffirmed that line, set coal production and the modernisation of mining towns as priorities, and reviewed a new five year plan (KCNA, 22 April 2026; KCNA, 23 June 2026). The party also marked the eightieth anniversary of its founding with a meeting and parade in Pyongyang (Naenara, anniversary exhibition). These reports tell us what the leadership announced. They are the account published by the state news agency, and there is no independent reporting from inside the DPRK with which to compare them.

Vietnam: revolution, independence and the party#

Ho Chi Minh (1890 to 1969), also known as Nguyễn Ái Quốc, founded the Communist Party of Vietnam and wrote many of its founding texts. In a 1960 account of his own intellectual journey he recalled that after years of searching he found in Lenin’s Thesis on the National and Colonial Questions the answer to the colonial question: “This is what we need, this is the path to our liberation!” (Ho Chi Minh, The Path Which Led Me to Leninism, 1960). The Communist Party was founded in February 1930 as the Indochinese Communist Party. The appeal issued on 18 February 1930 stated: “The Indochinese Communist Party has been founded. It is the Party of the working class. It will help the proletariat lead the revolution” (Ho Chi Minh, 18 February 1930). It was renamed the Vietnam Workers’ Party and, in 1976, the Communist Party of Vietnam (CPV).

Vietnam’s independence declaration of 2 September 1945 puts the revolution in a wider frame. Reading from the steps of the Ba Dinh Square rally, Ho Chi Minh invoked the American and French revolutionary traditions: “All men are created equal. They are endowed by their Creator with certain inalienable rights, among them are Life, Liberty, and the pursuit of Happiness.” He went on to accuse France of having “violated our Fatherland and oppressed our fellow-citizens” for more than eighty years in the name of liberty, equality and fraternity (Ho Chi Minh, Vietnamese Declaration of Independence, 2 September 1945). The declaration was a political act addressed as much to the United States and to world opinion as to the Vietnamese crowd. Its language of universal rights sat alongside a party programme shaped by Lenin’s theory of revolution.

The August Revolution of 1945 took power in the gap left by Japan’s surrender. The war against French reconquest ran from 1946 to 1954 and ended with the Geneva settlement and the division of the country at the seventeenth parallel. A constitution of 1959 consolidated socialist institutions in the North. The conflict with United States military intervention in the South ended with the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975 (National Museum of American Diplomacy, “The Fall of Saigon (1975)”). The country was reunified as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam in 1976 (Britannica, “Vietnam”). Article 4 of the 2013 constitution defines the CPV as “the vanguard of the Vietnamese working class” and “the leading force of the State and society”, acting on “the Marxist-Leninist doctrine and Ho Chi Minh’s thought” (Constitution of the Socialist Republic of Vietnam, 2013, Article 4). Ho Chi Minh Thought is therefore a co equal ideological foundation in the official formulation, not an addition to imported Marxism-Leninism.

Renovation and the socialist oriented market economy#

Đổi Mới, meaning renewal, was adopted at the Sixth National Congress of the CPV in December 1986. It is best understood as a response to crisis. Adam Fforde, an economist who has studied Vietnamese reform since the 1980s, records that at the time of the congress market inflation was running above 700 percent a year, exports were worth about US$500 million against imports of US$1,221 million, and some areas were on the edge of famine. The congress agreed to end the system of centralised management through state subsidies and to allow a multi sector economy in which private firms could compete with state firms outside strategic sectors (Adam Fforde, Doi Moi: Ten Years After the 1986 Party Congress, Australian National University, 1997). The changes were not a single decree but a sequence. Output contracts introduced from about 1980 let farm households keep a surplus after meeting a quota. A party resolution of April 1988, known as Contract 10, gave households long term land rights, ended centrally planned targets, stopped forced membership of cooperatives and allowed farmers to sell on the open market. Price and trade reforms followed in 1989, ending the procurement of farm output below market prices on formal terms (Fforde, 1997). These reforms reached rural households directly and help explain why Vietnam’s growth after 1988 is usually dated from agriculture rather than from industry.

Historians have argued about the social effects. Jayne Werner argued in 1984 that subcontracting weakened collective institutions and shifted women’s work back into the household, with possible effects on income inequality. Fforde replied that the evidence for such conclusions was thin and that the older collective system had never been as equal as the argument assumed (Jayne Werner, Bulletin of Concerned Asian Scholars, 1984; Adam Fforde’s response, 1985). The dispute is a reminder that the household contract, which official accounts present as a straightforward success, redistributed work and power inside villages as well as raising output.

Vietnam’s constitution gives the economy a name. Article 51 defines a socialist oriented market economy as one with “multi-forms of ownership and multi-sectors of economic structure”, in which “the state economic sector plays the leading role” while actors in different sectors are equal before the law and cooperate and compete (2013 constitution, Article 51). In plain terms this means that private enterprise and foreign investment are allowed, but the state sector is expected to guide the economy. Article 53 adds that land, water, minerals and other natural resources are public property managed by the state. Article 2 calls Vietnam a socialist rule of law state whose power rests on the alliance of workers, peasants and the intelligentsia, meaning educated professionals and cultural workers. Article 8 requires state bodies to observe democratic centralism, the rule that members may discuss policy but must carry out decisions once they are made (2013 constitution, Articles 2, 8, 53). The National Assembly is formally the highest representative body and the highest organ of state power, serving five year terms.

The 2011 Party Platform, revised in 2016, remains the party’s main document for what it calls the transition period to socialism, meaning the long stretch between revolution and the eventual socialist society. The Fourteenth National Congress met in Hanoi from 19 to 23 January 2026. It elected 200 members to the Central Committee, 180 full and 20 alternate, and re elected To Lam as General Secretary (Government News, 23 January 2026). Its resolution sets targets rather than results: annual GDP growth of at least 10 percent, average income of about US$8,500 by 2030 and high income status by 2045 (Full Resolution of the 14th National Party Congress). Targets of that kind are promises about the future. They become evidence only when later data can be compared with them.

Cuba: from the Sierra Maestra to the 2019 constitution#

Fidel Castro (1926 to 2016) led the movement that overthrew the dictatorship of Fulgencio Batista on 1 January 1959 (Britannica, “Fidel Castro”). The 26th of July Movement took its name from the attack on the Moncada Barracks on 26 July 1953. In his defence speech of October 1953, History Will Absolve Me, Castro listed six problems the revolution would address: “The problem of the land, the problem of industrialization, the problem of housing, the problem of unemployment, the problem of education and the problem of the people’s health: these are the six problems we would take immediate steps to solve, along with restoration of civil liberties and political democracy” (Fidel Castro, 16 October 1953). The list is striking because it is practical and does not name socialism. The historian Ada Ferrer has argued that Cubans did not join the movement against Batista because they wanted socialism or a confrontation with the United States. The revolution became both socialist and anti American in the two years after it took power. Ferrer’s book is written deliberately from the standpoint of ordinary Cubans, placing unnamed people alongside the generals and presidents who dominate political histories (Ada Ferrer, Cuba: An American History, Simon and Schuster, 2021; review in the American Historical Review, 2022).

After exile in Mexico the Granma expedition landed in December 1956 and the Rebel Army established itself in the Sierra Maestra (Castro, 1961 retrospective). The revolutionary government’s first measures included the agrarian reform of 17 May 1959, the nationalisation of major industry and public services, and a literacy campaign in 1961. On 1 May 1961 Castro proclaimed the socialist character of the revolution: “Yes, this is a socialist regime. Yes!” and “This is a socialist revolution”. On 2 December he declared his Marxist-Leninist conviction (Fidel Castro, 1 May 1961; 2 December 1961). Ernesto “Che” Guevara was a central figure in the Rebel Army and the early government. Castro’s tribute of 1967 called him “a true example of revolutionary virtues” (Fidel Castro, 18 October 1967). The party’s own chronology gives 1961 as its founding and 1965 as the constitution of its central committee, the date often given in secondary accounts. Its statutes, as presented by Raúl Castro in 2021, claim descent from José Martí’s Cuban Revolutionary Party, from the first Cuban communist party founded by Carlos Baliño and Julio Antonio Mella, and from the three organisations that fought Batista (Raúl Castro, Central Report to the Eighth Congress of the PCC, 16 April 2021). That genealogy is the party’s own account of its legitimacy.

The literacy campaign is often cited as evidence of the revolution’s social achievement. Kimberly Waller’s oral history project collected the recollections of the volunteer teachers who took part in 1961. She observes that earlier accounts concentrated on urban young people who travelled to poor rural areas, and that the teachers themselves analysed conflicting statistics about the campaign’s results (Kimberly Waller, The Traveling Memories Project, doctoral dissertation, Antioch University New England, 2022). Even a celebrated campaign, in other words, has a contested numerical record and a wider cast of participants than the standard story allows.

The constitution adopted in 2019 sets out the current settlement. Its preamble says the republic is guided by “the ideal and example of Martí and Fidel, as well as the social emancipation ideas of Marx, Engels, and Lenin”. Article 5 calls the PCC “unique, Martiano, Fidelista, and Marxist-Leninist” and “the superior driving force of the society and the State”. Article 4 declares the socialist system “irrevocable”, and Article 18 describes an economy based on public ownership of the fundamental means of production and “the planned direction of the economy”, with planning described in Article 19 as the central component of economic governance (Constitution of the Republic of Cuba, 2019). Article 22 recognises several forms of property, including cooperative property, private property with a complementary role, mixed property, and institutional and associative property, meaning property held by state institutions and associations rather than by households or the public as a whole. Article 65 states the socialist principle of distribution: “from each according to their ability, to each according to their labor.” The same constitution prohibits discrimination on grounds that include sexual orientation and gender identity, and Article 82 defines marriage as based on free consent and equality of the spouses. The Family Code of 2022, which extended legal recognition to same sex marriage, was approved by referendum and published in the Official Gazette on 27 September 2022 (Cuban National Assembly, Código de las Familias).

The constitution was approved in a national referendum on 24 February 2019. According to figures published by the official newspaper Granma, 6,816,169 voters, or 86.85 percent of those casting ballots, voted yes, and 706,400 voted no (Granma, “New Constitution defended by the majority”, 2 March 2021). These are the state’s figures. They describe the vote as reported, not the conditions of the campaign that preceded it.

Updating the model, and its limits#

The Update of the economic model began at the Sixth PCC Congress in 2011 with the Guidelines for the Economic and Social Policy of the Party and the Revolution, known as the Lineamientos, revised at the Seventh Congress in 2016. The Eighth Congress report of 2021 says the 2016 revision kept 17 guidelines, changed 165, removed 92 and added 18, leaving 200 in total. From 1 January 2021 the government began the Tarea Ordenamiento, or Reordering Task, which unified the currency and exchange rates, reformed wages, pensions and benefits, and reduced subsidies (Raúl Castro, Central Report to the Eighth Congress, 2021). Raúl Castro described the aim as a better mix of central planning and local decision making, with more autonomy for enterprises and local government.

The same report is more revealing when it describes what has not worked. It names “excessive bureaucracy, inadequate control of resources”, corruption and an economic model that “does not provide sufficient incentives for work or innovation”. It also says the country will not allow shock therapies that hurt the poorest and that no one will be left unprotected. That combination matters. It is an official admission of serious problems together with an official limit on the solutions considered acceptable. In June 2026 a report to the Cuban parliament set out 176 proposals in 23 areas for further changes to the economic and social model (Granma, 19 June 2026). The Eighth Congress itself met in April 2021 with 300 delegates claiming to represent more than 700,000 party members, and the party has announced a Ninth Congress (PCC congress announcement).

Cuban officials emphasise the reach of health care and education and the protection of living standards. Those achievements should not be dismissed, because they are documented by a range of observers as well as by the state. Neither should the other side of the record. In July 2021 protests over food shortages and frustration with the government broke out in Cuba, and historians such as Ferrer describe growing impatience among ordinary people with wages, diets and daily conditions (Carrie Gibson, review of Ferrer, The Guardian, 4 September 2021). Official statements about protection and stability are therefore claims to be weighed against evidence, not a complete account of life on the island.

What the three cases share#

Each constitution puts one party at the centre. The WPK is called “the General Staff of the Korean revolution”, the CPV “the leading force of the State and society”, and the PCC “the superior driving force of the society and the State” (Naenara; Vietnam 2013 constitution, Article 4; Cuba 2019 constitution, Article 5). Each retains public ownership of major productive assets. The wording of the economic model is different. The DPRK speaks of Juche oriented management and a socialist enterprise system; Vietnam of a socialist oriented market economy; Cuba of an updated model that combines planning with decentralisation and gives private property a complementary role. The party survived in all three cases, but the relation between the plan and the market, and between the official story and daily life, is not the same. Vietnam’s contracts returned farming to the household. Informal markets in North Korea grew despite official hostility. Cuba’s own congress reported that its model failed to reward work and innovation. Those concrete differences are more informative than the shared vocabulary of party leadership and public ownership.
