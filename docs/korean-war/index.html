The Korean War: Voices from Three Armies and a Divided Peninsula

On 2 October 1950, days before China committed its army to Korea, Mao Zedong warned Stalin that the step might be a mistake.

On 2 October 1950, two days before China committed its army to Korea, Mao Zedong warned Stalin that the step might be a mistake. The message passed through Nikolai Roshchin, the Soviet ambassador in Beijing, who relayed Mao’s words to Moscow in a coded telegram. “we now consider that such actions may entail extremely serious consequences.” (Mao to Stalin, 2 October 1950, relayed 3 October, translated by Kathryn Weathersby and Alexandre Mansourov). Mao listed the reasons: his divisions were poorly equipped, an attack might bring war with the United States, it might draw in the Soviet Union, and China was exhausted after its civil war. He proposed waiting and preparing. This is not the language of an unbroken march to war, and it sits uneasily beside later Chinese accounts of a firm, early decision.

The decision and the order#

By 8 October the decision had been taken. Mao told Stalin that Peng Dehuai had been appointed commander and that “The troops might begin to cross into Korean territory by about 15 October.” (Mao to Stalin, 8 October 1950). His order of the same day created the Chinese People’s Volunteers. The order states no reason for the name. “I herewith order the Chinese People’s Volunteers to march speedily to Korea and join the Korean comrades in fighting the aggressors and winning a glorious victory.” (Mao’s order, 8 October 1950). In January 1951 he instructed those troops to “take not a single needle or a single thread from the Korean people” (Mao’s directive, 19 January 1951). A separate set of political rules told them to “Strictly follow the political commands of the Korean government” (four principles for unity, October 1950), defining solidarity as obedience to the host party. That text comes from a 1968 internal compilation and a museum display, so its wording may reflect a later context, and an instruction states an intended standard rather than what happened in villages. The historian Chen Jian, using both Russian and Chinese records, notes that the 2 October telegram does not match the Chinese published version of the decision, a warning against treating either text as the whole story.

The hidden air war#

Soviet air power was committed only from November 1950, after an abortive air-war plan, and the engagement was kept secret. On 15 November 1950 Mao wrote to Stalin: “Thanks to the courage and efforts of the Soviet air force men in battle, in the past 12 days 23 invading American planes have been shot down.” (Mao to Stalin, 15 November 1950, translated by Jingxia Yang and Douglas Stiffler). The number is Mao’s report to Stalin, not an independently verified loss count. Porfiriy Ovsyannikov, a Soviet pilot flying MiG-15 jet fighters, recalled decades later that Soviet airmen flew from bases in China, were told to limit Russian radio talk, and trained Chinese and Korean pilots. “But when we began to fly with the Chinese, some of them fainted from the G-forces. From hunger.” (Ovsyannikov interview, published 2019, translated by James F. Gebhardt). The editor’s footnotes repeatedly challenge the pilot’s victory claims against American records, so his recollection is evidence of what a pilot believed, not of enemy losses. It also links the secret air war to the material condition of China’s own air force.

The soldiers’ war#

British, American and Commonwealth veterans describe a war of sudden attacks and long stagnation. A speaker in the Imperial War Museums’ “Voices of the Korean War” collection remembers the fighting of September 1950: “The end of the day we had ninety casualties and it just decimated them two platoons.” (IWM oral history). Another recalls an officer’s order to a bugler, “Play anything you like bar a retreat.” (recalled by Private Anthony Eagles, IWM). Marguerite Higgins, a war correspondent, said of the opening weeks, “I saw my countrymen mauled and humiliated” (American RadioWorks transcript, 2003). Billy J. Scott, an American sergeant, remembered sharing rations with children in the ruins of Seoul: “we got our c-rations, and we’d toss it, you know, out to some of the children” (Scott interview, published 2017). Colonel Peter Ormrod described refugees moving south: “It was very tragic seeing all the refugees coming through in thousands four or five people wide on the road just marching along in great column carrying their baggage” (Ormrod recollections, published 2021). Yu Liangshi, a Chinese war correspondent who crossed the Yalu in 1952, recalled, “Almost everyone had lice in their clothes and we called them the ‘glorious worms’ and the ‘revolutionary worms’ because we thought they were the signs of our hard work.” (Yu Liangshi interview, China Daily, 27 November 2020). These are retrospective accounts recorded decades after service. Yu’s appeared in a state newspaper in the seventieth anniversary year, within a commemorative frame that selected for endurance and courage; the others were shaped by the museums, foundations and interviewers that sponsored them.

Civilians and the armistice#

Civilians appear most sharply in a child’s memory collected sixty years later. “We called bombing planes ssaeksaek. We didn’t know whether the planes belonged to inmin or our own; what mattered was that ssaekssaek dropped bombs anywhere there was most people.” (“Lee 1”, interview collected 2015, English translation in the Borealis dataset). She describes flight from Seoul through Wonju and Chungju, checking points where suspected soldiers and officials were killed, and her grandmother’s death after being shot in a clash between South Korean troops and retreating northern soldiers. The transcript warns that the Korean rendering is not exact, the witness was a child of thirteen or fourteen, and the collection of twenty-five interviews came from one welfare centre in Seoul.

At No Gun Ri in the last week of July 1950, Korean witnesses described an air attack and four days of firing into a railway tunnel. The two governments’ joint review recorded that “U.S. ground forces fired toward refugees in the vicinity of No Gun Ri during this period.” (US–ROK Statement of Mutual Understanding, 11 January 2001). The US Army’s own review found that American soldiers killed and injured Korean civilians near No Gun Ri during a withdrawal under pressure, but could not confirm the tunnel account, found no documentary proof of an order to shoot civilians, and could not establish a death toll. The Korean side listed 248 killed, injured or missing, a figure the review did not verify. The two official statements agree that soldiers fired toward refugees and disagree about what that established.

Fighting ended on 27 July 1953 with an armistice, an agreement to stop fighting rather than a peace treaty. Its preamble pledged to “insure a complete cessation of hostilities and of all acts of armed force in Korea until a final peaceful settlement is achieved” (Korean Armistice Agreement, 27 July 1953). It drew a military demarcation line, a demilitarised zone four kilometres wide from which forces were withdrawn, and commissions to supervise the ceasefire and the transfer of prisoners. A political conference was only recommended. The document records what was agreed, not whether either side complied, and no peace treaty followed.

Limits of the record#

The sources do not support a total of civilian deaths across Korea. The No Gun Ri review could not establish how many died there, and Korea as a whole was never counted in the testimony that survives. That testimony is also uneven. It comes mainly from literate men with institutional sponsors: officers, pilots, correspondents and veterans’ organisations. Korean civilians were interviewed decades later as children, through a transcript that admits it is not verbatim; North Korean voices and Chinese soldiers who did not survive or later write are almost absent, and women appear mostly as relatives in someone else’s story. Orders and instructions show what leaders wanted said, not what individual soldiers believed about the war’s purpose. The armistice and the telegrams record decisions and terms, not compliance. A fuller account would need the original full oral-history recordings, Chinese and North Korean archival material, Korean-language local histories, and the complete witness statements behind the No Gun Ri summaries. None of those was available, and no figure from a single witness should be turned into a general one.

How historians have read it#

  • Kathryn Weathersby, Soviet Aims in Korea and the Origins of the Korean War, 1945–50: New Evidence from the Russian Archives (1993). A working paper using newly declassified Soviet records to reconstruct Stalin’s aims and his permission for Kim Il Sung’s attack. Source: wilsoncenter.org
  • Chen Jian, China’s Road to the Korean War: The Making of the Sino-American Confrontation (1994). Uses Chinese and Russian sources to trace Beijing’s decision; its opening chapter flags the discrepancy between the 2 October Russian-archive telegram and the Chinese published version. Source: ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu
  • William Stueck, The Korean War: An International History (1995). Draws on materials from seven countries and UN archives; argues the war functioned as a substitute for a third world war and that allies and neutrals constrained American policy. JSTOR: j.ctt7s1ss
  • Richard Peters and Xiaobing Li, eds., Voices from the Korean War: Personal Stories of American, Korean, and Chinese Soldiers (2004). Twenty first-person accounts from all sides, including Chinese and Korean combatants previously unavailable in English. Source: kentuckypress.com

Scholars agree on the broad sequence: a Korean civil war widened into an international war, China intervened after UN forces crossed the 38th parallel, Soviet air power was committed secretly, and the fighting ended in a military armistice in 1953. Weathersby and Chen value the new archives but differ in emphasis, she on Soviet aims, he on Chinese decision-making and its domestic setting. Stueck places the war inside the international system and the later Cold War. Disagreement is live over how far the war was a success for the socialist states, how to read the Chinese decision record and how deliberate the killing of civilians was; these disputes turn on evidence still being contested, not on equally credible competing readings.
