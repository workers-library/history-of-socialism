Jason Hickel: Global Inequality, Extraction and Degrowth

A research profile of the economic anthropologist's account of imperialist appropriation, global inequality and the case for degrowth, with critics.

Jason Hickel argues that global poverty is made rather than found: poor countries are poor because they are integrated into the world economy on unequal terms. His central term is appropriation, meaning the taking of land, materials and labour without equivalent payment in return. In his account the wealthy North appropriates from the global South through ecologically unequal exchange — trade in which the South exports far more embodied resources and labour than it imports, because its wages and prices are held down while the North monopolises technology and capital goods. His proposed remedy is degrowth: reducing excess resource and energy throughput while improving human well-being, rather than simply shrinking output. This profile reports those arguments as his claims, sets out the evidence he and his co-authors offer, and records the objections raised against them. The site’s source policy governs what can count as evidence here. No book was opened as a full text, no physical archive was available, and all verification was of online pages or registry metadata.

Biography#

Hickel describes himself as a political economist, an author and a Fellow of the Royal Society of Arts. He holds an ICREA professorship at the Institute for Environmental Science and Technology (ICTA) and the Department of Political Science and Public Law at the Autonomous University of Barcelona. He also serves on the Climate and Macroeconomics Roundtable of the US National Academy of Sciences, the advisory board of the Green New Deal for Europe, the Rodney Commission on Reparations and Redistributive Justice and the Lancet Commission on Sustainable Health (author’s About page). Penguin’s author record adds detail that his own page omits: it calls him an economic anthropologist, says he is originally from Eswatini (Swaziland), says he spent a number of years with migrant workers in South Africa writing about exploitation and political resistance after apartheid, and says he lives in London and Barcelona (Penguin author record). The ICREA memoir for 2025 confirms the Barcelona chair, a visiting professorship at the London School of Economics and a Chair Professorship of Global Justice and the Environment at the University of Oslo, and records an ERC Synergy Grant titled REAL: A Post-Growth Deal (ICREA memoir).

Some biographical details could not be confirmed. A search result for his ICREA curriculum vitae listed an MA and a PhD in anthropology at the University of Virginia, a Leverhulme Early Career Fellowship at the LSE, an associate professorship at Goldsmiths and a move to Barcelona in 2021. The URL returned a PDF that was not parsed in this research, so those dates are not verified and are not used here. No birth date or birth year appeared in any author, employer or publisher record opened for this profile.

His first book grew out of earlier ethnographic work on colonialism, anti-colonial struggle and the labour movement in South Africa. His own About page dates that work to Democracy as Death (University of California Press, 2015) and lists two co-edited ethnographic volumes, Ekhaya (2014) and Hierarchy and Value (2018). Hickel describes the shift from southern African anthropology to global inequality and ecological economics as a change of research focus. In the new afterword to The Divide he says that degrowth was an idea he engaged only in the final weeks of writing that book and developed properly afterwards (New Afterword).

Intellectual tradition#

Hickel works in the tradition of anti-colonial political economy and dependency theory, updated with ecological economics and degrowth. He does not present himself as a doctrinal Marxist, and he has criticised parts of the socialist left for defending growth. In a 2021 interview he summed up his position in a formula that has become a signature phrase: “ecosocialism is the horizon, degrowth is the way” (The Trouble interview, 11 February 2021). In the same interview he argued that most degrowth advocates would call themselves ecosocialists of one stripe or another, while rejecting what he called a “left-wing ecomodernism” that assumes growth can deliver abundance without ecological cost. His disagreement is therefore not simply between growth and no growth. It is between a politics that treats ecological crisis as a technical problem and one that treats it as the outcome of a world economy organised around appropriation from the South.

He adds an explicitly anti-imperialist condition to that position. In the same interview he said that “Degrowth also adds an anti-imperialist ethic to ecosocialism” and that “Ecosocialism without anti-imperialism is not an ecosocialism worth having.” He also rejects the objection that degrowth is a doctrine for wealthy Westerners: in a 2022 interview he called it “a demand for global justice” and “a call to liberate the South from imperial appropriation and decolonise the atmosphere” (Green European Journal, 5 January 2022). Penguin’s jacket language calls him “one of the world’s leading thinkers on degrowth and eco-socialism” (Penguin author record). That is publisher promotion rather than a scholarly classification, though it reflects the position he argues for in his own work.

Major contributions#

The first contribution is a reframing of global poverty as a political product rather than a natural condition. His own description of The Divide states that “poverty doesn’t just exist, it has been created” and that “Poor countries are poor because they are integrated into the global economic system on unequal terms.” The same page gives his headline figures for the original edition: since 1960 the income gap between North and South had roughly tripled, 4.3 billion people (60 per cent of the world’s population) lived on less than five dollars a day, and the richest eight people controlled as much wealth as the poorest half of humanity (The Divide book page). These are the author’s own claims and measures, not independently verified findings; the five-dollar line is his chosen poverty threshold, not the World Bank’s.

The second contribution is the quantitative measurement of what he calls imperialist appropriation. With Christian Dorninger, Hanspeter Wieland and Intan Suwandi he used environmental input-output data and footprint analysis to estimate net flows of embodied resources and labour from South to North between 1990 and 2015. Their abstract reports that in 2015 the North net appropriated 12 billion tonnes of embodied raw material equivalents, 822 million hectares of embodied land, 21 exajoules of embodied energy and 188 million person-years of embodied labour, valued at 10.8 trillion dollars in Northern prices. Over the whole period they put the drain at 242 trillion constant 2010 dollars, equivalent to a quarter of Northern GDP, and found that Southern losses due to unequal exchange exceeded total aid receipts by a factor of thirty (LSE Research Online record). The paper’s own claim is that this drain is a significant driver of global inequality, uneven development and ecological breakdown.

The third contribution links that transfer to ecological breakdown and historical responsibility. Hickel has argued that high-income countries are responsible for 92 per cent of emissions in excess of the safe planetary boundary, a figure he repeats in the 2024 afterword and in a 2022 interview, and he co-authored a paper estimating that high-income countries have overshot their fair shares of the Paris Agreement carbon budget and owe about 192 trillion dollars in compensation to countries in the global South (New Afterword; Green European Journal; underlying papers tagged as empirical datasets below). He also co-developed the Sustainable Development Index, published in Ecological Economics in 2020, which measures the ecological efficiency of human development; a revised equity-based version appeared in 2026 (author’s research list).

The fourth contribution is the argument that degrowth means reducing resource and energy throughput rather than simply shrinking output. In his reply to Branko Milanović he wrote: “Degrowth is not about reducing GDP. Rather, it is about reducing excess resource and energy throughput, while at the same time improving human well-being and social outcomes” (Hickel’s response). His 2020 paper with Giorgos Kallis, “Is Green Growth Possible?”, asks whether decoupling can happen fast enough to stay within carbon budgets while GDP continues to grow, and answers no (Crossref record). The empirical programme that follows involves needs-based analysis of how much energy and material throughput is actually required to secure good lives for all (author’s research list).

The fifth contribution is a historical claim about capitalism and mass immiseration, relevant to this book’s account of what capitalism achieved. With Dylan Sullivan he published a global study in World Development using real wages, human height and mortality since the long sixteenth century to challenge the claim that capitalism rescued humanity from extreme poverty. In a reply to the economic historian Tirthankar Roy, Sullivan and Hickel state their finding that India suffered between 50 and 165 million excess deaths in the mortality crisis of 1881–1920 and attribute that crisis to British colonial policy, including asymmetric tariffs, technology restrictions and the forced export of foodstuffs (Hickel/Sullivan reply). In his 2024 afterword Hickel summarises the paper’s conclusion more broadly: the rise of capitalism and its global imposition was “associated with a striking decline in social indicators”, with wages often falling below subsistence and mass mortality crises in several regions.

Analysis of capitalism#

Hickel’s account begins from the purpose of production. In his 2025 speech at Fudan University he said that under capitalism “the purpose of production is not to meet human needs or to achieve social progress” but “to maximize and accumulate profit” (Fudan speech, 18 April 2025). Because accumulation requires ever more inputs at the lowest possible price, capital needs cheap labour and cheap nature. Intensifying exploitation at home risks revolution and ecological damage, so capital requires an external frontier where social and ecological costs can be pushed onto others. He states the conclusion directly: “Imperialism is a structurally necessary feature of capitalism.” Colonial rule is, on this reading, the first form of that arrangement and structural adjustment its restored form, a subject treated historically in Colonialism and Underdevelopment.

In the Fudan speech and in the 2022 Green European Journal interview he argues that anti-colonial movements pursued economic sovereignty through land reform, nationalisation, tariffs, subsidies and planning; that this raised wages and prices in the South and cut off cheap inputs to the core; and that Western governments responded with military intervention, coups against leaders such as Kwame Nkrumah, Salvador Allende, Sukarno and Mohammad Mosaddeq, and with IMF and World Bank structural adjustment programmes that privatised public industries and cut tariffs, subsidies and protections. The 1970s crisis of stagflation, on this reading, was a crisis of accumulation caused by Southern sovereignty rather than a domestic monetary event.

The mechanism that sustains the arrangement today is what he calls a hidden transfer of value. Because Southern wages and prices are suppressed while the North monopolises technology and capital goods, the South must export far more embodied labour and resources than it imports to pay for the same units. In the Fudan speech he put the ratio at more than ten to one and noted that about half of all consumption in the core is net-appropriated from the South. Aid, in his account, masks this flow rather than repairing it. His publisher’s description of The Divide makes the same case: “The aid narrative hides the deep patterns of wealth extraction that cause poverty and inequality in the first place: rigged trade deals, tax evasion, land grabs and the costs associated with climate change” (Penguin book record, seen as a search result and not fetched directly; the identical passage appears on his own book page).

He also attacks the cultural and measurement apparatus of capitalism. In his reply to Robert Pollin he called growth “a kind of propaganda term” that “takes processes of extraction, commodification and elite accumulation, which are quite often destructive to human communities and to ecology, and sells them as natural, good and common-sense”, and called the language of growth “the bedrock of capitalism’s cultural hegemony” (Hickel’s reply). His argument about efficiency is that under growthism efficiency gains are reinvested in expanded production rather than saved, so technological improvement does not by itself reduce total resource use.

Analysis of socialism#

Hickel does not offer a blueprint for a socialist state in the sense of a party programme or a constitutional design, and his published work contains no detailed account of how a planning apparatus would be governed. What he offers instead is a set of institutional commitments that he associates with ecosocialism, degrowth and the anti-colonial tradition. In the 2021 Trouble interview he said the task was to “decommodify public goods, disaccumulate capital, and distribute income more fairly” and to reverse what he calls artificial scarcities rather than impose austerity. In a 2022 interview he listed the concrete programme as quality public housing, a radically fairer distribution of income, a climate job guarantee, a basic income, universal public services and the decommodification of housing (Green European Journal).

He favours democratic and local control over centralised ecological planning. In the Trouble interview he invoked James C. Scott’s Seeing Like a State to argue that ecosystems cannot be managed through abstract planning and that the relevant knowledge belongs to people with a relationship to the land. He cited Murray Bookchin on the claim that social hierarchy is reproduced in a hierarchical relation to nature, and defended commons, collective democratic control and the Rights of Nature movement. He explicitly rejects authoritarianism as “intrinsically anti-ecological” rather than only as politically undesirable.

On the international question, his preferred institutions are those that break the imperial arrangement. In the 2024 afterword he says Southern countries should pursue “unilateral decolonization” through economic sovereignty, reducing dependence on core imports, industrial policy, planning and public credit, and he co-authored a Progressive International blueprint on that theme (New Afterword). He supports debt repudiation and reparations, including a 2025 BMJ paper with Farwa Sial and Ndongo Samba Sylla on repudiation of global South debt to meet human need (author’s research list). He ends the afterword by saying that “The changes we need are not reformist but revolutionary”, while insisting that revolutions require both a change in imagination and organised struggle.

Recommended reading#

These selections are starting points, not a guide to all of Hickel’s books and articles.

  • The Divide: A Brief Guide to Global Inequality and its Solutions (2017). It argues that poverty is made rather than found because poor countries enter the world economy on unequal terms, and is the clearest statement of his central claim.
  • “Imperialist appropriation in the world economy: Drain from the global South through unequal exchange, 1990–2015” (2022). It measures the net flow of embodied resources and labour from South to North over that period, making it the best entry into the evidence behind his central term.
  • “Is Green Growth Possible?” (2020). It asks whether decoupling can happen fast enough to stay within carbon budgets while GDP keeps growing and answers no, making it the clearest short case for degrowth.
  • Less Is More: How Degrowth Will Save the World (2020). It argues that degrowth means cutting excess resource and energy use while improving well-being rather than simply shrinking output, making it the best book-length account of his remedy.
  • “Capitalism and extreme poverty: a global analysis of real wages, human height, and mortality since the long 16th century” (2022). It uses those measures to challenge the claim that capitalism ended extreme poverty, making it the best entry into his historical argument.

Selected lectures and interviews#

The most substantial verified lecture text is the speech “China, unequal exchange, and the present world-historic juncture”, delivered to a Fudan University forum on “Socialist Perspectives on Global Governance in a Multipolar World” and published by Progressive International on 18 April 2025 (speech record). The full text was opened. It is the clearest single statement of his argument about the imperial arrangement and about what he calls the second stage of decolonisation.

The LSE Ralph Miliband Programme listed a public event, “Capitalism, climate change and inequality: what is the way forward?”, for 18 May 2026, with Hickel and Branko Milanović as speakers and Robin Archer as chair (LSE event record). The event page states that the two scholars’ discussions on degrowth “have sparked global attention”. The recording was not opened, so no content from the event is quoted here.

Two long interviews were opened directly. Samuel Miller McDonald’s “Ecosocialism is the Horizon, Degrowth is the Way” appeared in The Trouble on 11 February 2021 (interview). “Degrowth Is About Global Justice” appeared in the Green European Journal on 5 January 2022 (interview). Hickel’s own site lists many further interviews and podcasts, including a BBC Viewsnight item titled “Our addiction to economic growth is killing us” and episodes of Upstream, Macro n Cheese, The Breakdown Journal and Green European Journal (media page). Those audio endpoints were not opened, so their dates and content are not independently verified beyond the host and year given on his own page.

Intellectual influences#

The influences documented from Hickel’s own words fall into three groups. The first is the unequal-exchange and dependency tradition. In the Fudan speech he names Samir Amin and “Roy Mauro Marini” (his spelling of Ruy Mauro Marini) as Global South economists who described unequal exchange as a continuation of imperialist appropriation without colonial occupation. His 2024 afterword refers to “early theorists of ‘unequal exchange’” whose methods underpin the newer quantitative work, and recommends Utsa and Prabhat Patnaik’s Capital and Imperialism, John Smith’s Imperialism in the 21st Century and Intan Suwandi’s Value Chains. Arghiri Emmanuel’s Unequal Exchange appears in the reference list of the scholarly critique of Hickel’s own work, and Emmanuel’s framework is part of the lineage Hickel invokes (Somerville article, full text).

The second is the anti-colonial and ecological political tradition. In the 2022 Green European Journal interview Hickel traces degrowth to Gandhi, Fanon and Thomas Sankara, and to the 2010 People’s Agreement of Cochabamba, the Vía Campesina food sovereignty movement, the Red Deal, the Managua Declaration and the Anchorage Declaration. In the Trouble interview he names Murray Bookchin, James C. Scott, David Graeber, Ursula Le Guin, Winona LaDuke, Ailton Krenak, Nemonte Nenquimo and Robin Kimmerer, along with the Red Nation’s slogan “All Relatives Forever”. These are not casual name-checks: each is attached to a specific argument about hierarchy, local ecological knowledge, commons or rights of nature.

The third is the empirical and scholarly community around him. His co-authors Giorgos Kallis, Dylan Sullivan, Andrew Fanning, Daniel O’Neill, Kate Raworth, Tim Jackson and Julia Steinberger appear throughout his research list, and his own writing treats their work as evidence for particular claims rather than as inherited doctrine. He has also said that he cites Branko Milanović’s work on inequality constantly, even while rejecting Milanović’s critique of degrowth (Hickel’s response).

Influence and legacy#

Within academic economics his measurable footprint is substantial but should be described carefully. Crossref recorded 1,591 citations to “Is Green Growth Possible?” when the registry entry was retrieved on 26 November 2026 (Crossref record). That is a registry count, not a verified measure of intellectual influence, and it is not evidence that the paper’s conclusions are accepted.

Within public debate, his books have been translated into many languages. His own site lists editions of The Divide in German, Italian, Macedonian, Chinese, Taiwanese, Romanian, Korean, Czech, Spanish, Turkish and Arabic, and editions of Less Is More in French, Italian, Polish, Belgian, German, Korean, Czech, Chinese, Turkish, Japanese, Spanish, Taiwanese, Estonian, Serbian and Slovenian (book pages; Less Is More page). Penguin describes Less Is More as an international bestseller and a Financial Times Book of the Year (Penguin book record).

Institutional recognition includes the ICREA chair, the ERC Synergy grant, and seats on the US National Academy of Sciences Climate and Macroeconomics Roundtable, the Lancet commissions and the Rodney Commission. His 2025 debate with Milanović at the LSE is a useful marker of where his arguments now sit: no longer confined to degrowth conferences but staged inside a mainstream economics institution as a contest over the shape of climate policy (LSE event record). This profile does not claim that the debate’s content was checked against a recording.

Criticisms and controversies#

The most direct critique from a development economist came from Branko Milanović in a post on his globalinequality blog on 18 November 2017, “The illusion of ‘degrowth’ in a poor and unequal world” (original post; indexed with a chronology of the exchange by degrowth.info). Milanović asked what would happen if global GDP were fixed at its current level and poor countries were allowed to converge towards Western median incomes. On his arithmetic this would require cutting Western production and income by roughly two-thirds; factories, trains and schools would work a third of their normal time. He called this “the immiseration of the West” and concluded that “Degrowing is not the way to go”, recommending instead taxes on emission-intensive goods and technological change. He explicitly described the calculations as provisional, and the piece is a blog post rather than a peer-reviewed article, so its evidential weight is limited. Hickel replied twice, arguing that Milanović had mistaken GDP for welfare, had assumed a one-to-one relation between income and well-being, and had ignored the distinction between necessary and unnecessary sectors (Hickel’s first response).

A second critique came from within the Marxist and anti-imperialist tradition. Peter Somerville of the University of Lincoln published “A Critique of Ecologically Unequal Exchange Theory” in Capitalism Nature Socialism 33(1), published online 5 December 2021 (Crossref record; full text mirror). Somerville’s abstract says the theory is “confused, internally inconsistent, and misrepresents the nature of global extractivism and labour exploitation”. He takes Hickel, Sullivan and Zoomkawala’s 2021 paper as a “good example” of the confusion. His specific objection is that biophysical flows are not the same as transferred value, because the value transferred depends on what was actually paid; that wage differentials reflect different costs of reproducing labour power and cannot by themselves explain global inequality; and that treating low Southern prices as the problem leads to the absurd conclusion that higher food and energy prices would be the solution. He argues instead for analysing value capture in global chains and the super-exploitation of labour. This is a substantive theoretical objection from a shared political starting point, not a defence of the status quo.

Ted Trainer published “What does Degrowth mean? Some comments on Jason Hickel’s ‘A few points of clarification’” in Globalizations 18(7) in 2021 (Crossref record). The publisher’s full text returned an access error, so the substance of Trainer’s argument was not verified; only its title, author, journal and date are confirmed.

The claim about British colonial mortality in India was challenged by the economic historian Tirthankar Roy. Sullivan and Hickel published a detailed reply, which concedes that Roy did not dispute the scale of excess deaths but denies his explanation. They argue that Roy wrongly treated their figures as derived only from the Deccan famines, that northern India also suffered elevated mortality, that the mortality crisis continued into the twentieth century, and that living standards were higher in the Mughal period than under the Raj (Hickel/Sullivan reply). Roy’s original tweets were linked from that page but were not opened, so his case is represented here only as Hickel and Sullivan describe it.

Within the left, Hickel has publicly disputed Robert Pollin’s claim that degrowth cannot work because a ten per cent cut in GDP would cut emissions by only ten per cent. Hickel called that claim “factually incorrect and, I think, intentionally misleading” and argued that the transition to renewable energy, not the elimination of all economic activity, is the mechanism for decarbonisation (Hickel’s reply). The Vox interview in which Pollin made the remark was not opened, so Pollin’s wording is available here only through Hickel’s quotation of it. The Hickel–Hallegatte debate is verified through Crossref metadata only, because the Wiley page returned an access error, and a 2023–2025 exchange in Ecological Economics over “the limits to degrowth” was identified in search results but no full text was opened; no claim is made here about that exchange.

Related thinkers#

Hickel’s closest intellectual partners are Giorgos Kallis, whose work on degrowth and ecological economics underpins his own, and Dylan Sullivan, with whom he wrote the historical poverty and India mortality papers. His empirical work on unequal exchange builds on Christian Dorninger, Hanspeter Wieland, Intan Suwandi, Morena Hanbury Lemos and Felix Barbour, and his work on ecological responsibility on Andrew Fanning and Daniel O’Neill. Kate Raworth, Tim Jackson, Daniel O’Neill, Julia Steinberger, Juliet Schor, Diana Ürge-Vorsatz and Paul Victor appear as co-authors of the 2025 Lancet Planetary Health review.

His principal disagreements, in the sources opened here, are with Branko Milanović over whether degrowth is feasible or desirable, with Robert Pollin over whether a Green New Deal can remain growth-oriented, and with Peter Somerville over what unequal exchange actually measures. He shares an anti-colonial framework with Walter Rodney, Samir Amin and Frantz Fanon, and a critique of development economics with contributors to the dependency and world-systems traditions. No direct comparison between Hickel and these figures was found in the sources opened for this profile, so their names are given without claimed links between their arguments. The profile of Michael Parenti in this collection covers a different strand of anti-imperialist political economy; the sources opened for Hickel record no direct comparison between the two.

Some summaries attach the phrase “imperial mode of living” to Hickel. No paper of that title appears on his research list, and the concept belongs to Ulrich Brand and Markus Wissen. Hickel’s verified vocabulary is “imperial arrangement” and “imperialist appropriation”.
