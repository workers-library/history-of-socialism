John Bellamy Foster: The Metabolic Rift and Ecological Marxism

A research profile of the Marxist sociologist's account of the rift between society and nature, monopoly-finance capital and his critics.

John Bellamy Foster is a Marxist sociologist and the editor of Monthly Review. His central claim is that Karl Marx identified a break in the material exchange between human society and the natural world, and that capitalism deepens it. Foster calls this the metabolic rift: in plain terms, industrial farming and city life draw fertility out of the soil, concentrate human and animal waste in cities, and fail to return what was taken, so that the “metabolism” between people and the earth is disrupted. In his own words, Marx “employed the concept of metabolic rift to capture the material estrangement of human beings in capitalist society from the natural conditions of their existence” (Foster, 1999). The same framework led Foster and Robert W. McChesney to the condition they analyse as monopoly-finance capital: an economy in which a few large firms dominate their industries and accumulate more capital than they can profitably invest, leaving growth dependent on financial activity that cannot last. This profile sets out what Foster argued, what can be checked in his published texts and first-party records, and where critics, including other ecological Marxists, objected.

Biography#

Foster was born in 1953 in Seattle, Washington. Monthly Review’s editors page names his father, William Foster, a schoolteacher who had attended school under the G.I. Bill, and his mother, Patrica Bellamy Foster, an administrative secretary. Both parents were socialists, and Foster describes an informal socialist education at home through his parents and his father’s library. The same page says he was concerned with environmental issues from his earliest years, but that in the late 1960s the urgent question for him was United States imperialism: he gave talks and took part in protests against the Vietnam War and the nuclear arms race while still in high school (Monthly Review, The Editors).

His degrees are recorded in his curriculum vitae and confirmed in part by the University of Oregon: a BA in Liberal Arts from The Evergreen State College in 1975, an MA in Political Science from York University in 1977, and a PhD in Political Science from York University in 1984, with a major area in political theory and a minor in international relations theory. His dissertation, supervised by Robert Albritton, was The Theory of Monopoly Capitalism: An Elaboration of Baran and Sweezy’s Approach to Political Economy. The Oregon directory independently confirms the York PhD and lists him as Professor Emeritus in Sociology (Foster CV; University of Oregon directory). Monthly Review adds that he began studies at Evergreen in 1971, met Robert W. McChesney there and was introduced by him to Monthly Review. The two studied neoclassical and Marxian economics together and helped organise the Northwest Symposium on Chile after the United States-backed coup against Salvador Allende. Foster entered graduate study at York in 1976, studied with Neal Wood and Ellen Meiksins Wood, worked with the historian Gabriel Kolko, and shifted from a “fundamentalist” Marxian political economy towards a more historical and empirical approach that included Michał Kalecki and Joseph Steindl alongside Baran and Sweezy (Monthly Review, The Editors).

After a short period teaching political economy at Evergreen, Foster became a professor of sociology at the University of Oregon, in a post advertised around “Marxism, political economy, class analysis, and imperialism”; according to Monthly Review he taught Marx’s Capital every year for the next forty years. He retired, teaching his last course in 2026, and is now professor emeritus. The two first-party records differ on the editorship. Monthly Review’s public page labels him “Editor (2000–present)” and says he joined the editorial committee in 1988–89 and became a director of the Monthly Review Foundation at that time. His own CV is more specific: member of the Monthly Review editorial advisory committee 1989–99, board member of the Monthly Review Foundation from 1988 and president from 2002, acting coeditor from April 2000 to April 2001, coeditor from April 2001 to December 2005 and editor from January 2006 onwards. Both records agree that he moved into the editorship during the transition from the generation of Paul Sweezy and Harry Magdoff (Monthly Review, The Editors; Foster CV).

One caution about that Monthly Review page: in Foster’s section it says “Sweezy died in 1994 and McChesney left that year,” while the same page heads Sweezy’s entry “Editor 1949-2004” and states in McChesney’s entry that he stepped down as coeditor in 2004. The page is internally inconsistent on this point. Sweezy’s death year was not independently verified in the sources opened for this profile, so the discrepancy is left unresolved here.

Intellectual tradition#

Foster works within the independent Marxian political economy associated with Monthly Review, the tradition of Paul A. Baran, Paul M. Sweezy and Harry Magdoff that treats monopoly, stagnation and financialisation as central to capitalism rather than as deviations from a competitive norm. His dissertation and first book were written inside that framework. Monthly Review’s editors page says he became close to Sweezy and Magdoff and began writing for the magazine in 1981. His later ecological work extends the same framework into the relation between society and nature: the publisher’s description of The Ecological Rift calls the result a “deep dialectical naturalism concerned with issues of ecology and evolution and their interaction with the economy,” while Foster’s own book pages describe a project that is materialist and dialectical, bridges social and environmental critiques of capitalism, and explicitly opposes ecological modernisation and market-based “green capitalism” (Monthly Review, The Editors; Monthly Review Press, The Ecological Rift; Foster’s website, The Robbery of Nature).

The tradition is also ecosocialist. The Return of Nature is subtitled Socialism and Ecology, and its publisher says it recovers a “long history of the efforts to unite questions of social justice and environmental sustainability.” Monthly Review’s editors page states that the unearthing of Marx’s theory of the metabolic rift in Marx’s Ecology “was to play a key role in the development of ecosocialism worldwide.” That is the journal’s own assessment of its editor’s work, not an independent scholarly finding, and it is attributed accordingly. No explicit passage in the opened sources shows Foster rejecting a label that others applied to him, so the claim that he rejected any particular label is not verified here (Monthly Review, The Editors; Monthly Review Press, The Return of Nature).

Major contributions#

The best documented contribution is his reconstruction of what he calls Marx’s theory of metabolic rift. In the 1999 article he writes that “Marx employed the concept of metabolic rift to capture the material estrangement of human beings in capitalist society from the natural conditions of their existence,” and summarises the textual basis in Capital as “the central theoretical construct … of a ‘rift’ in the ‘metabolic interaction between man and the earth,’ or in the ‘social metabolism prescribed by the natural laws of life,’ through the removal from the soil of its constituent elements, requiring its ‘systematic restoration.’” He traces the argument to Marx’s reading of Justus von Liebig’s soil chemistry, to the antagonism between town and country, to the long-distance trade in food and fibre, and to the pollution of cities by human waste. The article presents this as a historical-environmental materialism taking account of the coevolution of nature and human society, and as a classical foundation for environmental sociology (Foster, American Journal of Sociology, 1999).

A second contribution is the extension of that concept into a general account of capitalism’s ecological crisis. The publisher’s description of The Ecological Rift says the source of the crisis lies in “the paradox of wealth in capitalist society, which expands individual riches at the expense of public wealth, including the wealth of nature,” and that this drives “a huge ecological rift … between human beings and nature,” a rift “irreparable within capitalist society, since integral to its very laws of motion.” Foster and Brett Clark later separated the argument into two moments: capitalism as a “robbery system” and the resulting “irreparable rift in the metabolism between humanity and nature,” extended to an “anthropogenic rift in the Earth System” and to what they call a corporeal rift in living beings themselves (Monthly Review Press, The Ecological Rift; Foster and Clark, The Robbery of Nature).

The third contribution belongs to political economy rather than ecology. With Robert W. McChesney, Foster argued that the long stagnation of the world economy after 2007–09 was not a temporary failure but a tendency of monopoly-finance capital. Monthly Review Press describes the mechanism as increasing monopolisation, producing an over-abundance of capital and too few profitable investment opportunities, with financialisation offering only a temporary respite. The authors’ own 2012 article opens: “The Great Financial Crisis and the Great Recession began in the United States in 2007 and quickly spread across the globe, marking what appears to be a turning point in world history,” and identifies long-run stagnation, rather than financial crisis or recession alone, as the central question (Monthly Review Press, The Endless Crisis; Foster and McChesney, Monthly Review, May 2012).

Analysis of capitalism#

Foster’s account of capitalism joins two arguments. The first is the ecological one: capitalism breaks the material exchange between humanity and the soil, cannot return the constituents of food and clothing to the land, and turns every limit into a barrier to be overcome. In the 1999 article he writes that capitalist agriculture “prevents any truly rational application of the new science of soil management,” and that progress in capitalist agriculture is “a progress in the art, not only of robbing the worker, but of robbing the soil.” He treats the degradation of soil and the accumulation of human and animal waste in cities as two indications of the same rift, reflected in the antagonism of town and country, and notes that the pattern was later reproduced, in more distorted form, in the Soviet Union (Foster, 1999).

The second argument concerns accumulation. In The Endless Crisis, monopoly means that a handful of large firms dominate industries and generate surplus capital with too few profitable outlets; the economy depends on financial activity to realise profits, and that dependence cannot last. The publisher’s description traces the resulting “Great Stagnation” to slow growth, high unemployment, weak wages and benefits, increased poverty and continuing inequality. Foster’s wider work adds imperialism to this picture: the 2012 article treats China’s expansion as the one bright spot while questioning its stability, and the publisher’s list of his books includes Naked Imperialism, Imperialism in the Anthropocene and the co-edited Pox Americana (Monthly Review Press, The Endless Crisis; Foster and McChesney, 2012).

Crucially, Foster does not present the ecological crisis as an external cost that capitalism could price or regulate away. The publisher’s description of The Ecological Rift says the rift is “irreparable within capitalist society” because it is built into the system’s laws of motion, and rejects both market-based strategies and technological fixes as sufficient. Philip Lauro’s review of the book, reposted in excerpt by Monthly Review, reports that the authors use the Jevons paradox to argue that efficiency gains can raise total energy use and waste, so that technological innovation helps in the short term but cannot resolve the underlying contradiction. The full review was not opened for this profile (Monthly Review Press, The Ecological Rift; Lauro, 2013, excerpt).

Analysis of socialism#

Foster writes no detailed blueprint of institutions in the records opened for this profile, and the claim that he proposed a specific constitutional or planning model is not verified. What the sources give is the direction of his argument. The publisher describes The Return of Nature as ending with a call for “a long, ecological revolution, aimed at making peace with the planet while meeting collective human needs,” and as an effort to recover the radical and socialist origins of ecology from William Morris and Engels through Joseph Needham, Rachel Carson and Stephen Jay Gould. The publisher’s description of The Ecological Rift speaks of “moving beyond the regime of capital and toward a society of sustainable human development.” Foster and Clark’s description of The Robbery of Nature states that “No one, they conclude, following Marx, owns the earth. Instead we must maintain it for future generations and the innumerable, diverse inhabitants of the planet as part of a process of sustainable human development.” In the 1999 article the socialist content appears as the need for the “systematic restoration” of the metabolic relation and the overcoming of the antagonism between town and country. These are stated aims and theoretical commitments, not evidence about how any actually existing socialist institution functioned (Monthly Review Press, The Return of Nature; Monthly Review Press, The Ecological Rift; Foster and Clark, The Robbery of Nature; Foster, 1999).

Recommended reading#

These selections are starting points, not a list of everything Foster has published.

  • “Marx’s Theory of Metabolic Rift: Classical Foundations for Environmental Sociology” (1999). It reconstructs Marx’s idea of a rift in the metabolism between people and the earth from his reading of Liebig, the antagonism of town and country and long-distance trade, making it the shortest route into the central concept.
  • The Ecological Rift: Capitalism’s War with the Earth (2010). It argues that the rift between humanity and nature is built into capitalism’s laws of motion and cannot be repaired inside it, making it the best guide to his general theory of ecological crisis.
  • The Endless Crisis: How Monopoly-Finance Capital Produces Stagnation and Upheaval from the USA to China (2012). With McChesney it argues that monopoly leaves capital with too few profitable outlets, so stagnation and financialisation become central, making it the best entry into the political-economy side of his work.
  • The Return of Nature: Socialism and Ecology (2020). It recovers the socialist origins of ecology from Morris and Engels to Carson and Gould and ends with a call for a long ecological revolution, making it the clearest statement of where his argument points.

Selected lectures and interviews#

Foster’s CV and his website’s interview archive list a number of recorded items. None of the videos was played for the research behind this profile, so the listings establish title, host or venue and date, but not the content of the recording. A keynote address, “The Great Rift: Capitalism and the Metabolism of Nature and Production,” was given for the Rosa Luxemburg Foundation in Berlin on 28 May 2013. Monthly Review posted a page offering the video; that page was opened, but the article body did not render, so the title is taken from the CV and the page title (Foster CV; Monthly Review post).

Foster’s website lists “Epicurus and Marx,” a Critical Theory Workshop public lecture, as given in 2004 with the video posted 11 March 2025, but the heading of the same entry says 2024, so the year of the lecture itself is not reliably established from that page. The same archive records “The Physics of Capitalism” book launch, with Erald Kolasi and Blair Fix, at Monthly Review on 9 March 2025, and “Western Marxism and Imperialism,” with Gabriel Rockhill, at the Critical Theory Workshop in March 2025 (Foster’s website, interviews category).

“The New Denial of Imperialism on the Left,” recorded for 99 Media in Italy, appears in one entry with both 28 June 2025 and 28 July 2025, so no single date is settled; “Why MAGA Is Not Working Class,” also for 99 Media, is dated 1 December 2025. “Critical Realism and Human-Nature Relations: An Interview with John Bellamy Foster” appeared in the Journal of Critical Realism on 18 January 2026; Foster’s site links to a copy of it, but that copy was not opened for this profile (Foster’s website, interviews category).

The CV also lists printed interviews: “Marx’s Ecology: An Interview with John Bellamy Foster” by Michael Colby (Food & Water Journal, Summer 2000); “Ecology, Capitalism and the Socialization of Nature: An Interview with John Bellamy Foster” (Monthly Review 56(6), November 2004, pp. 1–12); “Climate Change and Socialism,” interviewed by Steve da Silva (Alternate Routes, 2014); and “‘The Present as History’ and the Theory of Monopoly Capital,” interviewed by Grzegorz Konat (Monthly Review 69(9), February 2019, pp. 11–14). These are bibliographic records, not opened interview texts (Foster CV).

Intellectual influences#

The influences are documented by first-party accounts rather than inferred from similarity of ideas. Monthly Review’s editors page says Foster’s parents gave him an early socialist education, that he studied neoclassical economics and the Marxian economics of Baran and Sweezy’s Monopoly Capital at Evergreen, that he studied the history of political theory with Neal Wood and Ellen Meiksins Wood at York, and that Gabriel Kolko pushed him towards a historical and empirical frame. It names Michał Kalecki and Joseph Steindl as influences on his monopoly-capital work and records his close friendship with Sweezy and Magdoff (Monthly Review, The Editors). The 1999 article confirms the intellectual sources of the ecological argument: Marx’s reading of Liebig’s agricultural chemistry, Marx’s letters to Engels, and the subsequent treatments by Kautsky, Lenin and Bukharin. The same article acknowledges Paul Burkett, Harry Magdoff, Paul Sweezy, Ellen Meiksins Wood and Robert McChesney among others for creating the “climate of intellectual exchange” behind the work (Foster, 1999). The publisher’s description of Marx’s Ecology names Epicurus, Darwin, Malthus, Feuerbach, Proudhon and Paley as thinkers covered in the book, and the description of The Return of Nature extends the line through William Morris, Engels, Joseph Needham, Rachel Carson and Stephen Jay Gould (Monthly Review Press, Marx’s Ecology; Monthly Review Press, The Return of Nature).

Influence and legacy#

Within ecosocialism, the claim that the recovery of the metabolic rift mattered comes from Monthly Review itself: its editors page says the unearthing of the theory in Marx’s Ecology “was to play a key role in the development of ecosocialism worldwide.” The awards are independently recorded in first-party sources. Marx’s Ecology won the best book award of the Marxist Sociological Section of the American Sociological Association in 2000; The Ecological Rift won the Gerald L. Young Book Award of the Society for Human Ecology in 2010; and The Return of Nature won the 2020 Isaac and Tamara Deutscher Memorial Prize (Monthly Review, The Editors; Foster’s website, Marx’s Ecology; Foster’s website, The Ecological Rift; Foster CV; Monthly Review Press, The Return of Nature).

William Sacher’s 2022 essay, opened in full, describes the concept as “remarkably successful since its formulation by John Bellamy Foster” and as an important contribution to integrating ecology into Marxism, while recording that it has generated a substantial debate within ecological Marxism, including rejectionist and revisionist positions. Sacher notes that Foster’s collaborators and students, including Brett Clark, Richard York, Rebecca Clausen and Philip Mancus, were described by Jason W. Moore as the “Oregon School” — a usage attributed to Moore in 2011 and reported by Sacher. The legacy is therefore that of an influential concept whose empirical foundations remain contested, rather than a settled result (Sacher, 2022).

Criticisms and controversies#

The earliest substantive criticisms were published as a symposium on Marx’s Ecology in Capitalism, Nature, Socialism in 2001, and two of those essays were opened directly. Maarten de Kadt and Salvatore Engel-Di Mauro, in “Failed Promise” (12(2), 2001, pp. 50–56), accept that the book is a contribution to the history of ideas but argue that Foster imports modern ecological concepts into nineteenth-century thought despite saying he will not. They write that “Foster ignores major historical developments in the theory and practice of ecology itself” and that his critique of Green theory fails because he “homogenizes a set of diverse frameworks associated with often opposing political perspectives.” They also object to his reliance on notions of ecological equilibrium, saturation and carrying capacity (De Kadt and Engel-Di Mauro, 2001).

Alan Rudy, in “Marx’s Ecology and Rift Analysis” (12(2), 2001, pp. 56–63), argues that equating ecology with rift analysis “does a major disservice to ecology,” that the concept has more affinity with natural resource economics than with the dialectics of ecological Marxism, and that Foster neglects the general communal conditions of production and the state. On the founding example, Rudy notes that English enclosure was accompanied by a large increase in rural livestock, so that “the problem may have been as much related to the maldistribution of rural wastes as the separation of rural from urban wastes.” He suggests that regional metabolisms were simultaneously oversaturated and depleted rather than simply broken by a one-way flow to the cities (Rudy, 2001).

Sacher’s 2022 survey compiles further criticisms, which are reported here as his summaries rather than as readings of the original articles. Paul Burkett defended the concept in 2001 against what he called a “non-dialectical conception of scientific progress as a unilinear accumulation of knowledge,” arguing that its contribution is a historical-materialist ontology rather than applicability in one context; Victor Wallis called the antagonisms in the debate “fictitious” and the criticisms “tangential.” Jason W. Moore argued in 2001 that the rift offers a powerful view of capitalism’s unsustainability and a succession of rifts corresponding to successive stages of global capitalism, and in 2011 proposed to “transcend” the rift as a falsifiable postulate in favour of historical-ecological materialism. Mindi Schneider and Philip McMichael argued in 2010 that Marx’s original conception was “empirically indefensible” because it idealised pre-capitalist soils and did not treat soil formation as a historical process, and proposed adding knowledge and epistemic rifts. Sacher also reports David Harvey’s dialectical objection that Foster accepts too clear a separation between humanity and nature and is too ready to speak of absolute limits and approaching catastrophe. Sacher’s own conclusion is that the concept has “an obvious lack of empirical foundations” and a “catastrophic” character, that Foster “has not explicitly responded to these criticisms” in his later writings, but that the concept remains theoretically and politically valuable (Sacher, 2022).

A shorter published criticism of the 2010 book comes from Philip Lauro’s review in Science & Society 77(2), April 2013. The excerpt reposted by Monthly Review notes an “abrupt shift towards theory” in the book’s third section; the full review was not opened. None of these objections establishes that the metabolic rift is false. They are recorded here because the profile must present the debate as well as the claim (Lauro, 2013, excerpt).

Related thinkers#

Paul A. Baran, Paul M. Sweezy and Harry Magdoff supplied the monopoly-capital framework in which Foster began working and which he continues to edit and defend at Monthly Review. Robert W. McChesney was his friend from Evergreen, a fellow coeditor and co-author of The Endless Crisis and Pox Americana. Fred Magdoff co-authored The Great Financial Crisis, What Every Environmentalist Needs to Know About Capitalism and Grand Theft Capital. Brett Clark and Richard York co-authored Critique of Intelligent Design and The Ecological Rift; Clark later co-authored The Robbery of Nature with Foster and became associate editor of Monthly Review. Paul Burkett co-authored Marx and the Earth and defended the metabolic rift against its critics, while disagreeing with aspects of Foster’s reading of Marx’s value theory in other work, which was not opened for this profile. Ellen Meiksins Wood was Foster’s teacher at York, a fellow Monthly Review coeditor between 1997 and 2000, and co-editor of two books with him. István Mészáros’s Beyond Leviathan was edited by Foster. Jason W. Moore, Mindi Schneider, Philip McMichael, Alan Rudy, Maarten de Kadt and Salvatore Engel-Di Mauro are critical interlocutors rather than collaborators, and David Harvey represents a different, less catastrophic tendency within dialectical ecology. Justus von Liebig, Karl Marx, Friedrich Engels, Charles Darwin and Epicurus are the principal figures whose texts Foster reconstructs.
