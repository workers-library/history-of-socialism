Michael Parenti: Class Power, Imperialism and the Politics of Anti-Communism

A research profile of the American political scientist: his class analysis, media critique, account of fascism and defence of existing socialist states, with his critics.

Michael Parenti wrote about power in a plain, direct way that made him one of the most widely read left-wing authors in the United States. He argued that democratic institutions could not be understood apart from the property interests they protected, that mass media served their owners rather than the public, and that the standard account of twentieth-century communism ignored both the pressures socialist states faced and the material gains they made. Those arguments made him influential and controversial. This profile sets out what he claimed, what can be checked in his published texts, and where historians and other socialists objected.

Biography#

Parenti was raised in an Italian-American working-class family in New York City. After high school he worked for several years, then returned to study, earning a BA from City College of New York, an MA from Brown University and a PhD in political science from Yale University (Michael Parenti Political Archive, biography). His own biography gives no birth year; obituary reporting gives 30 September 1933, and this should be read as reported by obituaries rather than confirmed by his archive.

He taught at colleges and universities in the United States and abroad, with fellowships from the Ford Foundation, the Social Science Research Council and other bodies, and a visiting fellowship at the Institute for Policy Studies in Washington. According to a memoir by Christopher D. Cook, his teaching career was disrupted in 1970 after he was struck by police while protesting the shootings at Kent State; he left the University of Illinois, moved to the University of Vermont and was dismissed there after a conviction connected with the protest (Cook, The Progressive, 2026). He then worked mainly as a writer and lecturer, and stood for Congress in Vermont in 1974 on the Liberty Union ticket. His archive records a Certificate of Special Congressional Recognition in 2007 and an American Book Award lifetime achievement award in 2014 (archive biography). He died on 24 January 2026 in Amherst, Massachusetts, aged ninety-two (Democracy Now!, 2026).

Intellectual tradition#

Parenti wrote as a Marxist political scientist and public intellectual rather than as a university-based scholar after the early 1970s. His archive does not state a party affiliation, and this profile makes no claim about one. Cook places his formation in the Old Left of the 1930s to 1960s rather than the student New Left; that is Cook’s interpretation, not a fact established by Parenti’s own records.

His arguments were built against three targets. The first was liberal accounts of American politics that treat government as a neutral referee. The second was what he called left anti-communism: the habit, as he saw it, of judging communist states by standards that critics did not apply to capitalist ones. The third was the study of fascism that described its ideology but avoided questions about who benefited from its class policies. In an excerpt from Blackshirts and Reds he wrote that most literature on fascism “say[s] little if anything about the class policies of fascist Italy and Nazi Germany. How did these regimes deal with social services, taxes, business, and the conditions of labor? For whose benefit and at whose expense?” (Parenti, “Rational Fascism”). He shared Chomsky’s interest in media and foreign policy but rejected the libertarian conclusion that existing socialist states were not worth defending. That disagreement is part of the record and is not smoothed over here.

Major contributions#

Five arguments recur in the work. First, Democracy for the Few, first published in 1974, connected political institutions to accumulated wealth. Cook quotes the book: “The close relationship between politics and economics is neither neutral nor coincidental. Large governments evolve through history in order to protect large accumulations of property and wealth” (Cook, 2026). The book itself was not opened for this profile, so the words are a reported quotation.

Second, Inventing Reality (1986) treated news media as businesses. Cook quotes Parenti: “As with any business, the mass media’s first obligation is to make money for their owners,” and owners decide “which person, which facts, which version of the facts, and which ideas shall reach the public” (Cook, 2026). This connects the media chapter of this book to questions of ownership rather than to bias alone.

Third, the idea of “rational fascism”, developed in Blackshirts and Reds (1997), holds that fascist movements were used when employers and landowners needed to break organised labour. Parenti traces money from Italian industrialists to Mussolini’s March on Rome and from German business to the Nazi rise, citing Dutt, Salvemini, Neumann and others in his own footnotes (Parenti, “Rational Fascism”). Those claims are his, with the sourcing he supplies; the underlying histories are treated in this book’s chapters on Germany, Spain and the Popular Front.

Fourth, his definition of imperialism in Against Empire (1995): “the process whereby the dominant politico-economic interests of one nation expropriate for their own enrichment the land, labor, raw materials, and markets of another people” (Parenti, “Imperialism 101”). He argued that countries described as “underdeveloped” were better described as overexploited, and that formal colonial rule had been replaced by control through finance, which he called neoimperialism or neocolonialism.

Fifth, the distinction between “pure socialism” and “siege socialism”. Parenti argued that critics compared an ideal socialism with actually existing states that had formed under military attack, and that the comparison was ahistorical. He did concede internal difficulties in those states; critics argue that he never explained their collapse from their own contradictions (Halpern, 2023).

Analysis of capitalism#

In his own excerpt from Against Empire, Parenti distinguishes older empires from capitalist ones. Emperors and conquistadors sought “plunder and tribute, gold and glory”; capitalist imperialism “systematically accumulates capital through the organized exploitation of labor and the penetration of overseas markets” (Parenti, “Imperialism 101”). He argues that capitalism is driven to expand because investors must reinvest in order to make more money, quoting Marx and Engels on the bourgeoisie that “chases over the whole surface of the globe”.

He is careful about necessity. Capitalism might survive without empire, he writes, but “it shows no inclination to do so”; imperial relations “may not be the only way to pursue profits, but they are the most lucrative way” (Parenti, “Imperialism 101”). That is a claim about strong tendency, not a proof that empire is logically required. His description of a “comprador class” that cooperates in turning its own country into a client state gives the mechanism a human agent, and connects his work to the anti-colonial chapters of this book.

In Blackshirts and Reds the same class lens is applied to fascism: when workers had won concessions and employers wanted wage cuts and subsidies, Parenti argues that the ruling interests chose “someone at the helm who could break the power of organized workers” (Parenti, “Rational Fascism”). This is an explanatory claim about why fascism was supported, not an account of every voter who backed it.

Analysis of socialism#

Parenti did not publish a detailed blueprint of a future socialist economy in the sources opened for this profile. His positive case is historical and defensive. He argues that the communist states achieved rapid industrialisation, reduced economic inequality, gave priority to public services and supported national liberation movements, and that the transition to markets after 1989 brought severe social costs (City Lights Books, description of Blackshirts and Reds). He calls these states examples of “siege socialism” and argues that their centralised institutions cannot be judged against a model that never faced invasion, blockade and sabotage.

Readers should keep two things apart. The first is what Parenti claimed about those states. The second is what the evidence establishes about them. This book treats the achievements and the coercion in separate chapters, including the collectivisation, gulag and workplace chapters. Parenti’s book is a polemic in a debate about how that record should be weighed; it is not a substitute for the record itself.

Recommended reading#

These selections are starting points rather than a complete account of Parenti’s output.

  • Democracy for the Few (eighth edition 2007; ninth edition 2011) — Connects political institutions to accumulated wealth and argues that large governments evolve to protect large accumulations of property; the best starting point for his class analysis of the state.
  • Inventing Reality: The Politics of News Media (second edition 1993) — Treats news media as businesses whose owners decide which facts and ideas reach the public; the clearest entry into his media-ownership critique.
  • Against Empire (City Lights Books, 1995) — Defines imperialism as the expropriation of another people’s land, labour, raw materials and markets, and argues that “underdeveloped” countries are overexploited through finance rather than formal colonial rule; the best starting point for his account of capitalism.
  • Blackshirts and Reds: Rational Fascism and the Overthrow of Communism (City Lights Books, 1997) — Develops the idea of “rational fascism”, in which fascism is used to break organised labour, and sets out his defence of “siege socialism”; the clearest entry into both his class analysis of fascism and his case for existing communist states.

Selected lectures and interviews#

The author’s archive points to “The Michael Parenti Library” on YouTube (youtube.com/@themichaelparentilibrary) as the public collection of his talks, and lists subjects including US politics and imperialism, ideology and class power, media manipulation, fascism and communism, and historiography (archive, recorded talks). Cook quotes a 1986 University of Colorado speech in which Parenti said that “these countries are not underdeveloped, they are over-exploited” (Cook, 2026). The words come from a secondary report, not from a transcript checked against the recording; no individual lecture date or URL is claimed here beyond the archive channel.

Intellectual influences#

Parenti’s own footnotes name Gaetano Salvemini, R. Palme Dutt, Franz Neumann, Daniel Guérin and James and Suzanne Pool as sources on fascism (Parenti, “Rational Fascism”). In Against Empire he quotes Marx and Engels, Engels on Ireland, and Frances Moore Lappé and Rachel Schurman on fertility and social provision (Parenti, “Imperialism 101”). These are documented citations, not inferred affinities. Where he drew on Lenin or Mao, the archive excerpts do not establish the extent of that debt, so this profile does not claim it.

Influence and legacy#

Cook describes Parenti as one of the most influential writers on the American left over half a century and names Democracy for the Few and Inventing Reality as his durable works (Cook, 2026). A left review written after his death records renewed interest among younger readers (Halpern, 2023). Both are judgments by the cited writers. No sales or circulation figures were verified, and none are given here.

Criticisms and controversies#

The New York Times obituary reported that Parenti was accused of being an apologist for brutal regimes, including the Soviet Union and Yugoslavia; the obituary itself returned an access error when checked, so this is recorded as reported criticism, with the primary wording unverified here (quoted in Cook, 2026).

A sharper objection came from another current of the left. Max Halpern argues that Parenti leaves out the sectarian “social fascist” policy of the German Communist Party before 1933, presents the revolutionary left in Italy and Germany as too weak to take power, and never explains the Soviet collapse by internal contradictions (Halpern, 2023). Halpern’s critique is a political argument, not a scholarly finding, and is identified as such. A sympathetic review by Stan Goff likewise notes that the book’s factual claims are not all documented equally (Goff, 1998); the excerpt opened for this profile does carry footnotes, so the defensible statement is that documentation varies.

The disagreement matters for this collection. Parenti’s value is that he forces questions about class power, media ownership and the double standards of anti-communism. It does not follow that his accounts of communist states are correct, or that his dismissals of anarchist and libertarian critics settle their arguments. Readers should use the historical chapters of this book to test the claims.

Related thinkers#

  • Noam Chomsky — converges on media and foreign policy; diverges on the defence of existing socialist states and on the “pure socialist” critique.
  • Domenico Losurdo — a similar defence of historical communist projects and a similar critique of liberalism, written as counter-history.
  • Edward S. Herman — a more formal account of the media mechanism Parenti describes through ownership.
  • Michael Hudson and Paul Sweezy — overlapping political economy of finance and monopoly; no direct comparison was found in the sources opened here.
  • Howard Zinn — grouped with Parenti as a popular left historian, though their methods differ and were not compared here.
