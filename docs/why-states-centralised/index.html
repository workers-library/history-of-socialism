Why States Centralised

From Kronstadt to the Prague Spring, revolutionary governments concentrated authority. Structural pressure, institutional design and deliberate coercion have to be separated.

On 28 February 1921 the crews of two battleships at Kronstadt met to ask whether a soviet, an elected council of workers’ and soldiers’ deputies, could exist without the Communist Party running it. Their resolution of 1 March demanded new soviet elections “by secret ballot, with freedom to carry on agitation beforehand for all workers and peasants”, equal rations and peasant freedom over land (resolution). Red troops crossed the ice on 16 and 17 March. On 16 March the Tenth Congress adopted “On Party Unity”, citing the uprising as proof that a faction, an organised group within the party campaigning for a distinct programme, helped counter-revolution (congress resolution; Kronstadt). Some centralisation answered war and hunger. Some was written into rules and institutions by people who chose it. Some was imposed by force on socialists who objected.

The material pressures were real. Civil war armies lived off the country they were trying to govern: Makhno complained that Red Guards advanced along railways while villages defended themselves, and the White general Wrangel recorded that “The excessive and disorderly requisitioning by the troops had completed the economic disruption” (Makhno; Wrangel). Compulsory grain requisitioning was meant to feed cities and soldiers but fell unevenly. Krestinsky told Arthur Ransome it was intended for “the propertied classes only”, while some districts assessed “rich and poor peasants” alike (Ransome). Ethel Snowden heard peasants call Lenin “a name with which to conjure out of both rich and poor peasant the stocks of food they are believed to be hiding” (Snowden). Famine and peasant resistance forced the tax in kind that replaced requisitioning in 1921 (Mann; Beeuwkes). In 1930 a secret police report from Dnipropetrovsk recorded a worker saying “All our newspapers lie about the gains of collectivization. In practice, everything is falling apart” (REESOURCES). Elsewhere the pattern was war and siege: the Chinese Red Army survived the Long March with heavy losses (Mao); Trường Chinh justified the haste of Vietnam’s 1945 rising by the risk of a restored French administration (Trường Chinh); Cuba faced blockade, invasion and the October missile crisis (Castro; Ocasio). These conditions explain why leaders valued command. They do not explain the forms command took.

Centralisation was also designed. Democratic centralism, the rule that local bodies may discuss policy but must carry out central decisions, was written into the Soviet constitution, which “combines central leadership with local initiative and creative activity” (Article 3). Lenin grounded “iron discipline” and “absolute centralisation” in small property, which “engenders capitalism and the bourgeoisie continuously, daily, hourly, spontaneously, and on a mass scale” (Lenin). Stalin stated the operating rule: nothing important was decided without “guiding directives from the Party”, and “In the U.S.S.R. there is ground only for one party, the Communist Party” (Stalin 1926; Stalin 1936). The official textbook called planned development “an economic law of socialism” (Political Economy). The security apparatus was an institution: the Cheka issued decrees ordering named numbers of hostages shot in 1918, and NKVD Order 00447 of 30 July 1937 gave each region quotas, dividing victims between shooting and eight to ten years in camp (Melgunov; Order 00447). Cuba merged its revolutionary organisations into the ORI, then PURS, then the Communist Party; the 1976 Constitution made the party the “superior leading force of society and the state” in Article 5 (Castro 1961; Castro 1965; Raúl Castro 2021). In Budapest the ÁVH fired on a crowd at the Radio Building on 23 October 1956 (UN report). Prague’s Action Programme of April 1968 kept the party’s leading role, while five senior officials wrote secretly to Brezhnev that “The very existence of socialism in our country is under threat” (Action Programme; secret letter).

Socialists objected from within the revolutionary parties. Luxemburg wrote from prison in 1918 that “Freedom only for the supporters of the government, only for the members of one party, however numerous they may be, is no freedom at all”, while calling the Bolsheviks’ work an “immortal historical service” and blaming isolation and Germany’s defeat for the distortions she saw (Luxemburg VI; VIII). Kollontai traced the Workers’ Opposition “to the depths of the industrial proletariat of Soviet Russia” and wrote that “Bureaucracy is a direct negation of mass self-activity” (Kollontai I; III). Pannekoek called minority rule “Blanquism”, after the French revolutionary Blanqui, and described Soviet industry as a system where “the directors of industry are appointed by the state” (Pannekoek 1920; 1936). Critics who wrote from outside the parties made a related case. Havel argued in 1978 that a regime which had stopped mass repression still ruled through ritual: the greengrocer’s slogan arrived “along with the onions and carrots”, and by displaying it “individuals confirm the system, fulfill the system, make the system, are the system” (Havel; Living in Truth). Solzhenitsyn’s novella counted the minutes a prisoner could call his own, and his Nobel lecture said “Woe to that nation whose literature is disturbed by the intervention of power” (Solzhenitsyn; lecture). These critics disagreed with one another: Sakharov treated Marxist ideology as “a convenient facade”, Solzhenitsyn as “the evil-smelling root of present-day Soviet life” (Chronicle, no. 35). Farber connected Cuba’s bureaucratic centralisation to its economic failures and its record on Black Cubans, homosexuals and dissidents (Farber).

Coercion is established, and the structural account does not excuse it. Red troops crushed Kronstadt after two failed assaults; the claim that a Tsarist general directed the revolt rests mainly on belligerents’ communiqués, while the demands are documented from several directions (Trotsky; Avrich). Khrushchev told the Twentieth Congress that of 139 Central Committee members and candidates elected in 1934, 98 were arrested and shot, that 1,108 of 1,966 congress delegates were arrested, and that torture was used and accusations fabricated (Khrushchev). Archival estimates of executions differ by period and category, but most victims were ordinary people selected by quota (Getty; Werth). The 1936 constitution promised rights and their guarantee, yet Ksenia Chekina, stripped of her passport, wrote “I know the Constitution, and somehow this is not working out that way” (constitution; Chekina). Soviet troops entered Czechoslovakia on 20–21 August 1968 and the protocol of 26 August was signed under threat of military dictatorship (Mlynář). Cuba’s UMAP camps held homosexuals, religious believers and political nonconformists, with dates and numbers disputed (Ocasio). Charter 77, a petition built on covenants the state had signed, was answered with proceedings for “subversion” and “sedition” (Charter; prosecutors’ opinion).

What remains contested is the weight of each cause and the judgement on necessity. Whether force at Kronstadt was avoidable, whether the rising was counter-revolutionary in effect, and its class character are live questions, and the conspiracy claim is the weakest documented part of the record (Avrich). Kádár’s “White Terror” and the UN committee’s finding of a “spontaneous national uprising” describe real violence but disagree on who was terrorising whom and on whether it justified a second invasion (UN report; Khrushchev, Presidium, 31 October 1956). Historians dispute how much consent existed inside coercion: Kotkin, Siegelbaum, Fitzpatrick and Lomb find agency within the plan, Thurston finds more room for influence over norms and housing, and the older totalitarian reading finds little of either (Kotkin; Siegelbaum; Thurston; Everyday Stalinism). None of this licenses the claim that repression was harmless or unavoidable.

Limits of the record#

Evidence for coercion is stronger than evidence for motives. No Russian-language archive was opened for the civil war, Kronstadt lacks rank-and-file voices and its casualty figures have no stated method (Civil War; Avrich). Police reports and party commissions record what the state noticed, not a complete death toll (Werth). Hungarian and Czechoslovak documents reach English through translations, and the Prague record contains editorial summaries and a noisy protocol scan (KGB transcript; UN report). No source measures how many citizens privately accepted the system. What can be established is the sequence of decisions, the rules adopted and the documented use of force. The necessity of each act of force is a judgement the documents do not deliver.

For the documentary basis see Kronstadt 1921: The Sailors and the State, Bread, Rifles and Fear, The “state capitalist” debate and the theory of transition, Stalin: The Complete History and Writings, Everyday life under Stalin, Budapest 1956, Socialism with a Human Face, After the Victory, Fidel Castro and Living in Truth.
