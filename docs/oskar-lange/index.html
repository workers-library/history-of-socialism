Oskar Lange: Market Socialism and the Calculation Debate

A research profile of the Polish economist's model of a socialist pricing board, its debate with Mises and Hayek, and later criticism of the model.

Oskar Lange was the Polish economist whose essays of 1936 and 1937 became the standard answer to the claim that a socialist economy could not work out how to use its resources without markets. His market socialism — the view that a socialist economy could keep prices and markets while the state owned the main means of production — rested on trial and error pricing: setting a price, watching whether buyers wanted more than sellers offered, and changing it until supply and demand matched. He wrote in several registers at once, as a Marxian economist, a user of neoclassical price theory and a participant in Polish public life. This profile sets out what he argued, what can be checked in the texts, and where later writers objected.

Biography#

The existing chapter on economic calculation covers the Soviet textbook claim that planning was an economic law, Stalin’s 1952 distinction between commodity production and the law of value, Soviet economic accounting, cybernetics and the Chilean network, and the incentive problem, and states that Mises, Hayek, Lange and Lerner are absent from its source base, while this profile supplies Lange’s own texts, the primary statements of Mises and Hayek, and the scholarly criticism that grew around them.

Oskar Ryszard Lange was born on 27 July 1904 in Tomaszów Mazowiecki, Poland, and died in London on 2 October 1965. These dates and the place of birth come from the University of Chicago’s finding aid to his papers, which calls him “Oskar Richard Lange” and names his parents Arthur and Sophie (Rosner) Lange, and which also gives the date and place of death (University of Chicago finding aid, Primary Official). A Polish biographical essay read only as a search-index preview gives the birthplace as Tomaszów Lubelski and says he died in Westminster Hospital after surgery following a recurrence of childhood bone tuberculosis. The discrepancy over the birthplace was not resolved, and this profile uses the Chicago record (Winczewski, 2023, Scholarly Secondary, preview only). The title page of the 1938 volume identifies him as “Lecturer in Economics, University of California; Lecturer (on leave) at the University of Cracow and at the Polish Free University in Warsaw” (1938 volume, contents page, Primary Theoretical).

The Chicago finding aid records that he attended the University of Kraków, taking a B.A. in 1926 and a Master of Law in 1928, worked at the Ministry of Labor in Warsaw from 1926 to 1927, and held a research assistantship at Kraków from 1927 to 1931. He was an honorary lecturer in statistics at Kraków in 1931, lecturer in economics at Michigan in 1936, honorary lecturer at Kraków again in 1936, honorary lecturer at the Polish Free University in Warsaw in 1937, and lecturer at California and Stanford in 1937–38. He had visited the United States from 1934 to 1937 under a Rockefeller grant, studying at Harvard and Minnesota before returning to Poland. On 1 July 1938 he began as associate professor of economics at Chicago, a post held until 1945. He and his wife Irene Alice Oderfeld became United States citizens in 1943; their son Christopher Stephen was born in 1940. In 1945 he renounced American citizenship, became a Polish citizen again and was the first Polish ambassador to the United States. The finding aid notes the controversy over his 1944 interview with Stalin, reports that by the mid-1950s he was spoken of as a possible premier of Poland, records his return to Chicago in 1962 to give a talk called “Planning Under Socialism”, and says that by 1962 he was vice chairman of the Polish State Council (finding aid, Primary Official).

The archive is small: a quarter linear foot, one box, containing an untitled manuscript, reprints of the two Review of Economic Studies articles, press releases and one incomplete letter from Maynard Krueger dated 30 January 1941. The manuscript’s chapter titles include “What Socialism Means to Me”, “The Socialist Attitude toward War” and “Strengthening the Economic Foundations of Democracy” (same record). A contemporary obituary in El Trimestre Económico, seen as a search-index preview, adds that Lange belonged to the Polish Academy of Sciences, chaired the United Nations Economic Commission for Europe from 1957 to 1959, sat on the central committee of the Polish United Workers’ Party and chaired Poland’s Economic Council from 1957 to 1962 (obituary, Scholarly Secondary, preview only). The 1963 Monthly Review Press pamphlet names him on its title page as “Chairman, The Planning Committee and The Economic Council, Poland” (pamphlet front matter, Primary Theoretical). These are positions stated by institutional and publisher records; they do not by themselves establish what power he exercised.

Intellectual tradition#

Lange worked in two traditions at once and refused to treat them as enemies. He was a socialist by political commitment, a Marxian economist by training and a user of neoclassical price theory as a tool. In the opening of his 1936 essay he thanked Mises for forcing socialists to recognise “the importance of an adequate system of economic accounting to guide the allocation of resources in a socialist economy”, and proposed a statue of Mises in the “great hall of the Ministry of Socialization or of the Central Planning Board of the socialist state” (1938 volume, pp. 57–58, Primary Theoretical). His preface acknowledges permission from the American Economic Review and the Review of Economic Studies to reprint the papers, and a footnote states that the essay was “Reprinted with additions and some changes from the Review of Economic Studies, Vol. IV, Nos. 1 and 2” (same text, Primary Theoretical).

The intellectual lineage he claimed ran through Pareto and Barone to Fred M. Taylor. In Lippincott’s introduction to the same volume, written by Lange’s editor and not by Lange himself, the argument is put plainly: Barone showed in principle that accounting prices in a socialist economy could be as economically significant as market prices, Taylor showed in 1929 how trial and error could actually find them, and Lange then answered Hayek and Robbins using Taylor’s analysis (Lippincott introduction, pp. 12–15, Primary Theoretical). Lange’s own text credits Taylor directly: “the way in which a socialist economy would solve the problem by a method of trial and error has been indicated quite clearly by Fred M. Taylor in a paper published in 1929” (1938 volume, p. 57, Primary Theoretical). He defined price in the “generalized” sense of “terms on which alternatives are offered”, citing Wicksteed, and treated the market price and the accounting price as two ways of supplying the same information (same text, pp. 59–60, Primary Theoretical; quoted in Cottrell and Cockshott, 1993, Scholarly Secondary).

After returning to Poland his writing moved towards Marxian categories of reproduction and accumulation, and later towards cybernetics. The 1969 English edition of Theory of Reproduction and Accumulation is built on Marx’s schemes of simple and expanded reproduction, which Lange restates using input–output tables; the book’s own text notes that “In Marx’s schemes component v is interpreted in the same way” and later that “In Marx’s schemes, and in later considerations by Lenin, the” analysis is extended (scanned text, Primary Theoretical). His Introduction to Economic Cybernetics is described in its own title matter as “the application of the principles of the theory of automatic control to the problems of managing the economic processes, and particularly the processes in a socialist economy” (publisher preview, Primary Theoretical). Rothbard, writing in 1971 from an Austrian position, argued that Lange’s late Political Economy had moved towards Mises’s praxeology, the formal logic of purposeful action, while still resisting the Misesian conclusion (Rothbard, 1971, Scholarly Secondary). That is Rothbard’s reading of Lange’s late work, not a label Lange accepted.

Major contributions#

The first and best known was the model set out in the two 1936–37 Review of Economic Studies articles and revised in the 1938 book. A central planning board would own the means of production outside labour and fix “accounting prices” for them; consumers would spend their incomes freely, workers would choose occupations, and managers of state enterprises would be required to treat prices as parameters, to choose the combination of factors that minimised average cost, and to expand output until marginal cost equalled the price of the product. The board would then adjust prices until supply and demand balanced. This is the argument in Lange’s own words, not a later summary (1938 volume, pp. 77–88, Primary Theoretical).

The second was the claim that the adjustment could be done without a market in capital goods and without anyone knowing the demand and supply functions in advance. Lange wrote that the board “does not need to have ‘complete lists of the different quantities of all commodities which would be bought at any possible combination of prices of the different commodities which might be available’” and would not have to solve “hundreds of thousands (as Professor Hayek expects) or millions (as Professor Robbins thinks) of equations” (1938 volume, pp. 87–88, Primary Theoretical). Cottrell and Cockshott judge this the tactical strength of the proposal: neoclassical economists who accepted Walrasian theory as an account of capitalism were compelled, “mutatis mutandis”, to accept Langean socialism (Cottrell and Cockshott, 1993, Scholarly Secondary).

The third contribution was a restatement of welfare economics. In “The Foundations of Welfare Economics” (1942) Lange argued that the older idea of total welfare as the sum of individual utilities depended on comparing utilities between persons, which he regarded as lacking operational meaning, and recast the problem as the ordering of a vector of individual utilities subject to a feasibility condition (article text, pp. 215–216, Primary Theoretical). The article’s own opening says it intends “to give a precise statement of the basic assumptions and propositions of welfare economics and to discuss their operational significance” (same source). The publisher record gives volume, issue and pages (Econometric Society record, Primary Official). A history-of-economic-thought reference site describes him as among the first to prove the two welfare theorems, but that site is not used as evidence here and the claim was not independently verified.

The fourth was work on stability and dynamics. The Econometric Society’s 1942 issue contents show two Lange papers side by side: “Theoretical Derivation of Elasticities of Demand and Supply: The Direct Method”, pp. 193–214, and “The Foundations of Welfare Economics”, pp. 215–228 (issue contents, Primary Official). His 1960 paper “The Output-Investment Ratio and Input-Output Analysis” argues that the output–investment ratio and the income–investment ratio are determined by the technological conditions of increasing production, and discusses maximising the growth of national product subject to minimum consumption requirements (Econometric Society record and abstract, Primary Official).

The fifth was the extension of the model into planning practice and cybernetics. The Elsevier contents list for Papers in Economics and Sociology includes “The Political Economy of Socialism”, “The Role of Planning in a Socialist Economy”, “From Accounting to Mathematics”, “Michal Kalecki’s Model of the Business Cycle”, “Closing Speech at the Second Congress of Polish Economists, Warsaw, 9th June 1956” and “How I See the Polish Economic Model” (Elsevier publisher record, Primary Official). The cybernetics book derives from lectures at the University of Warsaw and contains chapters on automatic regulation, the Keynesian multiplier, simple and expanded reproduction and matrix balance equations (publisher preview, Primary Theoretical).

Analysis of capitalism#

Lange’s account of capitalism was built from price theory rather than from a theory of exploitation. In Part Two of the 1936–37 essay he asked why anyone should bother with socialism if competition enforces the same rules as a rationally conducted planned economy, and answered that the analogy is purely formal. The actual distribution of resources differs because private ownership ties demand prices to the existing distribution of income, so that “demand price” reflects the ability to pay rather than the urgency of need (1937 article text, pp. 123–125, Primary Theoretical). He wrote that income distribution under capitalism “introduces a constant error—a class bias in favour of the rich” (same source, p. 125).

He also argued, following Pigou, that private cost and social cost diverge: the cost account of a private entrepreneur excludes the maintenance of workers he dismisses and the victims of industrial accidents, while some services rendered by producers are not included in the price. “Most important alternatives, like life, security, and health of the workers, are sacrificed without being accounted for as a cost of production”, he wrote, and a socialist economy could put all the alternatives into its economic accounting (same source, p. 126, Primary Theoretical). He accepted that Pigou’s legislation, taxation and bounties could remove much of this waste within capitalism, but thought socialism could do it “with much greater thoroughness” (same source).

His second objection concerned instability. He argued that a socialist economy could stop the cumulative shrinkage of demand and output that causes business cycles, because a mistaken investment could be corrected without closing a plant and setting off a general contraction: “Mistakes can be localised, a partial over-production does not need to turn into a general one” (same source, p. 126, Primary Theoretical). His third objection was that actual capitalism is not perfectly competitive: “it is one where oligopoly and monopolistic competition prevail”, and “the wastes of monopolistic competition” strengthened the case for socialism (same source). In the 1938 book’s appendix on socialist theory he took a more ambivalent line on distribution by need, saying that “free sharing” was “by no means such economic nonsense as might appear at a first glance” and that an expanding “socialized sector” of consumption might eventually provide the prime necessaries free (1938 volume, pp. 139–141, Primary Theoretical; quoted in Cottrell and Cockshott, 1993, Scholarly Secondary).

In his late Political Economy Lange gave capitalist enterprise a more explicit credit. Rothbard quotes him as writing that “the rationalization of economic activity within the capitalist enterprise, the practice of proceeding according to the principle of economic rationality, and especially the consciousness of this principle in human thought, all constitute an achievement of historic significance” (Rothbard, 1971, Scholarly Secondary, quoting Lange’s Political Economy, p. 176). This is a quotation of Lange by an adversary; the page reference is Rothbard’s, and the underlying book was not opened here.

Analysis of socialism#

Lange’s preferred institutions are set out in the 1938 essay and are unusually specific. Public ownership covered the means of production outside labour. Consumers retained freedom of choice in consumption, and workers freedom of choice of occupation. Consumers’ incomes had two parts: wages for labour and a “social dividend”, the individual’s share of the income from capital and natural resources owned by society (1938 volume, pp. 77–80, Primary Theoretical). Prices of consumers’ goods and of labour services were determined on a market; all other prices were fixed by the central planning board. Managers were told to use “always the method of production (i.e., combination of factors) which minimizes average cost and to produce as much of each service or commodity as will equalize marginal cost and the price of the product” (same source, p. 77, Primary Theoretical).

His own account of the trial-and-error procedure should be quoted rather than paraphrased. “Let the Central Planning Board start with a given set of prices chosen at random”, he wrote. “All decisions of the managers of production and of the productive resources in public ownership and also all decisions of individuals as consumers and as suppliers of labor are made on the basis of these prices. As a result of these decisions the quantity demanded and supplied of each commodity is determined. If the quantity demanded of a commodity is not equal to the quantity supplied, the price of that commodity has to be changed. It has to be raised if demand exceeds supply and lowered if the reverse is the case. Thus the Central Planning Board fixes a new set of prices which serves as a basis for new decisions, and which results in a new set of quantities demanded and supplied. Through this process of trial and error equilibrium prices are finally determined.” He added that in practice the board would start from historically given prices and make relatively small adjustments (1938 volume, pp. 85–86, Primary Theoretical). “The right prices are simply found out by watching the quantities demanded and the quantities supplied and by raising the price of a commodity or service whenever there is an excess of demand over supply and lowering it whenever the reverse is the case, until, by trial and error, the price is found at which demand and supply are in balance” (same source, p. 88, Primary Theoretical). The technical term here is tâtonnement, the Walrasian idea of finding equilibrium by successive trial prices; Lange used the English phrase “trial and error” rather than the French term in the 1938 text.

He insisted that the prices so found were not arbitrary, because a wrong price would “announce itself in a very objective way — by a physical shortage or surplus of the quantity of the commodity or resources in question” (same source, p. 84, Primary Theoretical). The question of concentrated power was addressed by Lippincott in his introduction, not by Lange in the essay. Lippincott granted that the board “would exercise great power” but argued that it “would not be the sovereign authority of the state”, would be responsible to the legislature, and would co-ordinate regional and local boards rather than plan everything itself (Lippincott introduction, pp. 34–35, Primary Theoretical). Lange’s own essay did not offer a detailed account of political accountability. On transition he was abrupt: socialisation of the industries and banks in question should be the first stage, not the last, because a socialist government trying to control private firms while leaving them in private hands would face constant friction and either abandon its aims or socialise them anyway. The essay ends with the line “Socialism is not an economic policy for the timid” (1937 article text, p. 135, Primary Theoretical; see also the 1938 volume, pp. 121–122). He allowed a sector of small-scale private enterprise provided it was competitive, did not create large income inequalities and was not more expensive in the long run than large-scale production (1938 volume, p. 121, Primary Theoretical).

Later he questioned whether the market mechanism was needed at all once computers existed. Cottrell and Cockshott quote his 1967 essay “The Computer and the Market”: “Were I to rewrite my essay today my task would be much simpler. My answer to Hayek and Robbins would be: so what’s the trouble? Let us put the simultaneous equations on an electronic computer and we shall obtain the solution in less than a second. The market process with its cumbersome tâtonnements appears old-fashioned. Indeed, it may be considered as a computing device of the pre-electronic age” (Cottrell and Cockshott, 1993, Scholarly Secondary, quoting Lange 1967, p. 158; the 1967 essay itself was not opened directly). Cottrell and Cockshott argue that this creates a crucial ambiguity: in 1938 the tâtonnement appeared to happen in real historical time, in 1967 in computer time, and both versions face objections (same source, Scholarly Secondary).

Recommended reading#

These selections are drawn from the dossier’s own recommended reading and are starting points rather than a complete account of Lange’s work.

  • On the Economic Theory of Socialism (1938) — the single volume containing the complete argument, including the rules for managers and the board’s trial-and-error pricing; start here.
  • “The Foundations of Welfare Economics” (1942) — shows Lange rebuilding welfare economics without comparing utilities between people; the clearest example of his method.
  • Theory of Reproduction and Accumulation (1969) — his mature attempt to restate Marx’s reproduction schemes in input–output form; essential for the later Lange, not the 1936 debater.
  • Economic Development, Planning and International Cooperation (1963) — three lectures in which he explains how he thought planning should serve development, in his own short and direct prose.
  • “The Computer and the Market” (1967) — his own revision of the 1936 model in the light of electronic computers; read with the warning that the full essay was not opened here.

Selected lectures and interviews#

No verified audio or video recording of Lange was located, so this section cannot list a filmed lecture or broadcast interview. Several lectures exist as texts, and these can be verified. The most accessible is the set of three lectures delivered at the Central Bank of Egypt in 1961 and published by Monthly Review Press in 1963 as pamphlet no. 20 under the title Economic Development, Planning and International Cooperation; the pamphlet’s own front matter states the place, year and that publication was by permission of the author and the Bank (pamphlet text, Primary Theoretical). The University of Chicago finding aid records that he returned to Chicago in 1962 to give a talk titled “Planning Under Socialism”; no transcript of that talk was located (finding aid, Primary Official). Both Theory of Reproduction and Accumulation and Introduction to Economic Cybernetics state that they were prepared on the basis of lectures delivered at Warsaw University; the books are the published record of those lectures, not recordings (reproduction book; publisher preview, Primary Theoretical).

Intellectual influences#

Mises supplied the challenge. Lange’s opening paragraph credits him with forcing socialists to take economic accounting seriously, and Hayek is quoted in the same paragraphs as having said that Mises first formulated the central problem of socialist economics in a form that could not disappear from discussion (1938 volume, pp. 57–58, Primary Theoretical). Hayek and Robbins supplied the second line of defence — that even if prices in a generalized sense are possible, a central board could not discover them — which Lange explicitly set out to answer (same source, pp. 56–57, Primary Theoretical). Lange cited Hayek’s “The Present State of the Debate” in Hayek’s Collectivist Economic Planning and Robbins’s The Great Depression by page and quoted their claim that the board would need “complete lists” of quantities at every possible combination of prices (same source, pp. 87–88, Primary Theoretical).

Barone and Pareto supplied the formal demonstration that a collectivist economy could satisfy the same equilibrium conditions as a competitive one, and Fred M. Taylor supplied the practical device of successive trials and physical surpluses or shortages; Lange said so in his own text (same source, pp. 56–57, Primary Theoretical). Wicksteed supplied the two meanings of price, Marshall the method of small marginal variations, and Pigou the treatment of divergences between private and social cost (same source, pp. 59–60, 88, 98; 1937 article, pp. 125–126, Primary Theoretical). A footnote acknowledges A. P. Lerner’s “A Note on Socialist Economics”, Review of Economic Studies, October 1936, on the risk of obsolescence (1938 volume, p. 84, Primary Theoretical). Marx supplied the reproduction schemes of the later work, and the 1969 book cites Kalecki on the dynamics of investment and national income in a socialist economy (Theory of Reproduction and Accumulation text and bibliography, Primary Theoretical). In his last book Lange engaged Tadeusz Kotarbinski’s Polish praxeology and Mises’s version of it, as reported by Rothbard (Rothbard, 1971, Scholarly Secondary).

Influence and legacy#

The 1936–37 essays became the standard reply to Mises. Cottrell and Cockshott record what they call the “Standard Version” of the debate: Mises denied calculation was possible, Hayek and Robbins retreated to the computational objection, and “this weakened Austrian position was then successfully assailed by Lange in 1938, when he showed that the socialists could emulate the Walrasian auctioneer, using a ‘trial and error’ process to arrive at socialist general equilibrium” (Cottrell and Cockshott, 1993, Scholarly Secondary). They note that even critics of planning admitted the ingenuity of the solution and its tactical force (same source). Paul Craig Roberts, writing from the opposite position, described the same effect as an error that became orthodoxy for three decades and called the 1971 revival of interest a consequence of the Soviet bloc’s move towards market socialism (Roberts, 1971, Scholarly Secondary). Both accounts agree that the model shaped the terms of the debate; they disagree on whether that was a contribution or a confusion.

Later market-socialist writers built on and revised him. Jael’s survey of the debate notes that H. D. Dickinson, Abba Lerner and E. F. M. Durbin offered variations close to Lange’s in the 1930s, and that a later generation, including Pranab Bardhan and John Roemer, proposed models with “real competition” and addressed the “soft budget constraint”, the tendency of state firms to expect rescue when they run losses (Jael, 2015, Scholarly Secondary). Jael’s abstract judges that the Walrasian competition behind Lange’s model is purely passive, so that the market socialism derived from it “is not really a MARKET socialism”, lacking both innovative competition and markets for capital goods (same source). James Yunker’s “Post-Lange Market Socialism: An Evaluation of Profit-Oriented Proposals”, Journal of Economic Issues 29, 1995, pp. 683–717, is cited in that survey’s reference list but was not opened here. Lange’s “The Computer and the Market” also fed the later technosocialist argument that computers could replace price adjustment, an argument Boettke and others have attacked; the version of that exchange seen here was in a search-index preview and is not used as evidence (Boettke-related paper, preview only).

Lange’s institutional legacy inside Poland is harder to assess from the sources opened. His chairmanship of the Polish Economic Council and State Planning Commission, his seat on the Polish United Workers’ Party central committee and his role at the United Nations are recorded in the obituary and the 1963 pamphlet’s title page (obituary, preview only; pamphlet). The contents of Papers in Economics and Sociology show him writing in 1956 on “How I See the Polish Economic Model” and speaking at the Second Congress of Polish Economists on 9 June 1956 (Elsevier record, Primary Official). Whether those proposals were adopted, and with what effect, has not been established from opened sources.

Criticisms and controversies#

Mises attacked the model at its foundation. In “Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth” he wrote that “Where there is no free market, there is no pricing mechanism; without a pricing mechanism, there is no economic calculation”, and considered the suggestion that state trusts could trade with one another: exchange ratios between producer goods “can be formed only on the basis of private ownership of the means of production”, so that a coal community trading with an iron community would amount “not to socialization but workers’ capitalism and syndicalism” (Mises text, Primary Theoretical, in the English translation from Hayek’s 1935 collection). Lange’s answer was to separate the exchange ratio from the broader idea of price as terms on which alternatives are offered, and to rely on physical shortages and surpluses to reveal error. Cottrell and Cockshott judge that Mises’s specific objections to calculation in labour time are less compelling than he thought, but accept that “Mises has a valid objection” to the computer-time version of Lange’s system if the relevant information must be assembled in advance (Cottrell and Cockshott, 1993, Scholarly Secondary).

Hayek’s criticism developed in a different direction. “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945) argues that the data of an economic problem “never exists in concentrated or integrated form, but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess”, and that central planning based on statistics “cannot take direct account of these circumstances of time and place” (Hayek, 1945, Primary Theoretical). He described the price system as “a kind of machinery for registering change, or a system of telecommunications” (same source). The essay does not name Lange, and this profile does not claim it was written as a direct reply; it is the clearest primary statement of the objection that Lange’s board would lack local knowledge. Cottrell and Cockshott report a further Austrian objection from Hayek’s 1949 writing: that centrally dictated prices respond less flexibly than market prices to constant change, and they report Don Lavoie’s 1985 argument that Lange answered a question Mises regarded as trivial while missing the dynamic discovery problem (Cottrell and Cockshott, 1993, Scholarly Secondary). They dispute Lavoie’s claim that Mises refuted Lange in advance (same source).

Paul Craig Roberts’s 1971 Journal of Political Economy article is the most sustained scholarly attack on the model. Its abstract states that belief in an alternative to the market is based on error and that “the outcome of the socialist controversy is the vindication of market relationships” (Roberts, 1971, Scholarly Secondary). In the text he identifies a “contradictory dualism” between Lange’s central planning vocabulary and his market organizational structure, says the model “takes the problem of socialist planning out of its historical context and obscures it”, and argues that its real subject is whether a market economy can be successfully simulated (same source). In a later memoir, seen only as a search-index preview, he sharpened this into the phrase “market simulation disguised in socialist vocabulary that creates the illusion of planning” (Roberts, 2002, Scholarly Secondary, preview only). Roberts’s argument presupposes a Marxist definition of planning as the elimination of market exchange; Lange explicitly rejected that definition, so the disagreement is partly about what socialism means.

Austrian criticism also targeted Lange’s late work. Rothbard argued that Lange’s posthumous Political Economy adopted praxeology, “the formal, general logic of human action”, while trying to keep Marxism alongside it, and that he accepted “the essential deductive Misesian methodology for economic theory” (Rothbard, 1971, Scholarly Secondary). Rothbard welcomed this as evidence that “Lange’s irony might well have boomeranged” (same source). This is an adversarial reading; the underlying chapters of Political Economy were not opened directly.

The political controversy concerns Lange’s relationship to Stalinism. A biographical reference and a Chicago newspaper were quoted by the finding aid calling him “one of the big men at the second level of the Polish Communist hierarchy” and noting the 1944 Stalin interview (finding aid, Primary Official, quoting a contemporary report). The 1953 article praising Stalin’s last work as an economic theorist is recorded in the International Economic Papers volume contents and in scholarly bibliographies (volume listing; Patinkin reference list, preview only, Scholarly Secondary). A history-of-economic-thought site seen in search results, not used as evidence, says he was “particularly taken to task by fellow economists” for such papers. Milton Friedman’s 1946 American Economic Review note, “Lange on Price Flexibility and Employment: A Methodological Criticism”, pp. 613–631, is listed in bibliographies seen in search results; the article itself was not opened, so no specific objection from it is reported here.

Related thinkers#

  • Ludwig von Mises — whose 1920 calculation essay set the problem Lange claimed to solve, and whose later methodological work Lange engaged at the end of his life (Mises text; Rothbard, 1971).
  • Friedrich Hayek — source of the computational and knowledge objections; Lange quoted his 1935 essay directly, and Hayek’s 1945 article states the dispersed-knowledge case (1938 volume; Hayek, 1945).
  • Lionel Robbins — named with Hayek as the second line of defence in Lange’s essay (same source).
  • Fred M. Taylor — the American economist whose 1929 presidential address supplied the trial-and-error device reprinted alongside Lange’s essay (Project MUSE record).
  • Enrico Barone and Vilfredo Pareto — the Italian general-equilibrium precursors of the claim that socialist accounting is formally possible (1938 volume).
  • Abba Lerner — co-author with Lange of “Strengthening the Economic Foundations of Democracy” (1944) and an early contributor to socialist pricing theory (Jael, 2015).
  • H. D. Dickinson and E. F. M. Durbin — parallel market-socialist proposals of the 1930s (Jael, 2015).
  • Pranab Bardhan and John E. Roemer — later market-socialist theorists whose models Jael contrasts with Lange’s passive competition (same source).
  • János Kornai — whose “soft budget constraint” concept Jael uses in assessing later market socialism (same source).
  • Michał Kalecki and Rosa Luxemburg — the Polish and Marxian reproduction-scheme tradition drawn on in Lange’s later work; a scholarly chapter seen in search results groups all three around Marx’s schemes of expanded reproduction, but that chapter was not opened (reproduction book; Luxemburg/Lange/Kalecki chapter, preview only).
  • Paul A. Sweezy — Lange reviewed The Theory of Capitalist Development in the Journal of Philosophy in 1943, according to a bibliography seen in search results; the review itself was not opened.
  • Paul Craig Roberts, Murray Rothbard, Don Lavoie and Milton Friedman — critics from Austrian, conservative and methodological positions (Roberts, 1971; Rothbard, 1971; Cottrell and Cockshott, 1993).
