Slavery, Race and Capital: From the Atlantic Trade to the Wage of Whiteness

What the book's sources establish about enslaved labour, racial hierarchy and the different forms of coercion that capitalism inherited and transformed.

In his testimony to the House Committee on Un-American Activities on 12 June 1956, Paul Robeson answered a question about Stalin by turning it back on the committee: “You are responsible, and your forebears, for sixty million to one hundred million black people dying in the slave ships and on the plantations.” The number was his own rhetorical summation, not a count. The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, known here through the National Endowment for the Humanities’ project description, estimates that about 12.5 million captives were embarked in Africa and about 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage. The figures measure different things (Robeson, HUAC testimony, 1956; NEH project description). Both statements belong in an account of how slavery figured in political argument. Chattel slavery means a system in which human beings are owned as property; wage labour means work sold by a formally free person who does not own the means of production; colonial forced labour means work compelled by an occupying administration through taxation, contract or punishment.

Trade, plantations and the wealth they made#

Rodney’s scholarly formation was in this history. His research on the Upper Guinea Coast used Portuguese merchant records in England and Portugal and appeared in 1970 (MIA Encyclopedia, “Rodney, Walter”). How Europe Underdeveloped Africa of 1972 set the slave trade inside a longer account of how Africa’s wealth contributed to European capitalist development and how European power reshaped Africa in return (Rodney, preface and contents). Drayton found the book’s roots in the Caribbean and in Eric Williams’s Capitalism and Slavery, a line of argument tying plantation and mining wealth abroad to poverty in the producing countries (Drayton, 2022). Araujo tested one of Rodney’s supporting claims, that enslaved people were bought with goods of little value, against later research (Araujo, 2022). These are disputes over evidence.

Marx placed the traffic inside the origin of capital rather than treating it as a prelude. He wrote that in actual history “conquest, enslavement, robbery, murder, briefly force, play the great part” (Capital, volume I, chapter 26), and described primitive accumulation as applying where it was not “immediate transformation of slaves and serfs into wage labourers, and therefore a mere change of form” (Capital, volume I, chapter 32). The Communist Manifesto opened with “freeman and slave, patrician and plebeian, lord and serf” as opposed figures in earlier class struggles (Manifesto, chapter I). The claim that capitalism inherited older forms of domination and recast them is therefore in the classical texts. What the sources here do not provide is a documented chain from a slave ship to a particular factory.

Du Bois and the colour line as a labour question#

Du Bois made the connection between slavery, labour and capitalism the centre of his work without always using Marxist language. The Souls of Black Folk of 1903 answered the question “What have we done for this country?” by reversing the burden of proof. The nation, he wrote, received from Black Americans “a gift of story and song,” “the gift of sweat and brawn,” and “the gift of the Spirit,” and he asked “Your country? How came it yours? Before the Pilgrims landed we were here” (The Souls of Black Folk). The same book named double consciousness and declared that “the problem of the Twentieth Century is the problem of the color line.” In 1915 he argued that “it was the competition for the labor of yellow, brown, and black folk” that caused the war, and compared the German invasion of Belgium with the Belgian record in the Congo (MIA-hosted review quoting the essay). Those words reach us through a review that selects and frames them.

The argument sharpened in Black Reconstruction in America of 1935. Its chapter “The General Strike” held that enslaved people, by withdrawing their labour and fleeing to Union lines, made emancipation their own act rather than a gift (Black Reconstruction, archive record). A Sojourner Truth Organization study guide quotes Du Bois on how the experiment ended: “When white laborers were convinced that the degradation of Negro labor was more fundamental than the Uplift of white labor, the end was in sight” (study guide). This is the theory of the psychological wage in compressed form: white workers receive a material and psychological bribe from the subordinate status of Black workers. In Darkwater of 1920 Du Bois treated whiteness itself as a historical making: “The discovery of personal whiteness among the world’s peoples is a very modern thing” (Darkwater). The African American Intellectual History Society notes both the later influence of Black Reconstruction and the criticism that Du Bois relied too heavily on a Marxist expectation of how history must turn out (AAIHS essay).

Racial hierarchy after emancipation#

Harry Haywood wrote in 1933 that the party’s line on the Black Belt rested on the concentration of Black people there, “the survival of plantation relations and debt bondage there,” and a system of segregation enforced by violence, “the most vicious being the peculiar American institution of lynching” (Haywood, 1933). In 1946 he called the Negro question basically agrarian, involving sharecropping, land hunger and the plantation system left by chattel slavery (Haywood, 1946). He later rejected both the liberal view that prejudice was ignorance to be cured by education and the social democratic view that the question was purely one of class (Haywood, 1958/59). These are party positions, not a record of how sharecroppers saw the afterlife of slavery.

Assata Shakur made the connection in another register. Writing to Pope John Paul II in 1998 she said: “Our fight had started on a slave ship years before we were born.” Her 2014 letter greeted Cuba as a great “Palenque,” a maroon camp, the word for communities founded by people who fled enslavement (Shakur, 1998 letter; 2014 letter). Philip V. McHarris reads her flight to Cuba as a form of marronage, a scholar’s interpretation of a political self-description (McHarris, Columbia Journal of Race and Law, 2026). Slavery persisted as a vocabulary for racial domination after legal ownership ended.

Three forms of coercion#

The distinctions matter. Wage labour rests on a formal sale of labour-power: Engels defined the proletariat as those “reduced to selling their labour power in order to live” (Manifesto, chapter I, notes). Chattel slavery rests on ownership of the person and is not a contract of that kind. Colonial forced labour rests on administrative compulsion. Speaking on Congolese independence day in 1960, Lumumba recalled forced labour paid too little to feed or clothe a family, land seized under laws that recognised the right of might, and towns divided between white mansions and Black huts (Lumumba, 30 June 1960). Ho Chi Minh told the Comintern in 1924 that French rule in Vietnam rested on forced labour and a land tax raised after floods (Ho Chi Minh, 1924). Padmore quoted East African governors on taxation as a device for pushing Africans into wage work, a description of a transition from compulsion to a labour market rather than of slavery persisting unchanged (Padmore, 1931). These arrangements could overlap, but should not be merged.

Limits of the record#

This book has no dedicated chapter on the Atlantic slave trade or on plantation slavery. The NEH database is known through a project description; the underlying records were not interrogated, and the figures of 12.5 million and 10.7 million are cited as the project’s estimates. No slave ship log, plantation account book, sale record or abolitionist petition has been opened. The voices of enslaved people reach us through Du Bois’s reconstruction, Rodney’s account of merchant archives, Haywood’s party documents and Shakur’s political testament. Each is mediated, and none is a sample. Robeson’s sixty to one hundred million was a charge delivered under hostile questioning. The scale and causal weight of the trade are documented by scholarship in the record; the precise mechanisms by which enslaved labour became industrial capital remain argued over.

The research priorities are slaving voyage records, plantation papers, slave testimony in its original languages, comparative work on the wealth accumulated from plantation products, and studies of how racial hierarchy was rebuilt in wage labour after emancipation. Without them this chapter can establish that the sources link slavery, race and capitalism in specific ways and that the links are disputed in detail. It cannot supply a balance sheet of profit or measure how much racial inequality followed from slavery rather than from later institutions.
