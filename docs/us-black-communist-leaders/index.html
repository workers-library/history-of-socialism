US Black Communist Leaders: Haywood, Ford, Hampton

The Black Belt thesis, The Negro and the Communist Party, and Hampton's Rainbow Coalition.

MarxismPart VI: Revolutionary Lives and Movements in the Americas

Between the late 1920s and the end of the 1960s, three American radicals argued that the freedom of Black people could not be separated from the struggle against capitalism. All three were communists. By that they meant that they belonged to organisations which took the writings of Karl Marx and Vladimir Lenin as their guide and which aimed to replace private ownership of the main industries with public control. Harry Haywood, James W. Ford and Fred Hampton worked in different periods and different organisations, and they disagreed about strategy. Yet each returned to the same claim: that racism was rooted in an economic system, that Black liberation required mass organisation rather than appeals to goodwill, and that the state would resist those who said so. Their published words survive in their movements’ presses and in the pages of the Communist Party’s newspapers. Read carefully, and with the limits of those sources in mind, they show how an American revolutionary tradition was made and how it was met.

Harry Haywood and the invention of the Black Belt nation#

Harry Haywood was born in 1898 and grew up in the world of railway work and the sea. The biographical notice attached to his archive says that in the early 1920s he worked as a trainman and maritime seaman before entering the circle around the African Blood Brotherhood, an early organisation of Black radicals. His own account places his political education abroad. He travelled to Moscow in 1926 and studied at KUTVA, the university established for workers from the East, where he joined the internal arguments of the Soviet party. He later recalled that he and the other Black students supported Stalin against Trotsky because they read Stalin’s policies as the continuation of Lenin’s. In his autobiography he wrote that those who used the word Stalinist as an insult were avoiding the real question, which was whether Stalin and the Central Committee had been right. He believed history had shown that they had. (MIA Encyclopedia, “Haywood, Harry”; Haywood, “Trotsky’s Day in Court”)

That formation mattered because the theory for which Haywood became known was built with the concepts of the Communist International, the Moscow-based body that coordinated communist parties in different countries. The Communist Party of the United States, usually shortened to CPUSA, was the American member. Haywood served on its Central Committee and, in the early 1930s, on its Politburo, the small group of senior leaders who took decisions between full committee meetings. He spent more than four years in the Soviet Union and helped draft the resolutions on the “Negro question” adopted by the Communist International in 1928 and 1930. He also fought in the Abraham Lincoln Brigade in Spain during the Spanish Civil War, and in the 1960s he became associated with the Maoist New Communist Movement, a current that looked to Mao’s China rather than to Moscow. A movement obituary described him as a worker intellectual who had transformed himself from a railwayman and seaman into a theorist of Black liberation. (Beal, “‘Black Bolshevik’ Harry Haywood Dies at 87,” 1985)

Haywood’s central proposition was that Black people in the Southern Black Belt formed an oppressed nation, not simply a racial minority. The Black Belt was the band of counties across the cotton-growing South where Black people formed a large share of the population. In his 1948 book Negro Liberation he argued that what was called racial persecution was in fact a form of national oppression, and that within the borders of the United States there existed two nations, one dominant and white, the other subject and Black. He set out the case with the criteria then standard in Marxist writing. A nation, in that scheme, needed a shared territory, a common economic life, a division of labour, and a culture. Haywood claimed all of these for the Black Belt. He described a region of roughly 470 counties containing five million Black people, with its own transport and banking systems, its Black bourgeoisie and professional stratum, and its industrial workers in coal, steel, timber, tobacco and chemicals. He quoted Stalin’s 1913 definition of a nation and concluded that Black Americans were “a nation within a nation.” (Haywood, “The Negro Nation,” Negro Liberation, chapter 7)

He was careful about what followed from this. Self-determination meant the right of a people to decide its own political status, and Haywood insisted repeatedly that this right was not a duty to secede. “Quite definitely, this right includes the right of separation,” he wrote, “but self-determination must not be construed as identical with secession and the establishment of an independent state.” He compared it to the right of divorce: a democrat should defend the right without arguing that every woman must use it. The available outcomes, in his account, were complete secession, federation with the former oppressor, or territorial autonomy with some control over local affairs. This distinction was not a footnote. It was the answer Haywood gave to critics who said that the Communist Party wanted to break up the United States. (Haywood, “The Negro Nation”)

The origins of the line lay in the Sixth Congress of the Communist International in 1928. Haywood’s 1933 article in The Communist stated that the party’s programme had been first formulated there, after what he called the most exhaustive consideration of the conditions of Black people in the United States. He identified three premises: the concentration of Black masses in the Black Belt, the survival of plantation relations and debt bondage there, and a political system of segregation enforced by violence, “the most vicious being the peculiar American institution of lynching.” He called the struggle of land-hungry sharecroppers for the land, together with the fight for democratic rights, the central axis of the movement. The political conclusion was unambiguous: Black workers in unity with the white working class and under Communist leadership were, in his words, the only force capable of winning the struggle against capitalism. (Haywood, “The Struggle for the Leninist Position on the Negro Question,” 1933)

Haywood’s own account of how the line was applied connects the theory to events familiar from the history of the 1930s. He cited the 1931 Yokinen trial, the Scottsboro campaign on behalf of nine Black youths falsely accused of rape in Alabama, the sharecroppers’ resistance at Camp Hill and Tallapoosa, and the electoral campaign of 1932. These were not abstract examples to him. They were the occasions on which he believed the party had shown that it would defend Black defendants and organisers in court and in the streets. A 1930 article attacked what he called the bourgeois liberal formula of the racial question and insisted that the question was a national one, with the Black Belt an internal colony of American imperialism. The article survives in an English PDF and in a Portuguese translation published by the same archive. (Haywood, 1930 article; Portuguese translation)

Haywood later defended the timing of the line against the charge that it was a product of Depression-era pessimism. “Our position was adopted in 1928 at the Sixth World Congress,” he wrote, “that is, the year before the 1929 crash, and at the height of the boom period.” He described the 1930 resolution as an elaboration of a thesis already adopted. In 1946 he applied it to a programme of agrarian reform, writing that the Negro question was basically agrarian in origin and involved a depressed peasantry, sharecropping, chronic land hunger and the plantation system left by chattel slavery. (Haywood, 1946)

Repression and the breaking with the party#

The sources through which Haywood described repression are movement sources, and that shapes what they can show. A Daily Worker report of 9 September 1935 described his appearance at Boulevard Hall in Chicago as his first since what it called his arrest and beating at a “Hands Off Ethiopia” parade. According to the report, police and Red Squad detectives massed at the hall, and Haywood, still weak and able to walk only with crutches, spoke to a crowded room about his defiance of a police ban by addressing a crowd from a roof. This testimony establishes that the party presented him as a victim of police violence and used the episode to mobilise its supporters. It does not independently establish the officers’ motives, and it was written for a party newspaper. (Daily Worker, 9 September 1935)

In his 1976 retrospective Haywood set his own treatment inside a wider account of the postwar repression of the party leadership. He noted that several members of the National Committee were in jail or just released, and he argued that imprisonment and terror had strengthened what he called illusions about the vitality of American imperialism inside the party. Under the pressure of the Smith Act prosecutions, he wrote, the leadership developed what he regarded as the dogma of peaceful transition to socialism. His expulsion from the CPUSA after thirty-six years was related in the third person, as a story told to him by a friend in Mexico City: an acquaintance asked after him and was told that he had been expelled, that he was a good guy, but that there had been differences. The anecdote conveys the personal cost of the break more clearly than a formal statement would. (Haywood, “The Degeneration of the CPUSA in the 1950s,” 1976)

The 1958 pamphlet and the argument over reform#

Haywood’s late pamphlet For a Revolutionary Position on the Negro Question was written in 1958 and issued by the Provisional Organizing Committee, a group formed after his departure from the CPUSA. He later described it as a summation of unpublished articles written against what he called reformism during the 1950s. The pamphlet’s main slogan was “New Look,” which he applied both to American foreign policy and to what he saw as the party leadership’s retreat on self-determination. He quoted Eugene Dennis explaining why the slogan of self-determination should be altered, Doxey Wilkerson arguing that patterns of Negro oppression were in flux, and Ben Davis claiming that full equality could be achieved within the American system. Haywood noted that Davis made this claim in the spring of 1956, months after the unpunished murder of Emmett Till. (Haywood, 1958/59)

Against these optimistic formulations he placed economic evidence drawn from Census and Commerce Department figures. The median wage gap between white and nonwhite workers rose, in the figures he cited, from 592 dollars in 1939 to 1,113 dollars in 1948 and 1,552 dollars in 1955. Black farm operators were displaced at a far higher rate than white operators in the South. His conclusion was that the plantation system was declining but was not likely to die of its own weight. The theoretical distinction he drew was between reforms won inside an existing system and a revolutionary transformation of the South. He argued that the Negro question was at bottom the question of an oppressed nation in the South and a national minority in the North, and that it could only be solved through revolutionary change in the Deep South. Against what he called the bourgeois liberal view, which treated prejudice as ignorance to be cured by education, and against the social democratic view, which treated the question as purely one of class, he asserted that the struggle required land revolution, self-determination in the Black Belt and equal rights in the North. (Haywood, 1958/59)

National liberation and the later years#

In the 1960s Haywood argued that the Black movement inside the United States should be understood as a national liberation movement, in the same sense used for anti-colonial struggles abroad. Writing with Gwendolyn Midlo Hall in 1964, he described the movement as the counterpart of the anti-colonial revolution shaking the world, a revolt against what he called semi-colonial oppression in the heart of the leading imperialist country. The essay named three forces feeding it: the worldwide anti-colonial upsurge, economic crisis among Black masses, and the erosion of belief in peaceful integration. Haywood had already set out what he considered the features of a consistent liberation movement: it had to rest on workers and peasants, build organisations of the most committed militants, and develop political and economic struggles together. His strategic demand was a mass Communist Party, and his line of march was full freedom in the Deep South, which he called the source of oppression throughout the country. (Haywood and Hall, 1964)

The pamphlet ended with a statement of identity. “We, Negro Communists, do not accept the status of aliens to which the Negro Resolution relegates us,” Haywood wrote. “We are an integral part of the Negro movement.” (Haywood, 1958/59). He called for an alliance with the white working class and for international solidarity with national liberation struggles. In 1976 he recorded that at the 1957 convention he had argued that the tremendous proletarianisation of Negroes in the Deep South had sharpened rather than softened the national character of the question, and he concluded that the right of self-determination had been formally dropped (Haywood, 1976). In 1984, writing in The Guardian, he revisited the strategic question and named American monopoly capital as the main enemy of American communists (Haywood, 1984).

Historians have not accepted Haywood’s own estimate of his thesis. Beverly Tomek, writing in the Journal of Labor and Society in 2012, argues that the Black Belt thesis failed to gain support beyond a small group of party intellectuals, and that CPUSA leaders eventually turned to more practical ways of supporting Black Americans against racism. She also notes that the thesis helped foster the growth of African American history as a field. Cedric Johnson, in a 2016 study of the exchange between Haywood and Harold Cruse in the 1960s, treats that debate as a serious argument about class politics and nationalism rather than as a settled question. These judgements do not settle the theoretical merits of Haywood’s case. They do show that his account of the thesis as the party’s necessary line is one interpretation among others. (Tomek, 2012; Johnson, 2016)

James W. Ford and the party of the Negro people#

James W. Ford was born in Pratt City, Alabama, on 22 December 1893. His father was a coal miner and his mother a domestic worker. The 1932 campaign biography, written by the party for the election in which Ford ran for vice-president, tells the story in the language of Communist campaign literature. It says that his grandfather, a track walker, was attacked one night, beaten and burned alive on a pile of cross ties, and that Ford went to work on a railroad at thirteen. He later worked as a blacksmith’s helper, a machinist’s helper and a labourer at a blast furnace, attended Fisk University and enlisted in the army in 1917. The account is a campaign document, and its details were selected to establish his credentials as a worker and a victim of racial violence. (Ford 1932 campaign biography)

The same biography describes the war as the turning point. Ford had enlisted, it says, on the false promise that if America won the war the American Negro would be free and equal. In the army he saw segregation and mistreatment, and helped organise protest meetings among Black soldiers. After the war, though he was an expert radio operator, he could not find work because he was Black, and took a job in a mattress factory. In the Chicago post office he fought the speed-up system and the bureaucracy of the union officials, and was elected a delegate to the Chicago Federation of Labor, where he opposed the exclusion of Black workers from unions. He was eventually dismissed, the biography says, because the authorities feared the growing unity of white and Black workers. The narrative then reaches its intended destination: his activity brought him into the ranks of the only party which championed the cause of the oppressed, the Communist Party, whose banner demanded equal rights for Negroes and self-determination for the Black Belt. (Ford 1932 campaign biography)

Ford’s career was international as well as domestic. In 1927 he was elected a delegate of the Trade Union Educational League to the fourth world congress of the Red International of Labor Unions, a Moscow-based body coordinating revolutionary trade unions, usually known by its initials RILU. He was elected to its executive committee. In 1928 he attended the sixth congress of the Communist International and served on its Negro Commission, the body set up to formulate policy on Black oppression. In 1929 he represented the International Committee of Negro Workers at a congress of the League Against Imperialism, and was arrested in New York for leading a protest against the shooting of natives in Haiti by American Marines. In 1930 he went to Germany as secretary of the International Committee of Negro Workers. In 1931 he spoke at a Geneva conference on African children, and the introduction to his 1938 book claims that his speech exposed colonial policy to the embarrassment of delegates. (Ford 1938, The Negro and the Democratic Front)

The institutional setting can be checked independently of Ford’s admirers. MIA’s introduction to The Negro Worker, a journal funded by the Communist International, says that it was first called The International Negro Workers’ Review and was renamed in March 1931. Its sponsoring body was the International Trade Union Committee of Negro Workers, which belonged to RILU and through it to the Communist International. Ford was its first head, and was soon replaced by the Trinidadian George Padmore. The journal’s contents list Ford’s articles on forced labour and on the defence of Ethiopia. (MIA introduction to The Negro Worker)

Ford’s 1930 pamphlet Negro’s Struggle Against Imperialism was a report to the second world congress of the League Against Imperialism, delivered at Frankfurt in July 1929. It opened with a class analysis of what it called the Negro problem and declared that the forces had to be mobilised for joint struggle with the world proletariat in the international struggle against imperialism. This was the frame he carried into his electoral work. (Ford, Negro’s Struggle Against Imperialism, 1930)

The claim that the Communist Party alone fought for equality#

Ford ran for vice-president three times, in 1932, 1936 and 1940, and the claim for which he is best known appears most plainly in his campaign statements. On 7 November 1932 the Daily Worker published a statement from Detroit under a headline attacking Hoover, Roosevelt and Thomas. Ford said that a vote for any of the three was like saying that voters liked starvation, lynching, breadlines and the Scottsboro frame-up. The Communist Party, he said, was uniting Black and white workers against the ruling class, and was fighting every day against lynchers and bosses for unemployment insurance and for political, economic and social equality for Negroes. A vote for the party was a vote for a workers’ and farmers’ government. (Ford, Daily Worker, 7 November 1932)

His acceptance speech at Madison Square Garden in June 1936 put the claim more starkly. He said the nomination was not a personal tribute but a tribute to the entire Negro people, and that the other parties lavished sweet words while only the Communist Party fought day in and day out for every demand of Negroes, in the terror-ridden South and the poverty-stricken slums of Harlem and Chicago’s South Side. He dismissed the Republicans for their ties to Chicago’s political machine and the Democrats for tolerating lynchings and the imprisonment of the Scottsboro boys, and ended by calling the Communist Party the party of the Negro people. (Ford 1938, appendix)

These statements are evidence of what Ford argued, not of the comparative record of the parties. They were campaign speeches printed by his own party, and their purpose was to win votes. The 1938 book repeated the claim in another form, quoting another party leader’s judgment that the Communists were a vital part of the united front because theirs was the only party that could win the support and vote of the Negro people. The same book opened with the sentence that America’s most bitter heritage was its Negro problem, and invoked the Marxist formulation that labour with a white skin could not emancipate itself where labour with a black skin was branded. (Ford, The Negro and the Democratic Front)

Women organisers and the wartime years#

Ford’s 1937 report to a CPUSA plenary meeting, Developing Negro Communist Leaders, is the most concrete of his documents about ordinary organisers. He began by saying that the recruitment of Black women into the party lagged behind the advances of the period, and that Negro women were, in his phrase, clamouring to get into the party and beating at its doors. He argued that women were the foremost leaders of the Negro people, and proposed that the party organise women domestic workers, a sector he identified from statistics as one of the largest employers of Black women. He quoted a women’s commission resolution calling for special training classes and for more Black women to be brought into leadership of the party, the trade unions, the Congress of Industrial Organizations and the unemployed and peace movements. He named Frances Duty, a Harlem Workers Alliance leader, and a nurse who had built a party unit of forty and a mass movement of more than four hundred people around Harlem Hospital. (Ford, “Developing Negro Communist Leaders,” 1937)

The report matters because it records the work of people whose names would not otherwise survive, and because it shows a leader arguing that the movement’s reach depended on women organisers rather than on a handful of male spokesmen. Its limits are those of its genre: it was a report to a party plenum, designed to press a particular organising line, and the examples were chosen to support that line. During the war Ford wrote a foreword to a pamphlet reprinting Frederick Douglass’s address, arguing that the crisis required national unity across classes and peoples and that discrimination against Black Americans in the armed forces and in industry was a blot that could never be satisfied until it was erased. In 1943 he wrote that discrimination was a political problem eating at the vitals of war mobilisation. (Ford, foreword, 1942)

Fred Hampton, class politics and the Illinois Panthers#

Fred Hampton was born in 1948 and was killed in Chicago on 4 December 1969, aged twenty-one. He chaired the Illinois chapter of the Black Panther Party and was deputy chairman of the national organisation. The Black Panther Party was a Marxist-Leninist organisation that had begun in Oakland in 1966 and combined armed self-defence with programmes that provided food and medical care to poor communities. Hampton’s chapter ran several breakfast programmes for children on Chicago’s West Side, helped create a free medical centre and organised door-to-door health services that tested for sickle cell anaemia and encouraged blood drives for Cook County Hospital. It also tried to draw local street gangs away from what the party called turf wars and into what it called the class war. (MIA Encyclopedia, “Hampton, Fred”; MIA, “History of the Black Panther Party”)

Hampton’s own account of founding the chapter was delivered at Northern Illinois University in November 1969. He described a meeting the previous year at 2350 West Madison, where he and other organisers decided to start a Black Panther Party in Chicago because the Party had to exist wherever people were. The speech repeatedly contrasts the valley, where he said commitment and oppression lived, with the mountain top, where students and comfortable people imagined themselves to be. The image was not decoration. It was his way of saying that political work had to return to the places where poor people lived. (Hampton, “It’s a Class Struggle, Godamnit!”)

His theory of political education was stated in almost the same words in several speeches. People learned, he said, through observation and participation. He told the story of Huey Newton and Bobby Seale putting up stop signs at a dangerous intersection: residents watched, then later took their own signs and guns and did the same at another crossing. He applied the method to his own attempt to make a citizens’ arrest of a police officer on 63rd Street, arguing that people who watched the confrontation would be more likely to act the next time. In April 1969 he tied the method directly to the breakfast programme, saying that theory without practice was worthless and that the two belonged together. The point was that people came to understand socialism by running a programme that fed children, not by reading about it. (Hampton, “Power Anywhere Where There’s People!”; Hampton, 27 April 1969)

The party’s newspaper supplied the programme’s rationale in its own voice. “To Feed Our Children,” published on 26 March 1969, said the free breakfast programme was being extended to every chapter because children needed a nourishing breakfast in order to learn, and added that hunger would no longer keep them down. Hampton himself described the scale of the work in Chicago in practical terms, naming a free health clinic at 16th and Springfield and contrasting it with the affluent suburbs. In one passage he imagined a conversation in which a man defended the breakfast programme to a police officer who called it socialist, answering that he did not care if it was communism, because the programme fed children. The anecdote is Hampton’s rendering, preserved in a speech transcript, and it reveals how he wanted supporters to understand the politics of a meal. (BPP, “To Feed Our Children”; Hampton, “It’s a Class Struggle, Godamnit!”)

The Rainbow Coalition#

Hampton argued that the division of poor people by race served the interests of the wealthy. The masses were poor, he said in “Power Anywhere Where There’s People!”, and they included white, Black, brown and yellow people. His formula was that racism should be met with solidarity rather than with racism, and capitalism with socialism rather than with Black capitalism. He returned to it in April, May and November 1969, on one occasion describing capitalism as the source of racism and saying that slaves had been brought to America to make money. The consistency across the speeches suggests this was the core of his politics rather than a passing phrase. (Hampton, “Power Anywhere Where There’s People!”)

The organisational form was the Rainbow Coalition, so called after the colours of the groups it joined together. No surviving speech in the Hampton archive bears that title, so the coalition’s theory has to be assembled from his own words and from the MIA encyclopedia entry. According to that entry, it united the Young Lords, a Puerto Rican group; the Young Patriots, a white Appalachian migrant group; the Black Panthers; and others. Hampton also organised a non-aggression pact between Chicago street gangs, on the argument that turf wars among the poor served the rich. The Encyclopedia describes this as groundbreaking, and the Party’s own history page says the Panthers followed Malcolm X’s belief in working-class unity across colour and gender and formed coalitions with other revolutionary groups. (MIA Encyclopedia, “Hampton, Fred”; MIA BPP history)

His programmatic conclusion followed the coalition logic. He said that people would run the People’s Party whether anyone liked it or not, that they had shown they could run things in China and would do so in the United States, and that calling the idea communism would not frighten anyone. The method he had learned from Leninist theory he called the mass line, meaning the practice of learning from ordinary people and then returning their ideas to them in organised form. He described dialectics, the Marxist claim that change comes from clashes within a system, in plain terms in one speech: if a political contradiction was stretched far enough it became war, which he described as politics with bloodshed. (Hampton, “Power Anywhere Where There’s People!”)

The speeches#

Hampton spoke often, and the transcripts preserve a voice that is direct, colloquial and frequently profane. In “You Can Murder a Liberator, but You Can’t Murder Liberation,” delivered on 27 April 1969, he described what he saw as the repression of Panthers around the country and demanded the release of Huey Newton, the party’s Minister of Defense, who was in prison. He rejected the idea that a leadership should move faster than its followers could follow. He also criticised his own party, saying that its ten-point programme was being changed because it had used the word white where it should have used the word capitalist, and that the Panthers were not seeking a dictatorship of the Panthers or of Black people but a dictatorship of the people. The closing passage gave the speech its title: a revolutionary could be jailed but the revolution could not, and a liberator could be murdered but liberation could not. (Hampton, 27 April 1969)

In “We Have to Protect Our Leaders!” on 19 May 1969, delivered on Malcolm X’s birthday, he asked why so many others were no longer present, quoted Malcolm on using whatever was necessary to correct unjust conditions, and repeated the stop-sign story. The speech ended with a threat of comic violence against anyone who entered the community to set up something that did not meet the needs of the masses, saying he would beat such a person with a Black Panther newspaper, which had an ideology of its own. In “It’s a Class Struggle, Godamnit!” at Northern Illinois University in November, he called the Chicago conspiracy trial a hecatomb, meaning a public sacrifice, defined political power as the ability to control and define events, named what he called the three-in-one of greedy businessmen, lying politicians and racist police, and dismissed armchair radicals who had never fed anyone or asked anyone about the health clinic. (Hampton, 19 May 1969; Hampton, November 1969)

On Martin Luther King, Hampton said in a church that King had spoken of the moral universe bending toward heaven. This is not the better-known formulation, in which the arc of the moral universe bends toward justice. The speech transcript preserves Hampton’s own rendering, and it should be read as that rather than as a reliable quotation of King. Hampton also told his audience that if they dared to struggle they dared to win, and that if they did not they did not deserve to win. An undated recording, titled “Untitled (Why don’t you die for the people?)” in the archive, develops the same theme. He said that anyone who thought about him without doing revolutionary work had better forget him, that a twenty-year-old who refused a commitment because he was too young to die was already dead, and that he expected to die doing the things he was born for. (Hampton, “Power Anywhere Where There’s People!”; Hampton, “Untitled”)

The raid of 4 December 1969 and how it has been explained#

The Party’s published account, preserved by MIA, says that at four in the morning on 4 December 1969, Chicago police raided the Panthers’ apartment on the basis of information from an FBI informant. Hampton was shot twice in the head and once in the arm and shoulder while he slept, and Mark Clark, aged seventeen, was killed in the living room. Hampton’s fiancée, Deborah Johnson, eight months pregnant, was shot but survived, and four others in the apartment were wounded. The account says that a federal grand jury found that ninety bullets were fired inside the apartment, of which one came from a Panther, and that all the surviving Panthers were arrested while no police officer spent a moment in jail. It frames the raid as part of a campaign in which J. Edgar Hoover called the Panthers the greatest threat to internal security and the FBI used assassinations, espionage and psychological warfare against the party. (MIA, “History of the Black Panther Party”)

This is a movement account and its language reflects that. The documentary record assembled since then confirms part of its outline and complicates other parts. Katelyn Stieva, writing in the Journal of Military and Strategic Studies in 2018, notes that no declassified document shows the FBI directly authorising a political assassination, but that the bureau’s informant system and its practice of passing information to local police made the raid possible. She identifies William O’Neal, Hampton’s bodyguard and the Illinois chapter’s head of security, as the informant who supplied a map of the apartment. A report from the Chicago FBI office dated 3 December 1969, the day before the raid, recorded that information about weapons had been passed to the police, who were planning what it called a positive course of action. Jeffrey Haas, in a 2010 study, argues that the FBI and the Chicago police together murdered Hampton. A Washington Post account in 2021 noted that a grand jury report after the raid concluded that virtually all the gunfire came from the police. Historians therefore agree on the informant and on the lopsided pattern of shooting, while disagreeing about how far up the chain of command the plan to kill Hampton reached. (Stieva, 2018; Haas, 2010; Washington Post, 2021)

The legal aftermath was likewise mixed. A civil suit seeking 47.7 million dollars was brought by survivors and relatives and ended in a deadlocked jury. In 1982 the City of Chicago, Cook County and the federal government agreed to pay 1.85 million dollars to nine plaintiffs. State’s Attorney Edward Hanrahan and other officials were indicted on obstruction charges but were acquitted. The MIA encyclopedia entry records the birth of Hampton’s son four weeks after his death and the civil settlement, and it also discusses the FBI’s counter-intelligence campaign, the informant O’Neal and the disputed claim that Hampton had been given a sedative before the raid. That claim was contested at the time, and the surviving accounts do not resolve it. What can be said with confidence is that the raid ended the life of a twenty-one-year-old organiser whose programmes were feeding children and whose coalition was drawing poor people of different races into the same organisation. (MIA Encyclopedia, “Hampton, Fred”)

Four weeks after Hampton’s death, Deborah Johnson gave birth to their son, Fred Hampton Jr. The movement continued to commemorate the date. MIA preserves a 1975 notice from The Call describing National Days of Resistance to Repression, initiated by the October League to mark the fifth anniversary of the killing. The surviving speeches, however, are the clearest record of what Hampton wanted. In the end his argument was not that a hero had been killed but that the work had to continue without him: the line he repeated was that a liberator could be murdered but liberation could not. (The Call, January 1975)

What the three lives show#

Haywood built a theory of the Black Belt nation and spent three decades defending the right of self-determination against liberal gradualism and against what he saw as the simplification of the slogan into a duty to secede. Ford carried a related politics into elections, trade unions and the international institutions of communism, returning always to the claim that his party fought daily for Black equality. Hampton rejected racial nationalism in favour of class solidarity, taught through observation and participation, and built a coalition across racial lines around programmes that fed and treated poor people.

The differences are as instructive as the similarities. Haywood’s frame was national, rooted in a region and in the Marxist criteria of nationhood. Ford’s was electoral and institutional, rooted in the Communist Party’s claim to represent the Negro people. Hampton’s was local and practical, rooted in the breakfast table and the health clinic. Each met repression and each described it in the publications of his own movement, which is to say that each account has to be read with its purpose in mind. The academy has since offered its own judgements: Tomek on the limits of the Black Belt thesis, Stieva and Haas on the FBI’s role in the Chicago raid. None of this work makes the movements’ own words less worth reading. It makes them evidence to be weighed rather than conclusions to be repeated.
