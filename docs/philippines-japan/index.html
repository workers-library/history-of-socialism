Two Archipelagos: Communist Movements in the Philippines and Japan, 1942–1972

On the rice plain of central Luzon during the Japanese occupation, a song passed among the Hukbalahap, the peasant movement that took up arms in 1942.

On the rice plain of central Luzon during the Japanese occupation, a song passed among the Hukbalahap, the peasant movement that took up arms in 1942. Its English version begins:

“We are what they call mere peasants; / Who were created by God in sincere love; / We who live by our own toil,”

— Huk peasant song “We Are Peasants”, English version reproduced by Benedict Kerkvliet, translator not named, reproduced by Rodney Azama, 1985 (Source: globalsecurity.org).

It speaks of hunger, usury and a country that would go hungry if the peasants stopped, giving the land question a voice that party documents do not. An American Army counterinsurgency study reprinted it in 1985. Founded on 29 March 1942, the movement built a local administration while fighting the Japanese; Luis Taruc, based at Mount Arayat from December 1941, became its best-known commander. After the war its programme of rent reduction and land met a restored landlord state.

What the rebels asked for#

In February 1947 Taruc gave a journalist his minimum terms of peace: democratic rights restored, charges from 1946 dropped, local officials and the police command in central Luzon replaced, the six Democratic Alliance congressmen elected in 1946 seated, and land reform toward the abolition of tenancy (Kerkvliet, The Huk Rebellion, 1977, p. 171, citing the Manila Chronicle, 7 February 1947).

A top secret State Department paper for National Security Council staff, dated 20 June 1950, called the Huks a serious military problem rooted in the rice plain and recorded that the army and constabulary had mistreated the population while the rebels gained by their conduct:

“In marked contrast to the Philippine army and constabulary, which has usually mistreated and oppressed the population in areas where they have operated, the Huks have increased their popular support by correct behavior.” — US Department of State draft paper for NSC staff, 20 June 1950 (Source: history.state.gov).

It argued for American military advice to “insure the liquidation of the military phase” and called the leadership “totally captured by the Communists”. It was a policy paper written to secure a decision. Its value is the convergence: a rebel song and a hostile intelligence estimate point to the same source of support.

The Army chronology supplies the skeleton: insurrection in May 1947, the name HMB in November 1948, a raid on the Politburo, the party’s top leadership body, in October 1950, resettlement under a programme known as EDCOR in February 1951, Taruc’s surrender in May 1954. Written to draw lessons for future counterinsurgency, its foreword celebrates the defeat; it concedes that time pressure limited primary research and that General Lansdale, who helped shape the campaign, reviewed the draft. Whether reform or force defeated the rebels remains disputed, and the rebel sources do not address it.

A party rebuilt#

Two decades later the countryside became the objective of a new party. The Communist Party of the Philippines was re-established on 26 December 1968:

“The Communist Party of the Philippines is now re-established and rebuilt as a party of Mao Tse-tung’s thought.” — Communist Party of the Philippines, “Programme for a People’s Democratic Revolution”, ratified 26 December 1968, quoted by Azama, 1985 (Source: globalsecurity.org).

This was Maoism in its Philippine form, revolution from rural base areas rather than city strikes, with “armed struggle and the national united front”, an alliance of political groups, as its two weapons. It broke with the older pro-Moscow party, whose leaders it called revisionist, and its authors were teachers and students around Manila rather than peasants. The founding resolution, drafted by Jose Maria Sison as Amado Guerrero, put the break more sharply:

“What we need to rebuild in the Philippines today is a proletarian revolutionary party that is armed with Mao Tsetung Thought.” — “Rectify Errors, Rebuild the Party!”, CPP, 26 December 1968 (Source: philippinerevolution.nu).

It pledged a Chinese-style rectification, agrarian revolution and making the countryside surround the cities. A programme records an intention, not an achievement.

Sison’s own account documents the other side. Arrested on 10 November 1977, blindfolded and taken to Fort Bonifacio, he described in a statement intended for the Supreme Court beatings and water torture meant to make him name successors and the strength of the New People’s Army, the party’s armed wing:

“Water was poured into my nostrils through the towel and my mouth was held shut for strangulation effect in order to force me to seek relief and answer questions.” — Jose Maria Sison, statement intended for the Supreme Court, 26 April 1979 (Source: bannedthought.net).

He refused a statement without a lawyer and smuggled writings out through his counsel. It is a detainee’s account written for a legal and political purpose under Marcos’s martial law, with no independent medical record attached, and one of the few detailed first-hand descriptions of martial-law detention in English.

A treaty and a street#

Japan’s communists worked under different conditions: a legal party in a wealthy country first occupied by the United States, then allied to it. Its general secretary Kyuichi Tokuda defended the 1951 New Programme in an article dated 20 December 1951:

“Our Party defines the future revolution in Japan as a national-liberation democratic revolution.” — Kyuichi Tokuda, General Secretary of the JCP, dated 20 December 1951, published 15 February 1953 (Source: revolutionarydemocracy.org).

He presented it as a struggle against the occupation and the Yoshida government. The party later repudiated the armed turn; its 2022 centenary account called 1950, when Stalin and China pressed it toward armed struggle, its gravest error, though the 1951 article had presented that line as revolutionary necessity. The same account recalled an Okinawan rally of 1950 where Senaga Kamejiro argued for collective voice:

“If I shout alone, it could be heard up to fifty meters away. If all the people gathered here shout in unison, all the citizens of Naha City could hear it.” — Senaga Kamejiro, Okinawan rally speech, 1950, quoted in Shii Kazuo’s JCP centenary speech, 2022 (Source: jcp.or.jp).

The words survive through the party’s address, not an independent transcript. The 1961 programme was one of anti-imperialism and anti-monopoly; the present one, adopted in 2004 and revised in 2020, seeks a democratic revolution ending subordination to the United States through a parliamentary majority, with socialist transformation later. It states a goal, not an achievement.

In 1960 the security treaty with the United States, known as Anpo, brought such arguments to the streets. Hamaya Hiroshi photographed the demonstrations from 20 May to 22 June. His sequence records Zengakuren students, members of the national student federation, entering the prime minister’s residence on 3 June, the storming of the Diet’s south gate on 15 June, the car of James Hagerty, President Eisenhower’s press secretary surrounded at Haneda on 10 June, three general strikes, schoolchildren signing petitions and mourners for Kanba Michiko. The treaty survived, but Prime Minister Kishi resigned and Eisenhower’s visit was cancelled. Hamaya was a sympathiser and the curators note that he framed and cropped to emphasise crowds. His afterword located the cause at home:

“It wasn’t international communism that created the students the government saw as enemies, but Japanese politics and the institutionalized violence (bōryokushugi) among Japan’s politicians.” — Hamaya Hiroshi, afterword to his 1960 photo book, translated and quoted by MIT Visualizing Cultures (Source: visualizingcultures.mit.edu).

His coalition, students, workers, housewives, farmers and schoolchildren, was broader than the militants who stormed the Diet. A photograph shows who was present, not how many or why.

The revolt turns inward#

Eight years after Anpo, protest moved from the street to the university. During the Zenkyoto occupations of 1968 and 1969, students barricaded campuses including the University of Tokyo. A Zenkyoto leader there, Yamamoto Yoshitaka, explained what the barricades meant to a scholar writing at the time:

“negation of the university which produces men to serve capital as if in a factory, and also negation of the existence of students whose only future was to be cogs in the power machine thus created” — Yamamoto Yoshitaka, Todai Zenkyoto leader, quoted by Chushichi Tsuzuki, 1970 (Source: theanarchistlibrary.org).

The words reached English through Tsuzuki’s article, whose footnotes lead to Japanese-language sources; he wrote as a sympathiser and there is no indication Yamamoto reviewed the wording. A minority occupied campuses and factional beatings drove others away, though the occupations closed many universities; another activist called the militants “aristocratic anarchists”.

The United Red Army, formed in the early 1970s from a merger of armed groups, turned that violence inward. It purged its own members at a mountain base, killing twelve; two earlier victims brought the group’s internal death toll to fourteen (Miki Takeshi, “Terror in Japan,” Nippon.com, 2022; Unseen Japan, 2021). In February 1972 a small group held a lodge at Asama Sansō, and the final police assault on 28 February was broadcast live for over ten hours. A survivor, Katō Michinori, gave a BBC interview in 2019, though the audio is not used as evidence here. The clearest verdict came from its leader Sakaguchi Hiroshi, refusing by telephone when the Japanese Red Army demanded his release during a 1975 hostage crisis in Kuala Lumpur:

“I won’t go. I’ve decided violence was the wrong way to pursue the struggle.” — Sakaguchi Hiroshi, from prison during the 1975 Kuala Lumpur hostage crisis, reported by Miki Takeshi, 2022 (Source: nippon.com).

The sentence reached print decades later through a journalist, but it marks the destruction of the New Left’s standing from inside. The purge victims speak nowhere in this record; the voices that survive are perpetrators, journalists and memory activists.

How historians have read it#

  • Benedict J. Kerkvliet, The Huk Rebellion: A Study of Peasant Revolt in the Philippines (University of California Press, 1977). Reconstructs the revolt from participants and sympathisers rather than counterinsurgency officials; the necessary counterweight to the Army and Marine studies. Source: globalsecurity.org
  • Nick Kapur, Japan at the Crossroads: Conflict and Compromise after Anpo (Harvard University Press, 2018). Treats 1960 as the moment postwar Japan’s political settlement was remade: the treaty survived, Kishi resigned and Eisenhower’s visit was cancelled. Open-access excerpt: Treats 1960 as the moment postwar Japan’s political settlement was remade: the treaty survived
  • Eiji Oguma, 1968 (Shinyosha, 2009, two volumes). Reconstructs the youth revolt from newspapers, reminiscences, handbills and newsletters; argues that “self-negation” named a new, nonmaterial alienation produced by rapid economic growth. Its claim that the rebels adapted to the consumer society they attacked is contested by reviewers. Source: harvard-yenching.org
  • Vera Mackie, “Embodied Memories, Emotional Geographies: Nakamoto Takako’s Diary of the Anpo Struggle”, Japanese Studies 31:3 (2011), pp. 319–331. A reading of a 1963 published diary, useful for the emotional and gendered dimensions of participation. Source: visualizingcultures.mit.edu
  • Chelsea Szendi Schieder, Coed Revolution: The Female Student in the Japanese New Left (Duke University Press, 2021). Studies women students and how male activists and the media reframed them; Patricia Steinhoff’s review notes that Kanba Michiko’s radical writings were recast as those of a naive schoolgirl. DOI: mni.2022.0041

Broad agreement fixes the main outlines: the Huk rebellion grew from tenancy and wartime mobilisation, and government abuse helped the rebels; the Anpo protests were the largest postwar Japanese movement and failed in their immediate aim; Zenkyoto involved a minority of students but closed many campuses; the United Red Army’s internal killings discredited the New Left. Interpretation divides on the weight to give Magsaysay’s reforms against peasant grievance, on whether Oguma’s account of adaptation understates lasting radical commitment, and on how much responsibility for the JCP’s 1950 split belongs to Moscow and Beijing. These are disputes over evidence, not equally supported claims.

Limits of the record#

The record is uneven and politically shaped. Philippine Huk testimony in English comes mainly from leaders who later broke with one another, and the authorship of Taruc’s best-known memoir is contested. Party documents are authoritative about the language parties used and polemical about their pasts. The JCP’s centenary speech is the clearest Japanese narrative available, but it is an anniversary address by the incumbent chair. Student testimony reaches English through a few translations selected by scholars; the United Red Army’s purge victims speak nowhere in this record.

No Japanese- or Tagalog-language source was consulted in the original, and no physical archive was visited; the Hamaya prints, the Army history and the State Department paper are digital copies. The collections that would strengthen the account are the Hamaya archive in Tokyo, Japanese court records from the Red Army trials, National Archives copies of intelligence on the Huks, and Philippine detention records from the martial-law years. A party programme records an intention, not a deed, and a counterinsurgency study written to draw lessons for future wars is not a neutral account.
