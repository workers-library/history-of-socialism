The First International: An Organisation in Documents, 1864–1876

On 28 September 1864 a public meeting at St Martin's Hall in London, called after an address on the Polish insurrection to Paris workers, founded the International Working Men's Association.

On 28 September 1864 a public meeting at St Martin’s Hall in London, called after an address on the Polish insurrection to Paris workers, founded the International Working Men’s Association. Its purpose was to link workers’ organisations in different countries in one permanent body. Wilhelm Eichhoff, a German socialist whose first history of the Association was written with Marx’s help, described the moment: “The meeting gave birth to the International Working Men’s Association.” (Eichhoff, The International Workingmen’s Association, 1868). This was internationalism before it meant a state system or a Comintern. In practice it was a correspondence network, a subscription of a penny a member, a statistical inquiry into working conditions, strike solidarity and a running argument about whether workers should take political power at all. The organisation kept records of unusual detail: an address and rules drafted by Marx, a Minute Book of weekly meetings, congress resolutions with named vote counts, and a quarrel conducted in circulars that both sides intended as evidence.

The address and the rules#

The founding documents were written in a sub-committee that contained rival programmes. Marx told Engels on 4 November 1864 that Odger and Cremer had organised the meeting, Le Lubez had invited him, and he had supplied Eccarius, a German worker, as a speaker. Major Wolff put forward Mazzinian statutes, named for the Italian nationalist Giuseppe Mazzini, and the Owenite Weston, a follower of Robert Owen’s cooperative socialism, offered his own programme. Marx displaced both with a text of his own. “It was very difficult to frame the thing so that our view should appear in a form that would make it acceptable to the present outlook of the workers’ movement.” (Marx to Engels, 4 November 1864). The General Council, the body elected to conduct business between congresses, adopted the address and rules on 1 November. Its Minute Book is terse but not silent: “The amendment being carried, the word “profitmongers” was struck out and the Address was unanimously agreed to.” (General Council minutes, 1 November 1864).

The address drew on British parliamentary and official statistics to argue that growing trade had not reduced working-class misery, and ended with the claim that working-class emancipation had to be won by workers themselves: “To conquer political power has, therefore, become the great duty of the working classes.” (Marx, Inaugural Address, October 1864). The rules gave the Association an annual congress and an elected General Council with power to add to its own number, act as an agency between sections and publish reports. Their shortest maxim was “no rights without duties, no duties without rights” (General Rules, October 1864). A programme is not a neutral description, and the text circulated in languages that altered it. Marx later noted that the 1864 Paris translation had dropped both the phrase “as a means” and the words about the abolition of all class rule, which changed what the preamble said about political movements. Arguments about Proudhonism were live in the German-speaking sections as well. When the editors of Der Social-Demokrat asked Marx to judge the recently dead Proudhon, he objected: “You yourself have now to accept responsibility for having imposed upon me the role of a judge of the dead so soon after this man’s death.” (Marx to J. B. Schweitzer, 24 January 1865). He praised the style of What is Property? while dismissing Proudhon’s later economics as the outlook of the petty bourgeoisie, the small property-owning middle layer.

The weekly work and the Commune#

The Minute Book records the ordinary business of building an organisation: a thousand copies of the address and rules ordered, the Bee-Hive adopted as its organ, personal guarantees taken out by Council members for a rented room, and an address to Abraham Lincoln on his re-election in 1864 pasted into the volume as a newspaper cutting. At the Geneva Congress of 3 to 8 September 1866, Eichhoff reported sixty delegates, a dispute over the credentials of French members who could not form a legal organisation at home, the adoption of the rules, a decision to keep the Council in London, a vote of 50 to 10 for the eight-hour day, and a plan for workers to investigate their own conditions. These are recorded votes, not an account of what the delegates thought worth debating.

The Council’s work reached the Paris Commune of 1871. It published an address in defence of the rising, and two of its own members, Odger and Lucraft, resigned because they opposed that support. For more than eight months the Council organised relief for Commune refugees. The question of what workers’ political action meant had ceased to be abstract.

The Alliance#

Mikhail Bakunin, a Russian revolutionary who argued for the abolition of the state and of coercive authority, an outlook later called anarchism, gathered the International Alliance of Socialist Democracy in Geneva in 1868. Its public programme declared itself atheist and socialist, demanded an end to the right of inheritance, and stated that “it also rejects any political action which does not have as its immediate and direct aim the triumph of the workers’ cause against capital.” (Bakunin, Programme of the International Alliance, October 1868). Its rules described it as a branch of the Association while giving it a central bureau, national bureaus, separate meetings at congresses and its own monthly subscription of ten centimes. On 22 December 1868 the General Council refused affiliation on constitutional grounds: “The International Alliance of Socialist Democracy may not be admitted as a branch of the International Working Men’s Association;” (General Council resolution, 22 December 1868). The stated reason was that a second international body working both inside and outside the Association would disorganise it.

The quarrel then ran through the Swiss federations, Spain and the London Conference of 1871. In a confidential communication of 28 March 1870, Marx wrote that “Bakunin then sought to achieve his aim – to transform the International into his private instrument – by other means.” (Marx, Confidential Communication on Bakunin, 28 March 1870). In 1872 Marx and Engels issued a circular preparing the case against him: “To this end, he created a special instrument, the International Alliance of Socialist Democracy, intended to become an International within the International.” (Fictitious Splits in the International, 5 March 1872). Engels told the Hague Congress’s investigating commission that “The Alliance of Socialist Democracy was founded by M. Bakunin towards the end of 1868. It was an international society claiming to function, at the same time, both within and without the International Working Men’s Association.” (Engels, Report on the Alliance, August 1872). The Council also produced rules it described as secret, which required the Alliance to work inside local federations without revealing itself. Those rules were supplied by dissidents from the Spanish Alliance, and the accused did not appear before the commission. The documentary case is substantial, but it was made by one party and never tested adversarially.

The Hague and after#

The Hague Congress of 2 to 7 September 1872 settled the dispute in votes. Article 7a made the formation of the working class into a political party indispensable and repeated the founding address in constitutional form: “The conquest of political power has therefore become the great duty of the working class.” (Hague Congress, Article 7a, September 1872). It passed by 29 votes to 5 with 8 abstentions. The Council’s powers to suspend sections were enlarged; Bakunin was expelled by 27 votes to 6 and Guillaume by 25 to 9, while Schwitzguébel survived by 16 to 15; and the Council’s seat moved to New York by 30 votes to 14. Many French and German delegates had left before the Alliance votes were taken, a detail the official resolutions record and that matters when judging the majorities. Engels’s answer to the anti-authoritarians appeared in these years. Without naming Bakunin, he argued that a cotton mill, a railway and a ship at sea all require coordination and obedience: “Wanting to abolish authority in large-scale industry is tantamount to wanting to abolish industry itself, to destroy the power loom in order to return to the spinning wheel.” (Engels, On Authority, written 1872, published 1874).

Bakunin’s reply appeared within weeks. He credited the “few hundred anonymous workers” of five countries as the true makers of the Association and called the congress a managed affair: “This has caused a great split in the International. Let us not deceive ourselves; the basic unity of the International has been fractured.” (Bakunin, On the International Workingmen’s Association and Karl Marx, 1872, translated by Sam Dolgoff). His claim of a fictitious majority cannot be confirmed or refuted from the vote counts alone. In 1873 Engels wrote from London on the Spanish cantonal revolts, where men who had denounced political action sat on provincial juntas: “As soon as they were faced with a serious revolutionary situation, the Bakuninists had to throw the whole of their old programme overboard.” (Engels, The Bakuninists at Work, October 1873). The Geneva Congress of 8 to 13 September 1873 was the last, with 28 of its 31 delegates coming from Swiss organisations or Swiss-based exile sections. The Council in New York had no such reach, and the Association passed out of existence in 1876.

How historians have read it#

  • Boris Nicolaievsky and Otto Maenchen-Helfen, Karl Marx: Man and Fighter (1936). Their account of the Hague Congress treats the removal of the Council to New York as the effective end of the International and examines Marx’s motives at length. Excerpt
  • G. M. Stekloff, History of the First International (1928), translated by Eden and Cedar Paul. A Marxist history written from the movement’s documentary tradition, in two parts covering the Marxist International and then the anti-authoritarian International after Saint-Imier; it treats the split as a real division with its own later history, not as a conspiracy alone. Full text
  • James Joll, The Anarchists (1964; US edition 1965). Places the Bakunin–Marx conflict within the wider European history of anarchism; a corrective to accounts written only from the Council’s archives. Catalogue record
  • Paul Avrich, The Russian Anarchists (1971). Gives Bakunin’s Russian context and the student and exile milieu from which the Alliance drew recruits; it treats his centrality as established without reducing the dispute to personal rivalry. Catalogue record
  • Institute of Marxism-Leninism, Documents of the First International (Lawrence & Wishart, 1963–). The edited source base for the General Council minutes, indispensable for a writer, though its Soviet editorial framing should be separated from the documents it prints. Catalogue record

There is broad agreement on the dates and decisions: the International was founded in 1864, the Council sat in London, the Alliance crisis was real, and the Hague Congress of 1872 expelled Bakunin and moved the Council to New York. Historians disagree over what the split was chiefly about: defence of working-class politics against abstention, a clash of organisational principles, or an episode in the rivalry between two leaders and their exile networks. The primary sources support all three readings in part. Stekloff and the Council documents stress doctrine and organisation; Nicolaievsky and Maenchen-Helfen stress Marx’s conduct and motives; Joll and Avrich set the conflict in a wider anarchist history. These accounts are not equally weighted without returning to the documents.

Limits of the record#

The surviving archive is largely the winners’ archive as digitised by the Marxists Internet Archive, supplemented by Bakunin’s published reply. It is a reliable transcription service but a partisan selection; documents hostile to Marx are present chiefly because both sides published them. The General Council minutes come through a Soviet-edited volume from a Minute Book held in Moscow, and the congress proceedings for Geneva, Lausanne, Brussels and Basel were not consulted individually. The Alliance programme, the Council circulars, Engels’s Hague report and the Spanish material are all read in translation, with translators named where the pages name them. The force of words such as authority, autonomy and abstention from politics needs checking against the French and German originals. Almost everything here was written by the literate, organised and mainly male leadership. Ordinary members appear only when they dissented, as with the French delegates without credentials at Geneva, the New Madrid Federation and the Jura watchmakers, and even then through the reports of opponents. No first-hand account by a woman member survives in this record, though women signed the Alliance’s founding list. There is no account of life in the New York Council after 1872 and no serial record of local sections. The Minute Book names who proposed and seconded motions; it does not say what a thousand members thought. A full social history would need the archives in Moscow and Amsterdam and work in French, German, Italian and Spanish.
