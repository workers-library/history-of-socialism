Antonio Gramsci: Cultural Hegemony and the Modern Prince

A research profile of the Italian Marxist's account of hegemony, the party, and the dispute over how his Prison Notebooks were edited and used.

Antonio Gramsci wrote most of what he is famous for in prison, in notes that he never arranged into a book. He was a Marxist and a founder of the Communist Party of Italy who spent his last years under fascist imprisonment. His notes set out how a ruling class holds power by winning consent as well as by using force, and how a party might build the collective will needed to replace it. Those ideas became some of the most used and most disputed concepts in twentieth-century left-wing thought. This profile sets out what Gramsci wrote, what later editors made of his notes, and where scholars disagree.

Biography#

Antonio Gramsci was born at Ales in Sardinia on 22 January 1891, the fourth of seven children of Francesco Gramsci, a civil servant in the local registry office, and Giuseppina Marcias. The family moved to Ghilarza after his father was arrested and imprisoned for an administrative irregularity. Gramsci finished elementary school in 1903 with the highest marks and then, because the family had little money, worked for two years in the tax office before resuming his studies. He attended the lyceum at Cagliari and in 1911 won a scholarship to the University of Turin, where he enrolled in letters and studied linguistics under Matteo Bartoli. His health was fragile and he stopped taking examinations; his last examination was in April 1915. He had already begun journalism, writing for Il Grido del popolo, and in December 1915 he joined the Turin editorial staff of the Socialist Party daily Avanti! (Casa Museo Antonio Gramsci, biography and chronology; International Gramsci Society, chronology).

After the Turin uprising of August 1917, when the authorities arrested most of the city’s socialist leaders, Gramsci became secretary of the Turin section of the Italian Socialist Party. In 1919 he and Angelo Tasca, Palmiro Togliatti and Umberto Terracini founded the weekly L’Ordine nuovo (The New Order), the first issue appearing on 1 May 1919. The journal joined Socialist culture to the factory-council movement in Turin. Gramsci took part in the Livorno congress of January 1921 at which the Communist Party of Italy was founded, and joined its central committee. In 1922 he went to Moscow as the party’s representative to the Communist International, met Giulia Schucht there, and, according to the two institutional chronologies opened for this profile, met Lenin on 25 October 1922. Mussolini became prime minister that same month, and an arrest warrant prevented Gramsci from returning to Italy. He worked in Vienna from December 1923, was elected to parliament in April 1924, returned to Italy in May, and became general secretary of the Communist Party in August 1924. His only speech in parliament was on 16 May 1925, against a law on secret societies (Fondazione Gramsci, biography; Casa Museo, chronology; IGS, chronology; Gramsci, “Speech to the Italian parliament”, 16 May 1925).

Fascist authorities arrested him in Rome on the evening of 8 November 1926 despite his parliamentary immunity. He was held at Regina Coeli, sentenced to five years of internal exile, and sent to Ustica in December. A military court in Milan issued a new warrant in January 1927 and he was imprisoned there. The Special Tribunal for the Defence of the State tried him in Rome between 28 May and 4 June 1928 with twenty-one other Communist leaders, and on 4 June sentenced him to twenty years, four months and five days. Because of his health he was sent to the special prison at Turi near Bari. He received permission to write in his cell in January 1929 and began the first of his prison notebooks on 8 February 1929. An amnesty reduced the sentence to twelve years and four months in 1932. Serious illness led to his transfer to a clinic at Formia in November 1933, to conditional freedom in October 1934, and to the Quisisana clinic in Rome in August 1935. His sentence expired on 21 April 1937. He suffered a cerebral haemorrhage on the evening of 25 April and died early on 27 April 1937. His sister-in-law Tatiana Schucht saved the notebooks and his books; his ashes were first deposited at the Verano cemetery and later moved to the Protestant Cemetery in Rome (Casa Museo, chronology; IGS, chronology; Einaudi, Quaderni del carcere catalogue entry).

Two points of doubt should be stated. The Casa Museo attributes his physical condition to Pott’s disease, while the International Gramsci Society chronology records a uremic disorder and nervous exhaustion; no clinical record was opened for this profile, so no diagnosis is stated here as established fact. One older encyclopedia entry gives 23 January 1891 as his birth date, but the institutional records opened all give 22 January, which is the date used here.

Intellectual tradition#

Gramsci belonged to the Italian Socialist Party until 1921 and then to the Communist Party of Italy and the Communist International. He was a Marxist, but he resisted the economism he found in the Second International: the idea that a change in economic conditions automatically produces political and cultural change. In the 1917 article “The Revolution against Capital” he wrote that “The Bolsheviks renounce Karl Marx” in the sense that they did not treat Capital as a set of rules that history had to obey before a revolution could occur; “The facts have surpassed ideologies.” He insisted that “the vivifying immanent thought” of Marx survived in a living collective will, not in a dogmatic “outer doctrine of dogmatic and indisputable affirmations” (Gramsci, “The Revolution against ‘Capital’”, first published Avanti!, 24 December 1917). In his 1925 parliamentary speech he likewise treated fascism as a concrete historical form to be analysed rather than a temporary accident, and described the law against secret societies as aimed at workers’ and peasants’ organisations (Gramsci, 1925 speech).

In prison he used the phrase “philosophy of praxis” for Marxism, partly as a euphemism to pass the censor and partly, in the editors’ words, as “an autonomous term used by Gramsci to define what he saw to be a central characteristic of the philosophy of Marxism, the inseparable link it establishes between theory and practice, thought and action”. Hoare and Smith report that he also rejected economism, syndicalism and “spontaneism” in his notes on the party, and that he called the party a “Jacobin” force, by which he meant a force that binds the countryside to the town under working-class leadership (Hoare and Smith, “This Edition: Selection and Translation” and introduction to “The Modern Prince”, in Selections from the Prison Notebooks).

The label “Western Marxist” was applied to him by later writers, not, so far as could be verified, by himself. No primary statement was found in which he rejected a label others had given him; the rejections recorded above are of political doctrines, not of names.

Major contributions#

The question of authorship has to be settled before the concepts can be described. Gramsci wrote thousands of notes in thirty-three exercise books, of which twenty-nine contain notes and four translations. He never prepared a finished book. The first posthumous editions, published by Einaudi between 1948 and 1951, rearranged the notes by theme. Hoare and Smith, whose 1971 English Selections from the Prison Notebooks made Gramsci famous in the English-speaking world, followed that thematic arrangement but changed it further. They write that “our ordering, in terms of a rough division into two parts, on the Party and on the State, is original”, and that because the notes were fragmentary “there is clearly no alternative to a reordering of some kind” (Hoare and Smith, “Gramsci’s Prison Notebooks” and “This Edition: Selection and Translation”, in Selections; MIA, contents of Selections). A famous “text” such as The Modern Prince is therefore an editorial object built from notes written at different times, while the phrases inside it are Gramsci’s own. The distinction matters for every concept that follows.

Hegemony is Gramsci’s own term, in the Italian egemonia, but he inherited it from the socialist and Comintern argument about the “hegemony of the proletariat”: leadership of allied classes, especially peasants, as distinct from domination of enemies. In plain English, hegemony means leadership by consent, the way a dominant group gets other groups to accept its rule as natural instead of relying on force alone. Hoare and Smith note that in Gramsci “the term ‘hegemony’ … has two faces”: it is contrasted with domination, and it is also opposed to the “corporate” or “economic-corporate” phase in which a group defends only its own interest (Hoare and Smith, “Terminology”, in Selections). He extended the term to bourgeois rule. In a draft of 1929–30, quoted by the editors, he wrote that “a class is dominant in two ways, i.e. ‘leading’ and ‘dominant’. It leads the classes which are its allies, and dominates those which are its enemies”. The best-known formulation is his definition of the integral state as “State = political society + civil society, in other words hegemony protected by the armour of coercion” (Gramsci, “Hegemony (Civil Society) and Separation of Powers”, in Selections). The exact label “cultural hegemony” as a free-standing theory is a later construction. In the English Selections the adjective appears only in the paired phrase “political and cultural hegemony”, once in Gramsci’s own text on the ethical state and once in a passage the editors quote from another volume; the phrase “cultural hegemony” by itself does not occur in that edition.

Historic bloc, also translated “historical bloc” (Italian blocco storico), is Gramsci’s own term for the temporary unity of material forces and ideas. He wrote that “Structures and superstructures form an ‘historical bloc’. That is to say the complex, contradictory and discordant ensemble of the superstructures is the reflection of the ensemble of the social relations of production”. He added that material forces are the content and ideologies the form, while warning that this distinction “has purely didactic value” (Gramsci, “The Study of Philosophy”, in Selections). The fluctuation between “historic” and “historical” in English is a translation choice, not a distinction in Gramsci. The editorial chapter called “The Concept of ‘Historical Bloc’” is not a text Gramsci wrote under that title: the MIA contents page marks it as “published for the first time in The Reader” (MIA, contents of An Antonio Gramsci Reader, ed. David Forgacs, 1988).

Passive revolution is Gramsci’s own term, taken from the Italian historian and politician Vincenzo Cuoco, who used it of the Neapolitan revolution of 1799. Hoare and Smith note that Lenin also used the phrase in 1906 but that there is no evidence Gramsci knew that text. They distinguish two uses in Gramsci: a revolution without mass participation, such as the Risorgimento, and a slow transformation beneath the surface of society, as in Restoration France or under fascism (Hoare and Smith, introduction to “On Italian History”, in Selections). MIA’s contents of Notebook 4 lists a note headed “Vincenzo Cuoco and passive revolution” (MIA, Notebook 4 contents). The numbered pieces called “The Concept of Passive Revolution i, ii, iii” and “Fascism as Passive Revolution: First Version / Second Version” in Forgacs’s Reader are editorial groupings of notes, not titles Gramsci gave to finished essays (MIA, Reader contents).

The Modern Prince is Gramsci’s phrase, taken from Machiavelli, and it means the political party understood as the “myth-prince” that creates a collective will. His own words are: “The modern prince, the myth-prince, cannot be a real person, a concrete individual. It can only be an organism, a complex element of society in which a collective will, which has already been recognised and has to some extent asserted itself in action, begins to take concrete form. History has already provided this organism, and it is the political party”. He called for “a part devoted to Jacobinism” in this prince, meaning an original creation of political will rather than a restoration (Gramsci, “The Modern Prince”, in Selections). The assembled text is an editorial construction. Hoare and Smith state that the political notes came mainly from a late notebook datable to 1933–34 in which earlier texts were rewritten in an order with “no particular internal coherence”, and that they grouped them into a section on the party and a section on the state (Hoare and Smith, “This Edition: Selection and Translation”, in Selections; MIA, contents of Selections). The phrase had already become a book title in English with The Modern Prince and Other Writings (1957), well before the 1971 Selections (Hoare and Smith, preface to Selections).

Organic intellectual is Gramsci’s own term (Italian intellettuale organico). He distinguished “traditional” intellectuals, who imagine themselves independent of class, from those who give a social group “homogeneity and an awareness of its own function not only in the economic but also in the social and political fields”. His startling formulation was: “All men are intellectuals, one could therefore say: but not all men have in society the function of intellectuals.” The new intellectual, he wrote, must be “constructor, organiser, ‘permanent persuader’ and not just a simple orator” (Gramsci, “The Intellectuals”, in Selections). He thought of the party as the institution that joins a class’s own intellectuals to sympathetic traditional intellectuals. The essay “The Intellectuals” as printed in Selections is again an editorial assembly: it draws on Notebook XXIX and on the Einaudi volume Gli intellettuali e l’organizzazione della cultura (Hoare and Smith, “This Edition: Selection and Translation”, in Selections).

War of position and war of manoeuvre are Gramsci’s own terms, borrowed from military language. A war of manoeuvre is a frontal attack on state power; a war of position is a long struggle to win consent in civil society before that assault, a siege rather than a charge. He wrote that “The superstructures of civil society are like the trench-systems of modern warfare”, so that an economic crisis might destroy the outer defences of a ruling bloc without reaching the real line of resistance. In the same note he concluded: “In the East the State was everything, civil society was primordial and gelatinous; in the West, there was a proper relation between State and civil society, and when the State trembled a sturdy structure of civil society was at once revealed. The State was only an outer ditch, behind which there stood a powerful system of fortresses and earthworks” (Gramsci, “Political Struggle and Military War”, in Selections). Elsewhere he called the shift from frontal attack to siege politics “the most important question of political theory that the post-war period has posed” (Gramsci, “The Transition from the War of Manoeuvre (Frontal Attack) to the War of Position—in the Political Field as Well”, in Selections). Hoare and Smith caution that “war of position” is “by no means a consistent or univocal concept” in his writing, and that he sometimes linked it to passive revolution (Hoare and Smith, introduction to “On Italian History”, in Selections).

Two further terms are his own and matter for the chapters on the state. Transformism (trasformismo) described the slow parliamentary absorption of opponents into a ruling bloc; he wrote of “‘molecular’ transformism” between 1860 and 1900 and of the transformism of whole groups after 1900 (Gramsci, “Notes on Italian History”, in Selections). The integral state named the combination of consent and coercion: “State (in its integral meaning: dictatorship + hegemony)” appears in a note on politics and military science (Gramsci, “Politics and Military Science”, in Selections).

The verdict, stated plainly: hegemony, historic bloc, passive revolution, the modern prince, the organic intellectual, the war of position and manoeuvre, transformism and the integral state are Gramsci’s terms. The familiar English “texts” that carry those terms — The Modern Prince, “The Intellectuals”, “The Concept of Historical Bloc”, the numbered “Passive Revolution” passages — are editorial constructions made from unorganised notes. “Cultural hegemony” as a compact name for a theory of ideological domination is a later scholarly label, even though the words “political and cultural hegemony” occur in the English translation.

Analysis of capitalism#

Gramsci did not write an economics treatise. His account of capitalism is scattered through journalism and prison notes, and its central claim is that capitalist power has two sides: it produces goods and surplus through particular forms of work, and it secures consent for that arrangement through institutions outside the state as well as through law and force. The clearest early statement is his 1925 speech. There he described Italian capitalism as structurally weak: it lacked raw materials, lacked colonies that could support a permanent labour aristocracy, and failed to solve the southern peasant question. He said the state took taxes from the south and gave them as a base to the capitalism of northern Italy, and he called Italian “imperialism” an export of finance capital that used Italian emigrant labour for the profit of capitalists in other countries (Gramsci, 1925 speech).

His most systematic analysis of contemporary capitalism is the prison essay “Americanism and Fordism”. Its editors summarise his question as whether the changes in production after the First World War constituted a new historical epoch or a passing conjunction, and note that he linked changes in the base, such as scientific management, to changes in the superstructure, such as prohibition and the regulation of sexuality. Gramsci’s own text treats Taylorism as the deliberate breaking of skilled work. Taylor’s phrase about the “trained gorilla”, he wrote, “is in fact expressing with brutal cynicism the purpose of American society—developing in the worker to the highest degree automatic and mechanical attitudes, breaking up the old psycho-physical nexus of qualified professional work, which demands a certain active participation of intelligence, fantasy and initiative on the part of the worker, and reducing productive operations exclusively to the mechanical, physical aspect.” He expected part of the old working class to be “pitilessly eliminated from the world of labour” and a new psycho-physical type to be formed (Gramsci, “Americanism and Fordism”, in Selections; Hoare and Smith, introduction to “Americanism and Fordism”).

On high wages he was equally hard-headed. He described the rationalisation of American production as a “skilful combination of force (destruction of working-class trade unionism on a territorial basis) and persuasion (high wages, various social benefits, extremely subtle ideological and political propaganda)” and observed that “Hegemony here is born in the factory”. He argued that in the United States “the fundamental question of hegemony has not yet been posed”, because the struggle still concerned craft rights against “industrial liberty”. In Italy he saw the rural petty bourgeoisie and a reactionary intelligentsia resisting Americanism, while workers did not oppose productive technique as such but the form it took under exploitation and authoritarian cultural control (Gramsci, “Americanism and Fordism”, in Selections). Fascism interested him as a possible transitional form: he asked what modifications of the balance of forces were taking place beneath it, how Croce was organising long-term consent, and what state intervention in the economy meant in both the New Deal and Fascist Italy (Hoare and Smith, introduction to “On Italian History”, in Selections).

Analysis of socialism#

Gramsci wrote no blueprint of a socialist society. He left no constitution, no plan for ownership, and no detailed design of institutions beyond the party and the broad idea of a “regulated society”. What he did provide was a theory of how a class could become capable of ruling, and a set of warnings about what would defeat it. The party as modern prince had to create a “national-popular collective will” rather than express an already existing one; it had to bind peasants to the working class and overcome the “economic-corporate” stage in which a group thinks only of its immediate trade interest (Hoare and Smith, introduction to “The Modern Prince”, in Selections; Gramsci, “The Modern Prince”).

His institutional preferences can be stated from the notebooks. The state in the full sense included civil society; coercion was one element of it, not the whole. He wrote that “in a doctrine of the State which conceives the latter as tendentially capable of withering away and of being subsumed into regulated society, the argument is a fundamental one”, and that “It is possible to imagine the coercive element of the State withering away by degrees, as ever-more conspicuous elements of regulated society (or ethical State or civil society) make their appearance” (Gramsci, “Hegemony (Civil Society) and Separation of Powers”, in Selections). In another note he said “every State is ethical in as much as one of its most important functions is to raise the great mass of the population to a particular cultural and moral level, a level (or type) which corresponds to the needs of the productive forces for development, and hence to the interests of the ruling classes”, naming the school as a positive educative function and the courts as a repressive and negative educative function (Gramsci, “The State”, in Selections). That is a theory of state functions, not a plan for a post-capitalist constitution.

His account of democracy was tied to hegemony rather than to a formal franchise. In a note quoted by the editors from Note sul Machiavelli, he wrote: “Of the many meanings of democracy, the most realistic and concrete one in my view can be worked out in relation to the concept of ‘hegemony’. In the hegemonic system, there exists democracy between the ‘leading’ group and the groups which are ‘led’, in so far as the development of the economy and thus the legislation which expresses such development favour the (molecular) passage from the ‘led’ groups to the ‘leading’ group” (Hoare and Smith, footnote in “Notes on Italian History”, in Selections). The early writings add a workplace dimension: the L’Ordine nuovo group argued for factory councils as organs of workers’ democracy, and the IGS chronology records Gramsci’s articles on internal commissions and a workers’ defence militia during the 1920 factory occupation (IGS, chronology, 1919–1920). The full text of “Workers’ Democracy” was not opened for this profile, so no further claim is made about its contents.

Recommended reading#

These works are starting points for Gramsci’s arguments, not a substitute for the complete bibliography or for the notebooks themselves.

  • Selections from the Prison Notebooks of Antonio Gramsci (1971). This is the best first book because it gathers the terms his reputation rests on, from hegemony and historic bloc to the modern prince and the integral state, in one English volume, while making clear that the arrangement is the editors’ and not a book Gramsci finished.
  • The Modern Prince and Other Writings (1957). Begin here for the party argument: Gramsci’s “modern prince” is the party that creates a collective will rather than expressing one that already exists, and this short English volume made that idea available before the larger selections appeared.
  • “Contro la legge sulle associazioni segrete” (1925). This speech is the quickest route into his analysis of capitalism: he describes Italian capitalism as structurally weak, short of raw materials and colonies, and says the state took taxes from the south to support the capitalism of northern Italy.
  • A Gramsci Reader: Selected Writings 1916–1935 (1988). This is useful early on because its chapter headings show how editors grouped unorganised notes into pieces such as “The Concept of Historical Bloc”, a reminder that those titles are editorial constructions rather than texts Gramsci wrote under those names.

Selected lectures and interviews#

Gramsci left no audiovisual lectures and no recorded interviews. He died in 1937, and the surviving corpus is written: newspaper articles, party reports, letters and the prison notebooks. He spoke at workers’ study circles in Turin before his arrest, and the International Gramsci Society chronology records a talk on the Russian revolution in 1920 and other meetings, but none of these was filmed or recorded, and no sound or film archive holding his voice was found (IGS, chronology). The nearest equivalent to a lecture is the parliamentary speech of 16 May 1925, which survives as a transcript with interruptions by Mussolini and other deputies; it is a text, not a recording (MIA, “Speech to the Italian parliament”). MIA lists no Gramsci video or audio in its Gramsci archive. This section therefore records an absence, not a selection.

Intellectual influences#

The evidence for influence comes from Gramsci’s own notes and letters rather than from statements in interviews, of which there are none. Marx is everywhere, but the Marx he invokes in 1917 is the Marx of living political practice rather than of a fixed schema. Lenin is the decisive contemporary: Hoare and Smith describe the 1917 article as an “attempt to apply to the Italian situation the lessons of the Bolshevik Revolution” and note that in the notebooks Lenin is called “Ilich” or “Vilich” to evade the censor (Hoare and Smith, preface and “This Edition: Selection and Translation”, in Selections). The IGS chronology records that in 1927, in prison, he asked for Machiavelli’s works, and that Piero Sraffa kept an account at a Milan bookshop so he could obtain books; the resulting notes fill the Machiavelli sections of the prison writings (IGS, chronology, 1926–1927).

The Italian idealists Benedetto Croce and Giovanni Gentile were both targets and sources. Gramsci wrote entire notebooks against Croce’s philosophy and against a “Popular Manual” of sociology that he discusses repeatedly; his note titles in Notebook 4 include “Croce and Marx”, “The Restoration and historicism”, and “Croce and Marx. The value of ideologies” (MIA, Notebook 4 contents). In “The Intellectuals” he treats Croce and Gentile as examples of the traditional intellectual who claims independence while being tied to dominant classes (Gramsci, “The Intellectuals”). IGS records his own retrospective remark about his early phase: “My tendency was still rather Crocean” (IGS, chronology, 1917). Other named figures in the notebooks include Georges Sorel, whose letters to Croce and whose concept of the myth he discusses; Gaetano Mosca and Vilfredo Pareto, whose elite theories he treats as attempts to explain the function of intellectuals; Max Weber, cited in a footnote on the Prussian Junkers; Antonio Labriola, the Italian Marxist; Achille Loria, whose “unproductive worker” he criticises; and Vincenzo Cuoco, source of “passive revolution” (Gramsci, “The Intellectuals” and notes, in Selections; Hoare and Smith, introduction to “On Italian History”). These are documented references in his writing, not a claim that any one of them determined his position.

Influence and legacy#

Gramsci’s concepts became central to postwar Marxism in two distinct waves. The first was the Italian debate after 1945, in which the Communist Party published thematic selections from the notebooks under Palmiro Togliatti’s editorship. Giuseppe Vacca judges that this 1948–51 edition and Togliatti’s reading “constituted an adaptation of Gramsci’s work that was compatible with Stalinism”, while also serving a project of a “new party” and an “Italian road to socialism” (Vacca, “Italian interpretations of Gramsci before and after the critical edition of the Prison notebooks”, Le Carte e la Storia 1/2014, pp. 63–72; abstract and metadata opened, not the full article). The second wave followed the 1975 critical edition and the 1971 English Selections. Peter Thomas notes that the earlier absence of a critical edition shaped the famous critiques of Gramsci by Louis Althusser in Reading Capital (1965) and by Perry Anderson in “The Antinomies of Antonio Gramsci” (1976–77), and that the 1975 edition allowed the first close textual study of the notebooks’ development (Thomas, The Gramscian Moment, University of Amsterdam, 2008).

In English-language cultural studies, Raymond Williams and Stuart Hall made hegemony a central term, and Thomas J. Jackson Lears’s 1985 review in the American Historical Review shows how quickly historians took it up as a way to explain the stability of capitalist culture and the autonomy of subordinate groups (Lears, “The Concept of Cultural Hegemony: Problems and Possibilities”, American Historical Review 90(3), June 1985, pp. 567–593). Theodossopoulos argues that this reception “depoliticiz[ed]” the concept and that later anthropologists often rely on Williams more than on Gramsci, producing a “lighter” use of hegemony that reduces it to beliefs and ideas (Theodossopoulos, “De-essentializing hegemony, with Gramsci, Williams, and Hall”, Focaal 102, 2025, pp. 103–118). In international relations, Robert Cox’s 1983 essay “Gramsci, Hegemony and International Relations: An Essay in Method” adapted hegemony, historic bloc and passive revolution to world order, explicitly saying that Gramsci himself had little to say directly about international relations (Cox, Millennium 12(2), 1983, pp. 162–175; abstract opened, full article blocked). In party and movement theory, the “modern prince” and “war of position” became standard references for organisational strategy; the collected essays of Alastair Davidson show how wide that reception became, with pieces on hegemony and popular culture, Gramsci and Lenin, and Gramsci’s reading of Machiavelli (Davidson, Gramsci in Australia, Brill, 2024, table of contents).

Criticisms and controversies#

The most consequential dispute is about the text itself. Alastair Davidson’s 1977 review of Gerratana’s critical edition records that the earlier Einaudi arrangement “seemed authorised” by Gramsci’s own attempts to order his notes but was a “possible choice” and not the one Gramsci made. It also reports that scholars accused the Communist Party of suppressing, selecting and reordering evidence, and quotes Gerratana’s own method: a critical edition should provide “not so much an interpretation as a necessary philological support” (Davidson, chapter 12 in Gramsci in Australia, Brill, 2024, reproducing his 1977 review). Hoare and Smith concede the point from the other side: “any unequivocal assertions about the aim and status of Gramsci’s theoretical project as contained or sketched out in the Notebooks are necessarily speculative and must be recognised as such” (Hoare and Smith, “Gramsci’s Prison Notebooks”). A second editorial controversy concerns access: the Marxists Internet Archive records that in January 2008 Lawrence & Wishart asked it to withdraw the Hoare translations on the ground that permission had been given contrary to Hoare’s contract, with the result that many MIA Gramsci links, including the texts of “Historical Bloc” and “War of Position and War of Manoeuvre”, became dead links (MIA, “Translations most urgently required for the M.I.A. Antonio Gramsci Archive”). This matters for any project that plans to cite MIA as a stable primary-source archive.

There are substantive theoretical disputes as well. Althusser argued that Gramsci’s philosophy was inadequate because it lacked a proper theory of Marxist philosophy; Thomas replies that Althusser misrepresented Gramsci’s “organic” concepts because the critical edition was not available to him. Anderson argued that Gramsci’s concepts changed and deformed over time, especially on the state and hegemony, and that the prison notes hid a “true” order behind a “hidden order”; Thomas contests all four of the literary assumptions he finds in that reading and argues, in a chapter titled “Civil and Political Hegemony”, against the view that Gramsci located hegemony only in consensual civil society as opposed to the coercive state (Thomas 2008). Lears found “no precise definition of cultural hegemony” in the translated writings and stressed that Gramsci paired hegemony with domination and left the nature of consent ambiguous; he drew on Joseph Femia’s analysis of that ambiguity (Lears 1985). Theodossopoulos’s objection runs the other way: reducing hegemony to a fixed regime or to ideology loses its material and processual character (Theodossopoulos 2025).

The post-Marxist appropriation is contested from within the same broad tradition. James Martin argues that Laclau and Mouffe’s 1985 Hegemony and Socialist Strategy made an “outstanding argumentative manoeuvre” by “shifting the ontological status of hegemony from a theory of ideology to a ‘new political logic’ of social constitution”, and that in doing so they decontextualised Gramsci, treating his particular analysis of Fordist capitalism and his organisational commitments as expendable (Martin, “The Post-Marxist Gramsci”). Martin records that critics including Rustin and Mouzelis accused this move of voluntarism and of losing sociological grounding. This is a dispute about how Gramsci should be read, not a refutation of his own claims.

One limitation belongs in this profile’s own record. Because the research for it read English translations and publisher descriptions rather than the Italian critical edition, it cannot settle disputes that turn on Italian wording, for example whether “historic” or “historical” is the better rendering of blocco storico, or exactly how often Gramsci wrote egemonia culturale. Those questions are left unanswered rather than guessed at.

Related thinkers#

Marx and Lenin are the two necessary reference points: Marx for the critique of political economy that Gramsci refused to treat as a scripture, Lenin for the party and for the idea of hegemony as leadership of allies. Machiavelli supplied the language of the prince and the Jacobin; Gramsci’s notes treat him as a “precocious Jacobin” who tried to join the countryside to the city. Croce and Gentile were the Italian philosophers against whom he defined the philosophy of praxis, and Sorel supplied the concept of the myth that he reworked for the collective will. Vincenzo Cuoco gave him “passive revolution”; Mosca and Pareto gave him elite theories to argue against; Sraffa was the friend who made books available in prison.

For later figures, Thompson and Hall stand for the cultural-studies reception, Althusser and Anderson for the strongest Marxist criticisms, Laclau and Mouffe for the post-Marxist rewriting, and Cox for the international-relations use of hegemony and historic bloc. These are connections and arguments recorded in the sources, not agreements: where the sources show a dispute, this profile has left it as one.
