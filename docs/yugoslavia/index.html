The Yugoslav Experiment: Workers' Councils, Tito and the National Question

In November 1948, months after the Soviet-led Cominform, the association of communist parties through which Moscow directed the others, expelled Yugoslavia, Josip Broz Tito told the Slovene Academy of Arts and Sciences: “Socialism does not require of us that we renounce our love for our socialist country, that we renounce our love for our own people.” (Tito, 26 November 1948). His subject was the charge of nationalism. In his account the break was a defence of the right of a smaller socialist country to take its own road. That claim has to be weighed against what he did with the independence, and against what people inside Yugoslavia later made of it.

The break and the national question#

The case was put in principle by Boris Ziherl, a Yugoslav party ideologist, in 1949: “in addition to the Soviet Union, there is today a series of states whose working populations also quite rightfully consider them as their socialist fatherland” (Ziherl, Communism and Fatherland, 1949). The wartime liberation struggle, he argued, had proved the communists were the truest sons of their own countries. The vocabulary was that of 1949 propaganda and the booklet says nothing about conditions inside Yugoslav factories or republics, but it shows how the leadership wanted the split understood: as a question of equality between socialist states.

Tito put the same case to the French journalist Louis Dalmas in answers dated December 1949: “it is not only possible, but also very useful for small nations to pursue a genuinely principled policy in the settlement of international questions” (Tito, interview with Dalmas). He said the difference between parties “was later transformed – because it could not be otherwise – into differences between the USSR and the Federative People’s Republic” (Ibid.). The interview also shows the limit of the break: asked whether the Soviet show trials of 1936 and 1937 could have been staged, he declined to answer. It reached English through a French bulletin and was reprinted by Trotskyist opponents whose editorial note is not evidence, though the unanswered question is.

Tito’s 1948 speech claimed that the national question had been settled through the common liberation war, naming Macedonia, Montenegro and Bosnia and Herzegovina as republics that had gained equality, and describing personnel sent from Slovenia, Croatia and Serbia to assist the “backward” republics. That is a leader’s assertion of success. The later history of the federation makes it a claim to test, and the record here holds no testimony from people living in those republics about whether they experienced equality.

The factory question and the 1950 law#

On 26 June 1950 Tito introduced a bill to the Federal Assembly: “The adoption of this bill will be the most significant historic act of the Federal Assembly next to the Law on Nationalization of the Means of Production.” (Tito, 26 June 1950). State ownership, he said, had not delivered “the factories for the workers”; directors were still appointed from above, and the party had copied Soviet methods “uncritically”. The transfer would be gradual: “The state functions in the management of economy are not yet ceasing completely, but they are no longer the exclusive factor.” (Ibid.). He named Bosnia, Sandzak, Macedonia, Kosovo and Metohiya, Lika and Montenegro as regions where new factories would turn poor peasants into industrial workers.

The Basic Law passed that day supplies the machinery. “The workers’ councils of the enterprises and of the higher economic associations are to be elected and recalled by the workers’ collectives.” (Basic Law of 26 June 1950). Councils were to meet at least once in six weeks, choose management boards, approve plans and distribute retained accumulation. At least three quarters of board members had to be production workers, serving without pay while keeping their jobs. But the state economic plan remained in force and the director, appointed by a higher body, sat on the board, hired workers and represented the enterprise. A statute records what was authorised, not what happened; the tension between elected council and appointed director was built into the design.

Seven years of councils, and the independent road#

At the First Congress of Workers’ Councils in June 1957 Tito claimed the reform had reached a large part of the workforce: “This was a period of schooling on a giant scale, when one third of the workers employed, that is to say more than six hundred thousand workers and employees, participated in management.” (Tito, 25 June 1957). In the same speech he named localism, competition between enterprises and obstruction of cooperation: “certain people in leading positions in the enterprises deny these rights to the workers councils; and the workers in turn have given in too easily to such improper treatment of the role of the workers councils” (Ibid.). He was both sponsor and judge, his figures were his own, and he blamed individuals or material conditions rather than the design. Nothing in the speech says how many councils were genuinely contested. What it establishes is that the founder recorded participation and obstruction together.

The foreign policy ran on a parallel track. Tito told the Indian Parliament on 27 December 1954: “Is it not absurd to imagine that we, who have so vigorously opposed the division of the world into blocs, should now wish to create some sort of third bloc?” (Tito, 27 December 1954). He gave a Yugoslav government 1946 reparations figure of 1.7 million dead and 400,000 disabled, later judged exaggerated by historians who estimate roughly 1.0 to 1.07 million dead in the liberation war as the basis of independence, and described the 1950 transfer as a stage in democratisation. After Bandung he said: “the conception which prevailed at the Conference is completely in harmony with our conceptions” (Tito, 27 April 1955). A letter to the Soviet party’s Twentieth Congress in February 1956 spoke of “the announcement of the so-called Belgrade Declaration, not only ushered in a new period in the development of relations between our two countries” (Tito, 20 February 1956). The word “so-called” matters: the 1955 declaration between Moscow and Belgrade is not the declaration of the Non-Aligned summit held in Belgrade in 1961.

June 1968#

In May 1968 the Belgrade student newspaper Student wrote: “the lack of perspectives, the lack of solutions to numerous problems, is at the root of various forms of behavior” (Student, “Signs of Political Crisis”, 21 May 1968). Its front page of 4 June was banned. In the surviving excerpts the editors said the press had distorted the events and omitted the main slogans, including “Students with Workers”, and that the struggle concerned social inequality rather than student privileges. The original issues were not opened; the passages were selected by Fredy Perlman, an anarchist critic of the regime.

Dragomir Olujić, who took part in the Belgrade movement and was then a member of the League of Communists and the Student Alliance, recalled it fifty years later: “We were against the regime but not against socialism — we did not demand more socialism but a radically different socialism.” (Olujić, interview with Philippe Alcoy, 2018). He described the clash of 2 June, the burning of a police fire truck, the occupation of the university renamed the “Red University Karl Marx”, the “3+4 demands”, and money and supplies sent by workers at Teleoptik and Velebit. He is candid about what he cannot recall, including who gave a speech calling for Tito’s resignation. The interview is one man’s memory, translated from Serbian by Milica Popović, and it concerns Belgrade.

Tito’s reply of 11 June divided the students: “I’ve come to the conclusion that the vast majority of students, I can say 90%, are honest youth” (Tito, 11 June 1968). He called the revolt spontaneous, blamed later developments on foreign elements and told students to return to their studies. In March 1969 a worker interviewed by Student was asked whether workers governed themselves: “That’s only on paper. When the managers choose their people, workers have to obey; that’s how it is here.” (unnamed worker, 4 March 1969). The remark corroborates Tito’s admission of 1957 from the other side, but one unnamed person, chosen by an anti-regime paper and quoted by an anarchist author, cannot stand for all workers.

How the evidence has been read#

The institutional facts are not in serious dispute: the 1948 break, the 1950 law, the 1974 constitution and the 1961 Belgrade summit all happened. The argument concerns what self-management was in practice. Perlman’s contemporary pamphlet argues that it masked concentrated bureaucratic power, that students rather than workers began the 1968 revolt, and that university reform absorbed it (Birth of a Revolutionary Movement in Yugoslavia, 1969). Enver Hoxha’s party polemic concluded that self-management was capitalist in practice, a verdict Tito and the reformers rejected for different reasons (Hoxha, 1978). Madigan Fichter’s study of the revolt in Belgrade, Zagreb and Sarajevo is the principal recent scholarly treatment and should be set against Perlman, whose account is mainly of Belgrade (Slavic Review 75(1), 2016; full text not opened here). Branka Magaš takes the long view from the experiment to the wars that destroyed the federation, treating those wars as political outcomes rather than inevitabilities (The Destruction of Yugoslavia). Yugoslav social scientists debated the system in their own conferences, a check on reading it only through leaders and exiles (index of sources). The record supports Perlman’s doubts in part, because Tito conceded them, but supplies neither council minutes nor strike figures that would settle how often workers controlled production.

What the record cannot settle#

The founding declaration of the 1961 Non-Aligned summit could not be read in a usable text. Repository copies are image-only scans, so the summit’s existence and date are reported from Tito’s earlier statements and no line of the declaration is quoted here. The accessible testimony is skewed towards the leadership and towards Belgrade. Tito supplies most of the first-hand entries, one is a party ideologist, there is one unnamed worker reached through a hostile compilation and one student oral history recorded about fifty years afterwards. No factory-council minutes, ballot results, strike records or women workers’ accounts were opened; Zagreb appears only in passing and there is nothing first-hand from Sarajevo.

Almost nothing gives a direct voice to Bosnia or Kosovo. Tito’s speeches mention both as places of investment and as proof that the national question had been solved; those are leadership assertions, and the later wars make the silence a finding rather than a gap to fill by invention. The texts come through transcription and translation chains: volunteer HTML transcriptions of collected editions, a Serbo-Croatian exchange through Tanjug and French into English, and 1968 press excerpts with no translator named. Only the Olujić interview names its translator. The politics of each source must be stated when it is used, and the documents should not be averaged into a false balance. On dates, laws and institutional design they agree; the argument is over interpretation. A physical archive visit would be needed for the 1961 declaration in readable form, the original issues of Student and Susret, any council records, and testimony in the languages of the republics.
