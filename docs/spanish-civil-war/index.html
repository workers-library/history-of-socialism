Spain 1936–39: War, Revolution and the Retreat

In December 1936 George Orwell arrived in Barcelona to join a militia, a part-time citizen army rather than a regular force.

In December 1936 George Orwell arrived in Barcelona to join a militia, a part-time citizen army rather than a regular force. The English writer had gone to Spain to fight for the Republic against the military rising of that July. What he found was not simply a city preparing for war. “It was the first time that I had ever been in a town where the working class was in the saddle.” (George Orwell, Homage to Catalonia, 1938; Marxists Internet Archive: homage catalonia) Shops, transport and even bootblacks had been collectivised, meaning they were run collectively by the people who worked in them rather than by private owners. Tips and formal forms of address had disappeared, and red and black flags hung beside the Republican tricolour. Orwell had joined the militia of the Partido Obrero de Unificación Marxista (POUM), an independent Marxist party in Catalonia that believed the war and the social revolution had to be pursued together.

The government Orwell had come to defend was a Popular Front, a coalition of left and liberal parties and trade unions formed to oppose fascism. In July 1936 its best-known voice was Dolores Ibárruri, a leader of the Communist Party of Spain (PCE) and a Popular Front deputy, broadcasting from the Ministry of the Interior in Madrid on 19 July: “¡Viva el Frente Popular! ¡Viva la unión de todos los antifascistas! ¡Viva la República del pueblo! ¡Los fascistas no pasarán! ¡No pasarán!” (Marxists Internet Archive: jul 19) The Spanish original is given here; the English gloss, “Long live the Popular Front! Long live the union of all antifascists! Long live the Republic of the people! The fascists shall not pass! They shall not pass!”, is not a published translation. The appeal named workers, peasants, women and soldiers, and the closing words, “they shall not pass”, became the Republic’s watchword. A poster of the same weeks showed two men and a woman firing over a fallen comrade while reinforcements arrived, one man wearing the cap of the anarcho-syndicalist CNT-FAI federation, the other that of the socialist UGT union. The image made the alliance look like a single fighting force.

A coalition under fire#

The alliance was never as solid as its posters suggested. The military rising had split Spain, and in Toledo the Nationalists, as the rebel forces were called, held the Alcázar fortress. José Moscardó, the commander there, kept an official diary from 18 July to 28 September 1936 recording bombardments, casualty lists, requisitions of food, the mapping of a suspected mine and the dropping of supplies by rebel aircraft. On 23 July he noted, in restrained military language, that the commander of the Republican militias had telephoned to say he held Moscardó’s son and would shoot him unless the fortress surrendered. The relief of the garrison on 28 September made the siege a founding story of the Francoist cause, and later commemoration built a myth around an exchange the diary itself does not narrate in the famous terms. The log tells us what one officer recorded for his superiors; it says nothing about the disposition or losses of the besiegers.

Guernica#

On 26 April 1937 German and Italian aircraft flying for the Nationalists attacked Guernica, a small Basque market town well behind the front. Three reporters reached it independently and left accounts that corroborate one another in outline. Elizabeth Wilkinson of the Daily Worker was in the town before the raid: “Yesterday at about 1.30 pm I arrived in Guernica, the ancient capital of the Basque country. It was a peaceful town, with no factories, no munition works and no troops stationed there.” (Elizabeth Wilkinson, Daily Worker, 27 April 1937; Source: spartacus-educational.com) George Steer of The Times walked the ruins after midnight and again the following afternoon: “Guernica was not a military objective. A factory producing war material lay outside the town and was untouched. So were two barracks some distance from the town.” (George Steer, The Times, 28 April 1937; Source: web.viu.ca) Noel Monks of the Daily Express, the first reporter into the town, described what he found: “I saw 600 bodies. Nurses, children, farmers, old women, girls, old men, babies. All dead, torn, and mutilated.” (Noel Monks, Daily Express, 1 May 1937; Source: library.unr.edu) Monks’s figure was an estimate made at the scene, not a verified count, and the reporters’ politics shaped their emphasis. Their descriptions of fire, of machine-gunning, of refugees on the roads and of clergy blessing kneeling crowds agree closely enough to establish that the town was destroyed and that the news travelled worldwide within days.

The death toll remains contested. Steer declined to give a figure and wrote that deaths “may run into hundreds”; Wilkinson gave none. Xabier Irujo, working from Basque government records and thirty-eight contemporary witness statements, argues for a figure above 1,000, while Jesús Salas, drawing on a post-war Francoist municipal record, argues for roughly 120 to 200. These are incompatible claims built by different methods, and a single precise number would misrepresent them. Picasso, who had accepted a Republican commission for the 1937 Paris International Exhibition, made the political purpose of his mural explicit: “In the panel on which I am working which I shall call Guernica, and in all my recent works of art, I clearly express my abhorrence of the military caste which has sunk Spain in an ocean of pain and death…” (Pablo Picasso, 1937 statement, quoted in English translation by the Museo Reina Sofía from Alfred H. Barr Jr., Picasso: Fifty Years of His Art, 1946; Source: guernica.museoreinasofia.es)

The May Days and their aftermath#

In May 1937 the coalition turned on itself in Barcelona. The P.O.U.M.’s central committee, issuing a statement on 12 May days after the fighting, blamed the PSUC, the Unified Socialist Party of Catalonia, which had emerged from the merger of socialists and Communists in the region: “The May Days were the direct and immediate consequence of a monstrous provocation by the PSUC.” (P.O.U.M. Central Committee, 12 May 1937, translated by John Sullivan; Marxists Internet Archive: maydays) The statement described police trying to seize the Telefónica building and workers raising barricades across the city, and it insisted the party had sought to channel a spontaneous movement rather than launch a coup. That was a defence written by a party about to be repressed, and it cannot settle who fired first. Orwell, who was in the city and was wounded that May, doubted that any balanced account was possible: “It will never be possible to get a completely accurate and unbiased account of the Barcelona fighting, because the necessary records do not exist.” (George Orwell, chapter XI, 1938; Marxists Internet Archive: homage catalonia)

Among those removed was Andrés Nin, formerly the P.O.U.M.’s political secretary. Arrested in Barcelona on 16 June 1937, he left a final statement in police custody in Madrid on 21 June, recorded by his captors and surviving in translation: “He repeats himself once again, that that has nothing to do with the subject of espionage that he has been charged with.” (Andrés Nin, police declaration, 21 June 1937, translated by Louise McTavish; Marxists Internet Archive: final declaration) Sources differ on the date of his disappearance, and editorial notes in the cited archive express a partisan view. The statement proves his detention, not the fate that the archive’s editors assign to him.

Thousands of foreigners had come to fight for the Republic in the International Brigades, volunteer formations organised by the Communist International. Alvah Bessie, an American volunteer in the Abraham Lincoln Battalion, counted what remained after the Ebro fighting: “The Brigade went in with about two thousand men, came out with thirteen hundred; the Lincoln Battalion went in with about five hundred, and now we had about a hundred twenty.” (Alvah Bessie, Men in Battle, 1939; Source: spartacus-educational.com) In October 1938 the Republic withdrew the Brigades in a failed attempt to secure the withdrawal of Axis forces, and Ibárruri thanked them in a speech whose date is given variously as 28 October, 1 November and 15 November: “You can go proudly. You are history. You are legend. You are the heroic example of democracy’s solidarity and universality” (English translation reproduced by spanishsky.dk; Source: spanishsky.dk) The public celebration of international solidarity sat alongside the imprisonment of the Republic’s internal critics.

The retreat#

When the Republic collapsed in January and February 1939, roughly half a million people fled across the Pyrenees, an exodus known as the retirada. Madeleine Morena was six when her family left in January 1939: “It was bitterly cold and there was snow. We had to abandon our belongings, we couldn’t carry them.” (Madeleine Morena, interviewed in 2019; Source: r.jina.ai) Antonio de la Fuente y Ferraz, then nine, crossed at Puigcerdà in February and spent four years in French camps, moving through seven of them. The beach camps at Argelès and Saint-Cyprien held families separated at the border. These memories, recorded eighty years later, establish what the flight felt like to those who survived it, not its scale or exact sequence.

How historians have read it#

  • Hugh Thomas, The Spanish Civil War (1961, repeatedly revised). The long-standing general narrative, strong on international diplomacy and the mechanics of the Brigades. Later historians have revised its casualty and deployment figures, so it serves as a framework rather than a final authority. Internet Archive snapshot: international brigades spain
  • Pierre Broué, “The ‘May Days’ of 1937 in Barcelona,” Revolutionary History 1(2), 1988. A Trotskyist reading through the idea of dual power, arguing that a revolutionary opportunity was lost. It agrees with Orwell and the P.O.U.M. statement on the importance of the Telefónica and rejects that party’s own upbeat conclusion of 6 May. Marxists Internet Archive: spain04
  • Xabier Irujo Ametzaga, El Gernika de Richthofen (2012) and summary article (2013). Argues from thousands of documents in six countries’ archives that Guernica was a deliberate terror-bombing experiment, and rejects the low casualty figures derived from Francoist reports. A strongly argued position, not settled consensus. Source: albavolunteer.org
  • Danny Evans, “A Pile of Ruins? Pierre van Paassen and the Mythical Durruti” (2022). A source-critical study showing that the most famous anarchist interview of the war was probably invented. It does not deny Durruti’s importance; it shows how a romantic fabrication came to stand for real practice. Source: theanarchistlibrary.org
  • Mary Nash, “Federica Montseny: dirigente anarquista, feminista y ministra,” Arenal 1(2), 1994. Uses Montseny’s own writings to examine the anarchist who rejected feminism yet became the first woman minister in western Europe, a dimension the male memoirists omit. Source: revistaseug.ugr.es

There is broad agreement on the chronology, the fact of the Guernica bombing and the formation and withdrawal of the Brigades. Live disagreement concerns the Guernica death toll, responsibility for the May Days, whether a revolutionary alternative was viable in 1937, and how much weight to give to much-repeated testimony whose authenticity has been challenged.

What the record cannot show#

The strongest English-speaking testimony comes from a narrow group: foreign journalists, a volunteer, party leaders and one officer. Even Orwell, the most intimate of these witnesses, arrived in December 1936 and saw no major battle. The record is thin for Andalusia, Extremadura, the rural Basque population and the Republican soldiers who were neither Communists nor anarchists. Nationalist voices are almost absent apart from Moscardó’s official diary, which cannot stand for the rebel side as a whole. The reported words of women come chiefly through a poster and refugee memories, not through their own sustained testimony.

Language narrows what is available. The decisive material is largely in Spanish, Catalan and Basque, with Russian and German records for Soviet and air-force involvement. The Nin declaration, the Ibárruri farewell and the Picasso statement all reach the reader here in English translations whose originals were not examined line by line. Several source families remain outside this account: Montseny’s 1937 ministry lecture, the contemporary party press, the photograph archives of Agustí Centelles and Robert Capa, and Joris Ivens’s film The Spanish Earth. Famous images from the war have contested dates or authorship, and the van Paassen “Durruti interview” is widely reprinted but probably fabricated, so it is not quoted here. The Guernica death toll and the May Days casualty figures remain disputed, and much surviving testimony was shaped by the parties that preserved it. Distinguishing what a witness saw from what a movement later made of it requires archival work in Spanish and Catalan, not more published memoirs.
