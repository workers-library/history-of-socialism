Frantz Fanon: Colonial Violence, Liberation and the National Bourgeoisie

A research profile of the psychiatrist and anti-colonial writer's account of colonial domination, its psychological effects and the pitfalls of national liberation.

Frantz Fanon wrote about what colonial rule did to people and about what survived its end. He argued that colonial domination rests on force from the beginning, that the struggle to end it is itself violent, and that a local elite can take over the colonial economy after the flags change. By colonial violence he meant the force that sets up and holds together rule over another people, from conquest to policing, not only the fighting that brings that rule to an end. By the national bourgeoisie he meant the local class of business owners, officials and intermediaries who step into the positions settlers left behind after independence. His writing on the native intellectual and on that class made him a lasting point of reference in anti-colonial debate. This profile sets out those arguments from his own texts, records what his publishers and libraries establish about the books, and reports the objections made against him.

Biography#

The chapter on Empire and Underdevelopment already covers Fanon’s claim that the national middle class becomes a “transmission line between the nation and a capitalism, rampant though camouflaged, which today puts on the masque of neocolonialism”, and notes that his examples were his own selections; this profile adds his arguments about violence, the native intellectual, the internal structure of that class critique and what he thought should replace colonial rule, without retelling the history of decolonisation.

Grove Atlantic’s own author note, repeated on its pages for all four of his books, says Fanon was born in Martinique in 1925, served in the French Army during the Second World War, studied medicine and psychiatry in France, published his first book Black Skin, White Masks in 1952, joined the Algerian Nationalist Movement in the mid-1950s, and published The Wretched of the Earth shortly before dying of leukaemia in December 1961 (Grove Atlantic, product pages). Éditions La Découverte, which now issues the French editions first published by François Maspero, describes him as “né antillais et mort algérien (1925–1961)”, a psychiatrist and FLN militant who died at 36 (La Découverte). The Bibliothèque nationale de France authority heading on its catalogue records gives the years 1925–1961 and the identifier 12016577.

The exact day and month of his birth, and the place and date of his death, were not verified from any primary record opened for this profile; the publisher’s phrase “December 1961” is the most precise death date confirmed. No institutional record of his medical training, his post at the Blida psychiatric hospital, his expulsion from Algeria or his diplomatic posting was opened, so none of those episodes is asserted here. The two books dated 1952 and 1961 and the FLN connection rest on the publisher and library records above.

Intellectual tradition#

Fanon wrote as a psychiatrist and a political theorist, and the two roles are hard to separate in the texts. In the opening chapter of Black Skin, White Masks, as reprinted by Grove Atlantic in Richard Philcox’s translation, he moves between clinical observation, philosophy and politics, citing Césaire, Sartre’s Black Orpheus, the anthropologist Westermann, Michel Leiris, André Breton, Piaget and the linguists Damourette and Pichon, and saying he had read Cheikh Anta Diop’s linguistic work “with great interest”. He treats Sartre’s claim that the black poet turns against the French language as a position he partly accepts and partly qualifies for Antilleans (Grove Atlantic, Black Skin, White Masks).

The preface Sartre later wrote for Les damnés de la terre is itself a primary theoretical text. It reads Fanon as an “ex-native” who addresses the colonised rather than Europeans, and argues that “in order to triumph, the national revolution must be socialist” (Sartre, preface, 1961). That sentence is Sartre’s formulation of what he took Fanon to be arguing, not a sentence Fanon wrote.

His relation to Marxism was contested even among Marxists. The University of Cape Town thesis discussed below reports Jack Woddis’s 1977 complaint that Fanon “nowhere shows any understanding of Marxism” and that he downgraded the industrial working class; the same thesis judges Woddis too harsh and notes that Fanon’s account of the working class was undeveloped. Fanon himself is explicit that he wants to build on European thought rather than discard it: in the concluding chapter of The Wretched of the Earth he writes that “all the elements of a solution to the great problems of humanity have, at different times, existed in European thought”, while adding that Europeans “have not carried out in practice the mission that fell to them” (Fanon, “Conclusion”).

He also separated national consciousness from nationalism outright: “National consciousness, which is not nationalism, is the only thing that will give us an international dimension.” That distinction is the clearest instance of a label he refused. The 2021 Grove edition replaced Sartre’s 1961 preface with a preface by Homi K. Bhabha and an introduction by Cornel West, which shows how the publisher’s framing of his tradition has changed; the change is a fact of the publisher’s record, not a judgement on either reading (Grove Atlantic, The Wretched of the Earth).

Major contributions#

Three arguments recur. The first is that colonial rule is violence from the start and that decolonisation, because it replaces one ordering of people by another, is violent too. The Grove Atlantic excerpt from “On Violence”, in Philcox’s translation, opens: “decolonization is always a violent event”, a “substitution of one ‘species’ of mankind by another” that is “unconditional, absolute, total, and seamless”. Fanon insists this is a historical process rather than a natural event, and says “the ‘thing’ colonized becomes a man through the very process of liberation” (Grove Atlantic, The Wretched of the Earth). The chapter “Colonial War and Mental Disorders” is reported, in the scholarly article discussed below, as a clinical account of the psychological damage of the Algerian war; that chapter was not opened directly here.

The second contribution is the analysis of the native intellectual. In his 1959 speech “National Culture and the Fight for Freedom”, reproduced by the Marxists Internet Archive, Fanon describes two failed responses: the intellectual who “throws himself in frenzied fashion into the frantic acquisition of the culture of the occupying power” and the one who “takes refuge in setting out and substantiating the claims of that culture in a way that is passionate but rapidly becomes unproductive”. He calls both the “turncoat” and the “substantialist” “ineffectual”. The healthy development he describes is the writer who stops addressing the oppressor and begins “addressing his own people”, producing “a literature of combat”. Culture cannot be revived inside colonial rule: “the condition for its existence is therefore national liberation and the renaissance of the state” (Fanon, “National Culture and the Fight for Freedom”, 1959).

The third is the critique of the national bourgeoisie in chapter 3 of The Wretched of the Earth, “The Pitfalls of National Consciousness”. Fanon describes a class with “practically no economic power”, “completely canalized into activities of the intermediary type”, whose “historic mission” is that of “intermediary”. Its “mission has nothing to do with transforming the nation; it consists, prosaically, of being the transmission line between the nation and a capitalism, rampant though camouflaged, which today puts on the masque of neocolonialism”. He extends the argument beyond commerce: nationalisation becomes the transfer of colonial advantages into native hands; the landed proprietors intensify the exploitation of agricultural workers; tourism turns the country into, in his phrase, “the brothel of Europe”; the single party becomes “the modern form of the dictatorship of the bourgeoisie”; and the leader who embodied independence is turned by the same process into “the general president of that company of profiteers”. His conclusion is political: “African unity can only be achieved through the upward thrust of the people, and under the leadership of the people, that is to say, in defiance of the interests of the bourgeoisie” (Fanon, “The Pitfalls of National Consciousness”). These quotations come from the transcription on marxists.org, whose header dates the French source to Maspero in 1961 and does not name its translator; the wording matches the English text already used in the book’s chapter, but the translator was not confirmed on the page itself. No page numbers are given here.

Analysis of capitalism#

Fanon did not write a theory of the capitalist firm or of surplus value. His account of capitalism is an account of what colonial rule does and what survives it. In “On Violence” the colonist “derives his validity, i.e., his wealth, from the colonial system”, and the relation between colonist and colonised is maintained “at the point of the bayonet and under cannon fire” (Grove Atlantic, The Wretched of the Earth).

In “The Pitfalls of National Consciousness” the mechanism after independence is financial and commercial. The new class occupies the trading houses and commercial firms left by settlers, acts as the “business agent” of Western capital, reinvests little, exports profits, and sees no reason to transform agricultural methods or build industry. The state balances its budget “through loans and gifts”, while the former colonial power “accumulates concessions and guarantees”. The policeman, the soldier and foreign experts hold the system together. This is a polemical diagnosis, not a measurement of capital flows, and the book’s existing chapter correctly treats it as an argument rather than as data. The nationalisation he condemns is not nationalisation as public ownership: for that class, he writes, it “quite simply means the transfer into native hands of those unfair advantages which are a legacy of the colonial period”. His standard for an authentic national middle class is that it “repudiate its own nature in so far as it is bourgeois” and put its education “at the people’s disposal” (Fanon, “The Pitfalls of National Consciousness”).

Analysis of socialism#

Fanon wrote no blueprint of socialist institutions. He says explicitly that “we need a model, and that we want blueprints and examples”, but rejects the European model and warns that “we have no more to fear; so let us stop envying her”. What he offers is a set of requirements: no imitation of Europe; no catch-up at the cost of human beings — “No, we do not want to catch up with anyone”; an internationalism built through national consciousness rather than against it; and a political process in which the people lead. The last chapter’s formulation is that “for Europe, for ourselves and for humanity, comrades, we must turn over a new leaf, we must work out new concepts, and try to set afoot a new man” (Fanon, “Conclusion”). Sartre’s preface goes further than Fanon’s own text in naming the outcome, arguing that the national revolution must be socialist or the new state will remain in imperialist hands.

The South African thesis notes that Fanon’s “incomplete work” left the working-class question underdeveloped and that Woddis thought he gave the peasantry and urban poor a role orthodox Marxism reserved for the proletariat. The honest summary is that Fanon treated socialism as the popular and internationalist content of decolonisation and as a break with the bourgeois national state, and did not specify ownership, planning or party forms.

Recommended reading#

These selections are starting points into Fanon’s argument, not a survey of everything he wrote.

  • Les damnés de la terre (1961). It sets out his central claim that colonial rule rests on force from the beginning and that decolonisation is violent too, which makes it the shortest route into his argument.
  • “The Pitfalls of National Consciousness” (1961). It describes the local business class as an intermediary that takes over the colonial economy without transforming it, the clearest statement of what he thought independence could fail to achieve.
  • “National Culture and the Fight for Freedom” (1959). It contrasts the intellectual who adopts the coloniser’s culture with the writer who addresses his own people, giving the quickest access to his argument about culture and national liberation.
  • “Conclusion” (1961). It states his requirements for what should replace colonial rule — no imitation of Europe, national consciousness as a route to internationalism and a process led by the people — making it the best guide to his positive argument.

Neither is a verified recording, and the Bloomsbury page returned an access error when opened, so the contents of the speech were not read directly here.

Intellectual influences#

The influences that can be named from the primary texts themselves are Césaire’s poetry and politics, Sartre’s existentialist writing on black identity, the psychiatrist Octave Mannoni’s “Prospero complex”, which Fanon discusses critically, European anthropology and psychology, and the French language itself as the medium he both used and interrogated. In the Conclusion he claims European thought for the “great problems of humanity” while rejecting European imitation.

Sartre is an interpreter rather than an influence that can be proved from Fanon’s own words, though Fanon quotes Sartre’s Black Orpheus in Black Skin, White Masks. The Marxists Internet Archive places Nkrumah’s “African Socialism Revisited” (1967) and Kenan Malik’s “All Cultures are Not Equal” (2002) under “Further Reading” on its Fanon page; neither was opened for this dossier as evidence of influence. Claims that Lacan or Freud shaped his psychiatric work were not verified from a primary source opened here beyond the general psychoanalytic vocabulary visible in the Black Skin, White Masks excerpt.

Influence and legacy#

The publisher’s own anniversary description of The Wretched of the Earth says the book remains an influence on movements from Black Lives Matter to decolonisation and on civil rights, anti-colonialism and Black consciousness; that is a publisher’s claim and is tagged here as such. Grove also prints a New Yorker review by Pankaj Mishra naming Nadine Gordimer, Ayi Kwei Armah, Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o, Édouard Glissant and Walter Rodney among writers who read the book as a diagnosis of the post-colonial condition, and a Boston Globe line about Malcolm X, Eldridge Cleaver, Amiri Baraka and the Black Panther leaders. These are quoted judgements in a publisher’s marketing copy, not independent verification of influence.

Scholarly evidence of legacy is stronger. A search-result snippet for the 2025 ROAPE article reports that Fanon was cited in the first editorial of Review of African Political Economy in 1974, and Moodley’s 2020 thesis tests his predictions against later decolonisation and finds that they were “frequently correct”. The book’s existing chapter links Fanon to Nkrumah, Sankara, Cabral, Rodney and the Algerian War; this profile reuses those cross-references, including Cabral and Rodney and The Algerian War, rather than retelling those histories.

Criticisms and controversies#

The longest-running objection concerns violence. Hannah Arendt’s On Violence (1970), as quoted in Md. Ishrat Ibne Ismail’s 2019 article, argues that violence “is ruled by means-end reasoning” and that “the most probable change it will bring about is the change to a more violent world”; elsewhere she writes that violence “does not promote causes, neither history nor revolution, neither progress nor reaction”. Arendt also singled out Sartre’s preface: of “to shoot down a European is to kill two birds with one stone”, she wrote, “This is a sentence that Marx could never have written.” Ismail’s answer is that Arendt “is unfair to Fanon because she is picking up violence by definition not by context”, and he gathers other objections: Jean Améry’s charge that Fanon “has neglected to specify what actually happens when passive violence becomes active” and never explains why revolutionary violence is redemptive; Frazer and Hutchings’s point that Fanon’s own representations of perpetrators and victims “do not suggest this comfortable conclusion”; and Seshadri-Crooks’s observation that he distinguishes spontaneous rage from organised action. All of these were read in Ismail’s article, not in the original works, and are attributed accordingly. Benda Hofmeyr’s 2025 article in African Studies, written at the University of Pretoria, is described in its abstract as examining why Fanon has been called “the apostle of violence” and why his insistence that violence is necessary for anti-colonial freedom has been both praised and criticised; the full text was blocked, so no argument beyond that abstract is reported.

The Marxist objection is different. As reported in Moodley’s 2020 thesis, Woddis’s New Theories of Revolution (1977) accused Fanon of downgrading the role of workers, claimed he gave the peasantry and lumpenproletariat the revolutionary role, and said he “nowhere shows any understanding of Marxism”. Moodley replies that there is “some accuracy” to the charge but that Woddis was “perhaps too harsh”, and notes that Woddis himself admitted Fanon’s account of the working class was undeveloped and might have improved had he lived longer. Moodley’s own thesis is a critical use of Fanon rather than a rejection: it argues that he perceived problems with national liberation while they were still in their infancy and that his predictions were frequently correct, while supplementing him with Marxism and psychoanalysis to analyse nationalism, the national bourgeoisie, racism, consumerism and political “reification”.

Matthew Quest’s 2005 article in The CLR James Journal revisits why the national bourgeoisie has not been smashed and argues that its responsibility for postcolonial governance should be re-examined; only its title, metadata and a short abstract snippet were available. A 2025 article in Review of African Political Economy edited by Chinedu Chukwudinma, Christopher J. Lee and Bettina Engels is titled “Frantz Fanon at 100: class struggle and the future of African liberation”; a search-result snippet for an article of that title says he warned that decolonisation would become a curse if it passed to a national bourgeoisie willing to betray the people and cooperate with old and new imperialism. The full article was not opened. Hourya Bentouhami-Molino and Lewis Gordon published “Fanon, critique du ‘fétichisme méthodologique’” in Actuel Marx in 2014; only the metadata was opened. Kenan Malik’s 2002 essay “All cultures are not equal”, reproduced on marxists.org, argues that Fanon and C. L. R. James were Enlightenment universalists whose work is misread by contemporary cultural relativists. It was originally an opinion piece and is listed here only as a pointer, not as evidence.

Two editorial controversies belong in the record. Sartre’s preface, which the BnF record confirms was attached to the 1961 French edition, became the main text through which English-language readers encountered Fanon and became a target of Arendt’s criticism; the 2021 Grove edition dropped it in favour of Bhabha and West. Second, the English publication dates of A Dying Colonialism and Toward the African Revolution are given inconsistently across catalogues, as the bibliography records. Neither dispute is settled here.

Related thinkers#

Sartre is the closest interpreter and also a critic’s target; Césaire supplies the poetic and political background that Fanon quotes, and Aimé Césaire’s Notebook of a Return to My Native Land is discussed in the Black Skin, White Masks excerpt. Mannoni is the object of Fanon’s criticism in Black Skin, White Masks; Arendt, Woddis, Hofmeyr, Moodley, Ismail, Quest, Mbembe and Malik are critics or interpreters. Nkrumah, Cabral, Rodney and Sankara share the problem of what survives independence and are already covered in the book’s historical chapters, including Empire and Underdevelopment, Cabral and Rodney and The Algerian War. No published profile exists yet in the Authors and Intellectual Traditions collection to link to, so the chapter pages are the available cross-references; the collection index is Authors and Intellectual Traditions.
