The War Inside: Sendero Luminoso and Peru, 1980–2000

On 17 May 1980 members of the Partido Comunista del Perú burned ballot boxes at Chuschi, a highland town, and began a war against the state that lasted twenty years.

On 17 May 1980 members of the Partido Comunista del Perú burned ballot boxes at Chuschi, a highland town, and began a war against the state that lasted twenty years (CVR, Informe Final, 2003). The party was better known by its other name, Sendero Luminoso, or Shining Path. Its own documents set out a doctrine that historians call Maoism, after Mao Zedong, and that the party called Marxism-Leninism-Maoism. It claimed that “the scientific ideology of the proletariat, all-powerful because it is true, has three stages or landmarks in its dialectical process of development” (Communist Party of Peru, “On Marxism-Leninism-Maoism”, 1988, in Fundamental Documents, an English translation whose translator is not named; Foreign Languages Press). The claim was that Mao had completed what Marx and Lenin had started. Carlos Iván Degregori traced the party’s first cadres to a free-education movement of 1969 in Ayacucho and to regional inequality, with the university and the teaching profession as the main recruiting ground. The war that followed was fought chiefly in the countryside. Peru’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission, the CVR, found that 79 percent of reported victims lived in rural areas and that 75 percent spoke Quechua or another native language as their mother tongue, against 16 percent of the population. Quechua is an Indigenous language of the Andes. Those percentages give the conflict its shape: the people who bore it were mostly poor and Indigenous.

In the highlands the two sides fought through villages. Sendero had governed parts of Ayacucho through surveillance and compulsory attendance; at Lucanamarca, after local people killed party cadres, the party killed community members on 3 April 1983. The CVR’s own chapter gives two different totals, 69 in one paragraph and 67 in another. Abimael Guzmán, the party’s leader, put the number higher and described the killing in an interview of July 1988: “ahí fueron aniquilados más de 80, eso es lo real; y lo decimos, ahí hubo exceso” (there more than 80 were wiped out, that is the reality; and we say it, there was an excess there, my rendering). The words are the commission’s transcription of a newspaper interview, not the newspaper itself (Abimael Guzmán, interview with El Diario, Lima, July 1988, transcribed in the CVR’s Lucanamarca chapter; CVR final report, Tomo V). It is unusual for a movement’s leader to concede in his own words that an action went beyond what he would defend, though Guzmán framed the attack as a necessary blow with an unfortunate side effect.

At Uchuraccay, on 26 January 1983, eight journalists, their guide and a community member were killed. A commission set up that year explained the killings as a cultural misunderstanding; it had no Quechua speaker, visited once and never published its assembly transcript. The CVR later called this the “indigenista paradigm”. Its own account uses twenty-one testimonies and a list, compiled in two assemblies in 2002, naming 135 residents of Uchuraccay killed in the wider violence. Saturnina Figueroa, widow of Severino Morales, told the CVR in February 2002: “Si me matan, mátenme con mis hijos, sino quién los va a criar. ¡Traigan a todos mis hijos y mátennos!” (if they kill me, kill me with my children, otherwise who will raise them? Bring all my children and kill us too, my rendering). Her words were spoken nineteen years after the event, and the report does not say whether they were translated from Quechua or by whom (Saturnina Figueroa, interviewed at Uchuraccay 9 February 2002, quoted in the CVR’s Uchuraccay chapter; CVR final report, Tomo V). A local Sendero commander using the name “Suni Puni” wrote in March 1985 that “Desde 1981, Uchuraccay, Iquicha y Huaychao eran bases de apoyo donde existían ya pelotones de la fuerza local y unidades guerrilleras.” (since 1981 Uchuraccay, Iquicha and Huaychao were support bases where local defence squads and guerrilla units already existed, my rendering). The manuscript survives in the commission’s quotation, not in an original opened here (“Suni Puni”, Plumas y montañas, March 1985, quoted in the CVR’s Uchuraccay chapter; same URL).

Some communities armed themselves. The armed committees of self-defence formed from 1982 onward, received legal recognition only in November 1991, and contributed to Sendero’s military defeat. A former commander in Vinchos, Ayacucho, gave the CVR the cost of that victory: “Nosotros somos el defensor de la paz, ganamos a El PCP-SL y ahora estamos en una pobreza. El Estado se ha olvidado de nosotros” (we are the defenders of peace, we defeated the PCP-SL and now we live in poverty; the state has forgotten us, my rendering). His interview date is not recorded, and a commander speaking after the war has reason to stress both the defence and the neglect (former commander of a Comité de Autodefensa, Vinchos, Ayacucho, quoted in the CVR’s chapter on the committees; CVR final report, Tomo II). These armed anti-subversive committees are not the same as the unarmed northern rondas campesinas, which Orin Starn treats as a peasant justice movement; the two are often merged in retellings. In the forest, among Asháninka communities of the Tambo river, Sendero ran compulsory agricultural work, centralised food distribution, schools for children of eight or ten and militia drill. A resident of Quempiri recalled: “sopa aguada nomás, hoja de chalanca nomás comían, tierra, hasta culebra” (they ate only thin soup, only chalanca leaves, earth, even snakes, my rendering). The interview was recorded in Spanish translation, and the translator is not named in the passage (resident of Quempiri, CVR in-depth interview P737, quoted in the CVR’s Asháninka chapter; CVR final report, Tomo V).

The state’s own crimes were not confined to the counter-insurgency in the hills. The CVR documents sexual violence by soldiers, police and rebels. Dionisio Pariona Ventura told a public hearing: “Salían de patrulla al campo y violaban a las mujeres casadas en presencia de sus esposos.” (they went out on patrol into the countryside and raped married women in the presence of their husbands, my rendering) (Dionisio Pariona Ventura, CVR public hearing in Huanta, case 17, third session, 12 April 2002, quoted in the CVR’s chapter on sexual violence; CVR final report, Tomo VI). Many such statements are identified only by number, and the commission itself notes the difficulty of proof. In Lima on 3 November 1991 masked armed men entered a courtyard during a fundraising gathering and shot fifteen people, including an eight-year-old boy, wounding four more. Alfonso Rodas Alvitres, a survivor, recalled: “Saltó otro señor que dijo: yo soy el que organiza esto y conmigo háganlo. A él lo acribillaron a balazos.” (another man jumped up and said: I am the one who organized this, do it to me. They riddled him with bullets, my rendering) (Alfonso Rodas Alvitres, CVR public hearing in Lima, case 17, third session, 22 June 2002; CVR case 2.45, Barrios Altos). The commission linked the killings to army intelligence and to later amnesty laws that blocked prosecution. Between the night of 17 July and the early hours of 18 July 1992, soldiers seized nine students and a professor from the university residences of La Cantuta in front of about fifty witnesses. Military authorities denied the raid and habeas corpus petitions failed. A student who was a Sendero member detained at Santa Monica described a pact among the women: “Si a ti te dicen que te pongas a un lado, todas vamos y si te quieren llevarte a ti sola, no, vamos todas” (if they tell you to step aside, we all go; and if they want to take you alone, no, we all go, my rendering). The interview was recorded around 2002, and the speaker’s prison context may shape what she chose to emphasise (student at La Cantuta, 1989–92, CVR in-depth interview P342; CVR final report, Tomo V). Relatives searched for the bodies. Carmen Rosa Amaro-Cóndor, sister of one of the disappeared, stated in an affidavit: “They made him disappear completely; they burned his body with lime, with gasoline, that shows their complete inhumanity.” Gisela Ortiz-Pérez, sister of another, told the Court’s hearing that “[t]hey scratch[ed] the ground trying to uncover the truth hidden there.” The brackets are the judgment’s own (Carmen Rosa Amaro-Cóndor and Gisela Ortiz-Pérez, affidavit and testimony summarised in La Cantuta v. Perú, Inter-American Court of Human Rights, 29 November 2006; judgment). In Barrios Altos v. Peru of 14 March 2001 the Court found violations of the rights to life and humane treatment and of judicial protection, after Peru accepted international responsibility (judgment). These are legal findings about the state, not verdicts on named officers in a domestic court.

The number of dead is still argued over. The CVR’s conclusion reads: “La CVR estima que la cifra más probable de víctimas fatales de la violencia es de 69,280 personas.” (the CVR estimates the most likely number of fatal victims of the violence at 69,280, my rendering) (Comisión de la Verdad y Reconciliación, Conclusiones generales, 2003; CVR final report, Tomo VIII). That estimate carries a 95 percent confidence interval of 61,007 to 77,552 and attributes 54 percent of reported fatal victims to Sendero. Silvio Rendón reanalysed the capture-recapture method in 2019 and proposed about 48,000 killings with a larger share for the state; Daniel Manrique-Vallier and Patrick Ball replied that his estimate of Sendero killings was lower than the observed data and therefore impossible, and that his method suffered from selection bias. Both exchanges were peer reviewed, and they do not end in agreement. The CVR figure remains the standard one. It is an estimate with a method, not a count.

Broad agreement holds that Sendero began the war, that rural Quechua communities were its principal victims, and that the state committed serious crimes in counter-insurgency. Disagreement continues over the total death toll, the shares of responsibility and how much the 1983 “cultural” explanation of Uchuraccay should be trusted.

Limits of the record#

Almost all the testimony quoted here was recorded twenty years or more after the events, by a commission that could not compel evidence and worked through translators. The report identifies many witnesses only by number, so their other statements cannot be checked and no follow-up is possible. The record is strongest for people who survived and chose to speak. It is thin for those who fled, for ordinary Sendero members after the war and for soldiers and police below the rank of those who later cooperated with prosecutors. The commission’s nine volumes are a synthesis, not the archive of 17,000 statements, which is now held elsewhere; the court judgments summarise testimony rather than reproduce transcripts. Sendero’s documents are available here only in an English translation with no named translator, and the original Spanish party series and the “Suni Puni” manuscript are not reproduced. The 1983 Uchuraccay report is known through the CVR’s account of it, and no individual photograph from the Yuyanapaq collection was examined. Quechua and Asháninka translation would be needed for work in local archives, and the forest regions are under-represented in every source used. No source can recover a private conversation or what a person believed but never told a commission. The honest account stops where the record stops.
