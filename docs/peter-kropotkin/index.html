Peter Kropotkin: Mutual Aid and Anarchist Communism

A research profile of the geographer and anarchist's account of cooperation, decentralised federation and the critique of the state.

Peter Kropotkin made two connected arguments: that cooperation is a force in evolution, and that socialism cannot be built through the state. By mutual aid he meant practical support between members of the same species in the struggle against harsh physical conditions, which he set against the competition for scarce resources that many Darwinists treated as the main fact of evolution. “Sociability is as much a law of nature as mutual struggle”, he wrote, and “those animals which acquire habits of mutual aid are undoubtedly the fittest” (Gould 1988 [Scholarly Secondary, quoting Mutual Aid]). By anarchist communism he meant what he called “the no-government system of socialism”: a society of free groups and communes joined by agreement rather than law, with land and capital held in common and goods enjoyed according to need (MIA, “Anarchist Communism: Its Basis and Principles” [Primary Theoretical]). This profile sets out those arguments in his own words, records the objections made by Marxists, scientists and other anarchists, and marks where the evidence is thin.

Biography#

An Internet Archive catalogue record gives the subject heading “Kropotkin, Petr Alekseevich, kniaz, 1842-1921” (Internet Archive, The anarchist prince [Primary Official, catalogue metadata]). The Marxists Internet Archive index gives the fuller dates 9 December 1842 and 8 February 1921, but its biographical paragraph is attributed to “a tertiary encyclopedia”, so the exact days are not verified here and are not used as evidence under the book’s editorial source policy (MIA, Kropotkin archive index [directory, not evidence]). The scholarly biography identified through the catalogue is George Woodcock and Ivan Avakumovic, The Anarchist Prince: A Biographical Study of Peter Kropotkin, New York: Schocken Books, 1971, with a bibliography at pages 455–459. The scan is access-restricted and its argument was not read.

His own memoir is the best first-person source for the early years. The MIA edition is arranged in six parts: childhood, the Corps of Pages, Siberia, St Petersburg and a first journey to Western Europe, the fortress and the escape, and Western Europe (MIA, Memoirs of a Revolutionist, table of contents [Primary Theoretical]). The transcription carries inconsistent publication metadata: it labels the work “Written: 1899” but names In Russian and French Prisons, Ward and Downey, 1887, as its source. Catalogue records place Memoirs of a Revolutionist in London with Smith, Elder and in Boston and New York with Houghton, Mifflin in 1899, with later Swan Sonnenschein editions in 1906 and 1908 and a Dover edition in 1971 (Internet Archive catalogue search [Primary Official, catalogue metadata]). One catalogue entry dated 1916 is titled Memoirs of a revolutionary; the modern title has some bibliographic support, but the standard English text used here is the 1899 Memoirs of a Revolutionist.

Kropotkin described his scientific formation himself. In the introduction to Mutual Aid he recalled two impressions from his youth in Eastern Siberia and Northern Manchuria: the severity of the struggle against an inclement climate, and his failure to find “that bitter struggle for the means of existence, among animals belonging to the same species, which was considered by most Darwinists (though not always by Darwin himself) as the dominant characteristic of struggle for life, and the main factor of evolution” (MIA, Mutual Aid, Introduction [Primary Theoretical]). Gould, using Daniel Todes’s study of Russian evolutionary thought, places those years at 1862–1866 and says Kropotkin went as a military officer but used the commission to study geology, geography and zoology (Gould 1988 [Scholarly Secondary]). Kropotkin dated the origin of his theory more precisely: a lecture “On the Law of Mutual Aid” given by the zoologist Kessler at a Russian Congress of Naturalists in January 1880 gave him the idea, and he began collecting material after encountering it in 1883; Kessler died in 1881 (MIA, Mutual Aid, Introduction [Primary Theoretical]).

The political chronology is less fully verified. The headings of the Memoirs confirm that he wrote about the fortress, the escape and Western Europe, but the individual sections were not opened, so no detail from them is asserted here. The MIA index lists an 1874 imprisonment and a 1876 escape in its tertiary-derived summary; those dates are not independently confirmed in the research dossier. What his own text establishes is that by the 1880s he was writing in exile and using English and French periodicals. The preface to Fields, Factories and Workshops is signed “P. Kropotkin. Bromley, Kent., 1898” (Gutenberg, Fields, Factories and Workshops, 1913 edition, Preface [Primary Theoretical, seen as a search-result extract]), and the introduction to Mutual Aid is signed “Bromley, Kent, 1902”.

After the October Revolution he returned to Russia and lived at Dmitrov. In March and December 1920 he wrote to Lenin about the desperate condition of postal and telegraph employees, the failures of the soviets, and the Soviet government’s threat to take and execute hostages (MIA, “Two letters to Lenin”, 1920 [Primary Theoretical]). The MIA index dates his last letter to January 1921 and its tertiary-derived summary gives 8 February 1921 as his death date; the day is not verified from an institutional record opened for this profile.

Intellectual tradition#

Kropotkin was an anarchist communist, and the label was his own. In his 1910 article “Anarchism” for the Encyclopaedia Britannica he defined the term as “a principle or theory of life and conduct under which society is conceived without government – harmony in such a society being obtained, not by submission to law, or by obedience to any authority, but by free agreements concluded between the various groups, territorial and professional, freely constituted” (MIA, “Anarchism”, Encyclopaedia Britannica, 1910 [Primary Theoretical: a first-party article published in an encyclopedia, not a tertiary account of him]). This is a first-party definition, not a later scholar’s classification. In the same article he placed anarchists “in common with all socialists, of whom they constitute the left wing”, and described himself among the “anarchist-communist direction”.

His relation to socialism was argumentative rather than a rejection of it. He shared the socialist premise that private ownership of land, capital and machinery had to go, but denied that the state could be the instrument of abolition. In “Anarchist Communism: Its Basis and Principles” he called anarchism “the no-government system of socialism” and set out a double origin: an economic current shared with socialists and a political current shared with the most advanced radicals. “The no-capitalist system implies the no-government system”, he concluded, and insisted that the aim was not a distant ideal but a tendency already at work: “Free agreement is becoming a substitute for law. And free cooperation a substitute for governmental guardianship.” (MIA, “Anarchist Communism: Its Basis and Principles”, from Kropotkin’s Revolutionary Pamphlets, ed. Roger N. Baldwin, Vanguard Press, 1927 [Primary Theoretical]).

The dispute with state socialists is sharpest in The State: Its Historic Role. “It is above all over the question of the State that socialists are divided”, he wrote, between those who hope to make revolution “through the State by preserving and even extending most of its powers” and those like himself who saw the state as “an obstacle to the social revolution, the greatest hindrance to the birth of a society based on equality and liberty” (Anarchist Library, The State: Its Historic Role [Primary Theoretical, text seen as a search-result extract]). He distinguished state from government: “The State idea means something quite different from the idea of government”, because a state involves territorial concentration and the concentration of many social functions in few hands. The English translator, Vernon Richards, noted that Malatesta used the word state in a different sense, to mean the sum of political, legislative, judicial, military and financial institutions, and suggested that Malatesta’s definition corresponded more closely to the modern situation (MIA, The State, index, translator’s notes [Primary Theoretical, quoting Kropotkin]). That is a documented disagreement inside anarchism, not a settled point.

The war of 1914 split the movement, and Kropotkin took the pro-Allied side. His letter to the Swedish professor Gustav Steffen, printed in Freedom in autumn 1914, said it was the duty of everyone who cherished human progress “to do everything in one’s power, according to one’s capacities, to crush down the invasion of the Germans into Western Europe” (MIA, “Letter to Steffen on World War 1, published in Freedom in 1914” [Primary Theoretical]). In February 1916 he was one of the signatories of the “Manifesto of the Sixteen”, which declared that “we, anarchists, anti-militarists, enemies of war, passionate partisans of peace and the fraternity of peoples, are ranged on the side of the resistance”; a note to the text says that only fifteen names had actually approved it when it was published (MIA, “The Manifesto of the Sixteen”, 28 February 1916 [Primary Theoretical]). Against this, Malatesta, Emma Goldman, Alexander Berkman and others signed the Anti-War Manifesto of February 1915, which held that “No distinction is possible between offensive wars and defensive wars” and that the root of war lay “exclusively in the existence of the State, which is the political form of privilege” (MIA, Malatesta, “The Anarchist International: Anti-War Manifesto”, 15 February 1915 [Primary Theoretical]).

Major contributions#

The first contribution was the theory of mutual aid as a factor of evolution. Kropotkin did not deny struggle; he divided it. One form pits organism against organism for scarce resources and produces competition. The other pits organisms against harsh physical conditions and is best waged together. “Sociability is as much a law of nature as mutual struggle”, he wrote, and he asked whether nature favoured “those who are continually at war with each other, or those who support one another”, answering that “those animals which acquire habits of mutual aid are undoubtedly the fittest” (Gould 1988 [Scholarly Secondary, quoting Mutual Aid]). In his own conclusion he put the claim in comparative form: “The animal species, in which individual struggle has been reduced to its narrowest limits, and the practice of mutual aid has attained the greatest development, are invariably the most numerous, the most prosperous, and the most open to further progress. The unsociable species, on the contrary, are doomed to decay” (MIA, Mutual Aid, Conclusion [Primary Theoretical]). He extended the argument to human history, tracing mutual aid through the clan, the village community, the guild and the medieval city, and claiming that “the combination of mutual aid, as it was practised within the guild and the Greek clan, with a large initiative which was left to the individual and the group by means of the federative principle, gave to mankind the two greatest periods of its history – the ancient Greek city and the medieval city periods” (MIA, Mutual Aid, Conclusion [Primary Theoretical]).

The second was a critique of the state as a historical form rather than a necessary one. In The State he insisted that society long preceded the state, that the state was “only one of the forms assumed by society in the course of history”, and that in European history it was of recent origin. He treated it as the triple alliance of military chief, judge and priest, and traced the destruction of the medieval communes by monarchy, church and war (Anarchist Library, The State: Its Historic Role [Primary Theoretical, text seen as a search-result extract]). His broader claim about law was made in Law and Authority (1886): law was born of violence and superstition, mixed customs necessary to society with customs useful only to rulers, and served to protect property and government. “No more laws! No more judges! Liberty, equality, and practical human sympathy are the only effectual barriers we can oppose to the anti-social instincts of certain among us” (MIA, “Law and Authority”, 1886 [Primary Theoretical]).

The third was the case for anarchist communism rather than mere collectivism. The Conquest of Bread argued that scarcity was manufactured rather than natural: “All belongs to all. All things are for all men”, and “What we proclaim is THE RIGHT TO WELL-BEING: WELL-BEING FOR ALL!” (MIA, The Conquest of Bread, chapters 1 and 2 [Primary Theoretical, transcription of the 1926 Vanguard Press edition]). The means were equally blunt: “There must be EXPROPRIATION. The well-being of all – the end; expropriation – the means.” The Wage System, published as Freedom Pamphlet No. 1, attacked collectivism for retaining “two institutions which form the very basis of that rule, namely, representative government and the wage system” (MIA, “The Wage System”, 1920 edition [Primary Theoretical]).

The fourth was a practical programme of decentralisation. In the 1910 Britannica article he wrote that “True progress lies in the direction of decentralization, both territorial and functional, in the development of the spirit of local and personal initiative, and of free federation from the simple to the compound, in lieu of the present hierarchy from the centre to the periphery” (MIA, “Anarchism”, 1910 [Primary Theoretical]). Fields, Factories and Workshops supplied the economic case: industry could be dispersed, agriculture combined with manufacture, and brain work with manual work, so that the division of labour need not condemn workers to narrow, monotonous toil (Gutenberg, Fields, Factories and Workshops, Preface and contents [Primary Theoretical, seen as a search-result extract]; MIA, Fields, Factories and Workshops, contents [Primary Theoretical]).

The fifth was to connect these political claims to a theory of human moral development. In Mutual Aid he argued that society rests not on love or personal sympathy but on “the conscience – be it only at the stage of an instinct – of human solidarity”, and on “the unconscious recognition of the force that is borrowed by each man from the practice of mutual aid; of the close dependency of every one’s happiness upon the happiness of all; and of the sense of justice, or equity” (MIA, Mutual Aid, Introduction [Primary Theoretical]). In 1910 he summarised his own project as an attempt “to show the intimate, logical connection which exists between the modern philosophy of natural sciences and anarchism; to put anarchism on a scientific basis by the study of the tendencies that are apparent now in society and may indicate its further evolution; and to work out the basis of anarchist ethics” (MIA, “Anarchism”, 1910 [Primary Theoretical]).

Analysis of capitalism#

Kropotkin’s account begins with the accumulation of a common inheritance by a few. In The Conquest of Bread he described land, mines, railways, machinery and knowledge as the product of many generations, then asked “By what right then can any one whatever appropriate the least morsel of this immense whole and say – This is mine, not yours?” (MIA, The Conquest of Bread, chapter 1 [Primary Theoretical]). The result, in his argument, is that the worker “must sell his labour for a scant and uncertain wage” and that production is directed not to needs but to the gains of speculators. He connected this to empire: because workers could not buy back what they produced, industry sought markets abroad, “in the East, in Africa, everywhere, in Egypt, Tonkin or the Congo”, leading to “wars, perpetual wars”.

His critique of wage labour is most systematic in The Wage System. He rejected both unequal pay by skill and equal pay for all, on the grounds that neither escapes the wage relation. Wage differences, he wrote, are “a highly complex product of taxation, government interference, monopoly and capitalistic greed – in a word, of the State and the capitalist system” (MIA, “The Wage System” [Primary Theoretical]). He argued that no one can measure the contribution of each worker to a modern product, since the mine, the railway, the fields and the machines all enter into it. “One course remains: not to measure them at all, but to recognize the right of all who take part in productive labor first of all to live, and then to enjoy the comforts of life.” His verdict on the collectivist alternative was that “The Revolution will be Communistic; or it will be drowned in blood, and must be begun all over again.”

The state had a central place in the making of capitalism. In “Law and Authority” he wrote that law “has no title to the respect of men. Born of violence and superstition, and established in the interests of consumer, priest and rich exploiter, it must be utterly destroyed on the day when the people desire to break their chains” (MIA, “Law and Authority” [Primary Theoretical]). In 1910 he put the point compactly: the state was “the chief instrument for permitting the few to monopolize the land, and the capitalists to appropriate for themselves a quite disproportionate share of the yearly accumulated surplus of production”, and “State capitalism would only increase the powers of bureaucracy and capitalism” (MIA, “Anarchism”, 1910 [Primary Theoretical]). Mattick, writing in 1956, judged that this conflated nature and society: Kropotkin treated mutual aid as a “moral instinct” of “prehuman origin” and a “law of nature”, and so did not answer the Malthusian argument that Huxley had actually made (Mattick 1956 [Scholarly Secondary, Marxist]).

War was, for him, a product of these same structures. In the 1914 letter printed in Freedom he traced the war to German militarism and the annexation of Alsace-Lorraine after 1871, but added that “The causes of war must be attacked at the root” and that workers and educated middle-class people would see “the part that capital and state have played in bringing about the armed conflicts between nations” (MIA, “Letter to Steffen”, 1914 [Primary Theoretical]). In the November 1914 issue of Freedom itself, his article “Anti-Militarism. Was It Properly Understood?” argued that the general strike had been treated as a contradiction in terms: wars were said to follow inevitably from capitalism and the state, yet a strike was supposed to prevent them without abolishing either (Freedom, no. 307, November 1914 [Primary Theoretical, Kropotkin’s article; Primary Official, masthead and publication details]).

Analysis of socialism#

Kropotkin wrote a detailed institutional proposal, which distinguishes him from writers who left only a critique. The core was the free commune federated with other free communes. In the preface to The Conquest of Bread he described the Paris Commune of 1871 as asserting “the rights of the Commune to its full autonomy”, and drew the lesson that “the free commune would be henceforth the medium in which the ideas of modern Socialism may come to realization”. Such communes need not be small: they could be “vast agglomerations, like Paris, or, still better, small territories”, and “These communes would federate to constitute nations in some cases, even irrespectively of the present national frontiers (like the Cinque Ports, or the Hansa)”, while large labour associations would handle railways and docks (MIA, The Conquest of Bread, Preface, signed Brighton, January 1913 [Primary Theoretical]).

The economic rule was to be need rather than service rendered. In The Wage System he rejected both “To each according to his deeds” and the labour-note schemes of the collectivists, because both required the measurement of individual contribution and therefore preserved the wage relation. His positive formula was that society should recognise “the right of all who take part in productive labor first of all to live, and then to enjoy the comforts of life” (MIA, The Wage System [Primary Theoretical]). In “Anarchist Communism” he stated it as “everybody, contributing for the common well-being to the full extent, of his capacities, shall enjoy also from the common stock of society to the fullest possible extent of his needs” (MIA, “Anarchist Communism” [Primary Theoretical]).

Political organisation was to be rebuilt from below by free agreement. Kropotkin used existing institutions as evidence that this was possible: the railway network of Europe was “a confederation of so many scores of separate societies” linked “without even so much as a Central Board of European Railways”; the Postal Union and learned societies reached agreement through congresses whose delegates brought back “proposals of agreements”, not laws; and in Swiss villages a householder belonged to “at least a dozen different societies which supply him with what is considered elsewhere as a function of the municipal government”. He did not deny that coordination was needed; he denied that it required a central law-making body. “It is merely stupid to elect a few men and to entrust them with the task of making laws on all possible subjects, of which subjects most of them are utterly ignorant.” (MIA, “Anarchist Communism” [Primary Theoretical]).

His practical writing for the British movement came through Freedom and Freedom Press, not through a parliamentary party. In 1910 he said anarchists “refuse to be party to the present state organization” and had “endeavoured to promote their ideas directly amongst the labour organizations” since the founding of the International Working Men’s Association (MIA, “Anarchism”, 1910 [Primary Theoretical]). Barry Pateman’s bibliography of Freedom Press pamphlets records The Wage System as pamphlet No. 1 in December 1889, “Anarchist Communism: Its Basis and Principles” as No. 4 in September 1891, “Anarchist Morality” as No. 6 in 1892, “The State: Its Historical Role” as No. 11 in September 1898, “Wars and Capitalism” in August 1914 and “Revolutionary Government” in June 1923 (Pateman, “Freedom Press pamphlets”, Kate Sharpley Library via libcom [Scholarly Secondary]). The Freedom masthead in the issue opened for this profile described the paper as “A JOURNAL OF ANARCHIST COMMUNISM”, monthly, one penny, with an office at 127 Ossulston Street, London, money orders payable to T. H. Keell, and the warning that “The Editors are not necessarily in agreement with signed articles” (Freedom, no. 307, November 1914 [Primary Official]).

His experience of the Russian Revolution modified the emphasis without changing the principle. In the March 1920 letter to Lenin he wrote that “Even if the dictatorship of the party were an appropriate means to bring about a blow to the capitalist system (which I strongly doubt), it is nevertheless harmful for the creation of a new socialist system. What are necessary and needed are local institutions, local forces; but there are none, anywhere.” He judged that “Russia has already become a Soviet Republic only in name” because “it is the party committees, not the soviets, who rule in Russia”, and concluded that “Without the participation of local forces, without an organization from below of the peasants and workers themselves, it is impossible to build a new life”. In the December letter he protested against the taking of hostages as “a return to the worst period of the Middle Ages and religious wars”, while conceding that “With all of its serious deficiencies (and I, as you know, see them well), the October Revolution brought about enormous progress.” Both statements belong together; quoting only the criticism flattens his position (MIA, “Two letters to Lenin”, 1920 [Primary Theoretical]).

Recommended reading#

These five selections are the shortlist in the research dossier and are starting points rather than a complete account of his output.

  • Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution (1902) — the central scientific argument, and the book in which he states most clearly what he thought cooperation contributed to evolution.
  • The Conquest of Bread (1892 French; English 1906/1907) — the positive programme: expropriation, well-being for all and the federated free commune.
  • The State: Its Historic Role (1896 lecture; English 1898) — the shortest statement of why he thought the state had to be abolished rather than captured.
  • The Wage System (1889; new edition 1920) — his most concentrated critique of wage labour and of collectivist substitutes for it.
  • Fields, Factories and Workshops (1899; revised 1913) — the practical case for decentralised industry and the joining of brain work to manual work.

Selected lectures and interviews#

No sound or film recording of Kropotkin was opened or verified, and this section records an absence rather than inventing entries. He died in 1921 and the surviving corpus located in the dossier is written: books, pamphlets, articles and letters. The nearest equivalent to a lecture is The State: Its Historic Role, written for a series of lectures planned for the Milles Colonnes Hall in Paris in March 1896. The MIA translator’s notes record that Kropotkin chose that subject and “Anarchism: Its Philosophy and Ideal”, but that French police stopped him at Dieppe and expelled him from France, so the lectures were never delivered (MIA, The State, translator’s notes [Primary Theoretical]). It survives as a text, not as a recording. The Freedom symposium of November 1914 is likewise a printed debate rather than an audio source. No archive holding Kropotkin’s voice was identified in the sources opened here.

Intellectual influences#

Kropotkin named his influences himself, most fully in the 1910 Britannica article. He credited Godwin with being “the first to formulate the political and economical conceptions of anarchism” in Enquiry concerning Political Justice (1793), noting that Godwin claimed the abolition of the state and concluded in favour of communism. He credited Proudhon with first using the name anarchy in 1840 for a society without government, while recording that Proudhon rejected communist schemes and defended possession against the state through mutualism and labour cheques. Bakunin he described as “the leading spirit among these Latin federations” of the International after 1871, demanding the complete abolition of the state and full autonomy for nation, region and commune. In the same survey he discussed Zeno, Stirner, Tucker, Owen, Fourier, Saint-Simon, Considerant, Louis Blanc, Pecqueur, Josiah Warren, William Thompson, John Gray and J. F. Bray. These are documented references in his own writing; they establish that he read and argued with these writers, not that any one of them determined his position (MIA, “Anarchism”, 1910 [Primary Theoretical]).

Two influences stand out in the scientific work. Darwin supplied the vocabulary of the struggle for existence, which Kropotkin reinterpreted rather than rejected. Kessler supplied the specific hypothesis: in his introduction Kropotkin recalled Kessler’s 1880 lecture and its claim that “besides the law of Mutual Struggle there is in Nature the law of Mutual Aid, which, for the success of the struggle for life, and especially for the progressive evolution of the species, is far more important than the law of mutual contest” (MIA, Mutual Aid, Introduction [Primary Theoretical]). Huxley was an adversarial influence: Kropotkin wrote Mutual Aid in response to Huxley’s 1888 essay on the struggle for existence in human society, and Bates encouraged him to write the articles (MIA, Mutual Aid, Introduction [Primary Theoretical]). Gould adds a contextual influence that Kropotkin did not name: the Russian school of Darwinian critics, including Danilevsky, who rejected Malthus on grounds drawn from Russia’s sparse population and harsh climate (Gould 1988 [Scholarly Secondary]).

Influence and legacy#

His influence ran through the anarchist press and the international labour movement. The Kate Sharpley Library bibliography states that, together with Mother Earth pamphlets and those initiated by Benjamin Tucker in the United States, Freedom Press pamphlets “were responsible for introducing written anarchism to, initially, English speakers all over the world” (Pateman, “Freedom Press pamphlets” [Scholarly Secondary]). That judgement is the librarian’s, but the pamphlet list supports the factual part of it: Kropotkin’s texts were reissued repeatedly, in some cases through eight or nine editions. The Freedom masthead confirms a monthly journal of anarchist communism with a London office and an international subscription price, and the November 1914 issue shows Kropotkin and Malatesta arguing in the same number (Freedom, no. 307 [Primary Theoretical and Primary Official]).

His biological argument was revived, and qualified, by Stephen Jay Gould in 1988. Gould wrote that he had previously regarded Kropotkin as “daftly idiosyncratic” and as one of those “soft and woolly thinkers” presented in biology courses, but that reading Todes’s work on Russian evolutionary thought changed his view: “I learned that the parochiality had been mine in my ignorance of Russian evolutionary thought, not Kropotkin’s in his isolation in England.” His conclusion was that “Kropotkin’s basic argument is correct”, with the two objections set out below (Gould 1988 [Scholarly Secondary]). Paul Mattick, by contrast, treated the book as a useful corrective to Social Darwinism while denying that it settled the social question (Mattick 1956 [Scholarly Secondary, Marxist]).

His political legacy is visible in the persistence of the charge he levelled at Lenin. The 1920 letters anticipate the standard later anarchist and libertarian socialist criticism of party rule: that a party dictatorship can destroy the institutions it claims to be building. That is a statement of influence only in the sense that the text exists and has been repeatedly republished. The MIA index lists “Peter Kropotkin’s Last Letter” in the Communist Review and a 1917 account of a meeting between Lenin and Kropotkin, but neither was opened here, so the extent of the reception is not measured in the dossier. The scholarly biography by Woodcock and Avakumovic, repeatedly reissued, is the standard English life; only its catalogue record was opened, and claims about how later movements used his ideas should be checked against that book and specialist histories before they are put into a published profile (Internet Archive, catalogue record [Primary Official, catalogue metadata]).

Criticisms and controversies#

The biology drew the most substantial sympathetic scholarly assessment and the clearest technical objection. Gould judged that Kropotkin “did commit a common conceptual error in failing to recognize that natural selection is an argument about advantages to individual organisms, however they may struggle”, and that he “sometimes speaks of mutual aid as selected for the benefit of entire populations or species – a concept foreign to classic Darwinian logic”. Gould immediately qualified the point: Kropotkin “also (and often) recognized that selection for mutual aid directly benefits each individual in its own struggle for personal success”, so he “did include the orthodox solution as his primary justification for mutual aid”. Gould’s second objection was general: arguments that find kindness and harmony “intrinsically in nature” should be treated with suspicion, because “There are no shortcuts to moral insight. Nature is not intrinsically anything that can offer comfort or solace in human terms” (Gould 1988 [Scholarly Secondary]). His verdict supports the core claim while rejecting the group-selection reading and the inference from nature to ethics.

The Marxist objection came from Mattick. His 1956 review accepts that Kropotkin “demonstrated that both in the animal world and human society it is rather mutual aid which secures existence and makes for progress”, and calls the book “an important study of animal behaviour and of the evolution of human sociality”. His criticism is that Kropotkin “did not answer Huxley’s Malthusian argument, even though it is the only one Huxley advanced in support of his views”, because he “too did not clearly enough distinguish between society and nature”. Where Kropotkin saw a moral instinct of prehuman origin, Mattick held that “over-population” was relative to the needs of capital accumulation and was “a product of the capitalist mode of production not of ‘natural law’”. His final judgement is that the whole Huxley–Kropotkin controversy “is somewhat beside the point – it does not touch upon the relevant issues of society, namely that ‘mutual aid’ in human society presupposes the abolition of class relations” (Mattick 1956 [Scholarly Secondary, Marxist]). This is a political objection to method, not a refutation of the animal observations.

The disagreement among anarchists over war is documented on both sides. The November 1914 Freedom symposium printed Kropotkin’s case alongside a piece headed “Anarchists Have Forgotten their Principles”, which argued that the failure of anarchist principles was not due to their inadequacy but “from the fact that these have been forgotten and betrayed”, and insisted that “the only hope is in revolution” (Freedom, no. 307, November 1914 [Primary Theoretical]). The Malatesta–Goldman–Berkman Anti-War Manifesto of February 1915 went further: “No distinction is possible between offensive wars and defensive wars”, and anarchists should “summon the slaves to revolt against their masters” (MIA, Anti-War Manifesto, 1915 [Primary Theoretical]). Kropotkin’s own 1914 article answered the same point from the other side: the general strike could not prevent a war whose causes lay in capitalism and the state while millions of workers accepted the nationalist case. Both are primary statements of an unresolved dispute inside anarchism.

The disagreement over the state came from within the anarchist tradition too. Vernon Richards, translating The State in 1969 and adding notes in 1986, quoted Malatesta’s 1891 definition of the state as the sum of coercive institutions and suggested that it corresponded better to contemporary conditions than Kropotkin’s distinction between state and government. Richards also recorded that some readers would question the value of Kropotkin’s detailed history of the distant past for people seeking to change the present (MIA, The State, translator’s notes [Primary Theoretical, translator’s commentary]).

On the Bolshevik period, Kropotkin’s own letters contain both criticism and qualification, which is why the two should not be separated. He condemned the “dictatorship of the party” and the hostage policy, yet wrote that the October Revolution “brought about enormous progress” (MIA, “Two letters to Lenin”, 1920 [Primary Theoretical]). The MIA index records Lenin’s article “A Meeting with Kropotkin” and Kropotkin’s last letter in the Communist Review; neither was opened, so no claim about Lenin’s reply is made here.

A limitation of this research belongs in the same list. Most primary texts were read in MIA transcriptions rather than in first editions or critical editions. Translation and transcription choices can affect the exact wording of quotations, especially in The State, whose MIA editor notes that the text was corrupted in the source file and that some phrases were reconstructed from the French. No page references are given for quotations from these transcriptions.

Related thinkers#

Darwin supplied the concept of the struggle for existence that Kropotkin reinterpreted; Huxley supplied the gladiatorial reading of it that he attacked. Kropotkin’s relation to both is argued in his own introduction to Mutual Aid (MIA, Mutual Aid, Introduction [Primary Theoretical]). Kessler’s 1880 lecture gave him the hypothesis, and Gould, via Todes, places him in a wider Russian critique of Malthus (MIA, Mutual Aid, Introduction [Primary Theoretical]; Gould 1988 [Scholarly Secondary]).

Godwin, Proudhon and Bakunin were the three predecessors he discussed at length in the 1910 Britannica article: Godwin for no-state socialism, Proudhon for mutualism and the word anarchy, Bakunin for the federalist International and the abolition of the state (MIA, “Anarchism”, 1910 [Primary Theoretical]). Marx and the collectivists were targets in The Wage System: Kropotkin invoked Engels’s preface to the Communist Manifesto in describing the early English socialists, and argued that Marx’s adoption of Ricardo’s theory of value committed him to a wage scale Kropotkin rejected. Mattick’s 1956 review is the Marxist reply on the biological side (MIA, “The Wage System” [Primary Theoretical]).

Malatesta and Emma Goldman were allies in the broader anarchist movement and opponents over the war. Both signed the 1915 Anti-War Manifesto against the position Kropotkin took in Freedom and in the Manifesto of the Sixteen (MIA, Anti-War Manifesto [Primary Theoretical]); Malatesta also disagreed with his definition of the state (MIA, The State, translator’s notes [Primary Theoretical]). Lenin and the Bolsheviks were addressed directly in the 1920 letters: Kropotkin accepted that the revolution had achieved progress but denied that party dictatorship could build socialism (MIA, “Two letters to Lenin” [Primary Theoretical]).
