Angela Davis: Race, Class, Gender and Abolition

A research profile of the Marxist feminist's analysis of race, class, imprisonment and abolition, with her major works and critics.

Angela Davis argued that racism, sexism and class exploitation form one system, and that imprisonment is one of the ways that system defends itself. This profile sets out those arguments in her own words, records the sources behind them, and reports the objections made by other scholars, including writers who broadly share her concerns. It is a profile of claims, not a substitute for the historical chapters of this book.

Biography#

The existing chapter on Black Women Communists covers Davis’s childhood in Birmingham, her study with Herbert Marcuse, her entry into the Communist Party and the Che-Lumumba Club, the courthouse event of 7 August 1970, her flight, FBI listing, arrest on 13 October 1970, sixteen months in custody, trial and acquittal on 4 June 1972, and it quotes her prison interviews; this profile adds a check of the publication record, a connected account of her arguments about race, class, gender and imprisonment, her own statements about influences, socialism and communism, and the scholarly criticism those arguments have received.

Angela Yvonne Davis was born in Birmingham, Alabama, on 26 January 1944. The National Archives biographical page and the European Graduate School faculty biography both give that date and place, and both describe a middle-class family with long-standing links to communist and labour organisers. The National Archives notes that her mother, Sallye Bell Davis, worked for the Southern Negro Youth Congress, an organisation associated with the Communist Party. The two institutional records agree on the main sequence of her education: a B.A. at Brandeis in 1965, where she studied with Herbert Marcuse; study in Frankfurt; an M.A. at the University of California, San Diego; and a doctorate at Humboldt University in Berlin. Neither page gives a year for the doctorate, and the University of Alabama’s Alabama Authors entry, checked only as a search endpoint, also names Humboldt without supplying a year. No year is given here.

Davis joined the Communist Party USA in 1968, according to the European Graduate School biography and Joy James’s introduction to The Angela Y. Davis Reader. James, a political theorist and the editor of that collection, places her in the Che-Lumumba Club, one of the party’s all-Black collectives, and describes her party work as lasting more than twenty years, including service on the Central Committee. That is James’s account rather than an independently audited party record. The European Graduate School confirms the 1968 membership and her two candidacies for the vice-presidency in 1980 and 1984. She was appointed to teach philosophy at UCLA in 1969 and dismissed in 1970 after her party membership became public; the University of California’s own faculty page, checked only as a search endpoint, says she was removed because of her activism and her Communist Party membership.

The federal record fixes the dates of the manhunt more tightly than many retrospective accounts. The FBI’s chronological list of the Ten Most Wanted fugitives records “Angela Yvonne Davis” as number 309, placed on the list on 18 August 1970 and located on 13 October 1970, and says she was arrested in a motel room in New York. A separate FBI image caption gives “January 13, 1970”, which contradicts the Bureau’s own table and the contemporary New York Times report of 5 June 1972; the table and the contemporary report are preferred here. The European Graduate School and the National Archives agree that she spent sixteen months in custody and was acquitted in 1972. The New York Times report, filed from San Jose on 4 June and published on 5 June, records that an all-white jury found her not guilty on three counts after thirteen hours of deliberation. It also records the prosecution’s argument that the guns registered in her name were supplied for the courthouse action, and the defence’s answer that they were for the Soledad Brothers’ defence headquarters. Those are positions stated at trial, not findings of fact.

After the trial she taught at several California institutions. The European Graduate School records her appointment in History of Consciousness at the University of California, Santa Cruz, in 1991, a UC Presidential Chair in 1994, chairing of the Feminist Studies Department from 2003 to 2006, and emerita status in 2008; the National Archives gives the same UCSC appointment and chair. In the interviews collected in Abolition Democracy she confirms that she had left the Communist Party by the 1990s while adding that she still called herself a communist. James links her departure to the 1991 “Initiative to Unite and Renew the Party”, signed by roughly eight hundred members, and to the formation of the Committees of Correspondence. The National Archives gives 1991 as the year she left the party. The chapter on US Black Communist Leaders supplies the wider history of the organisation she criticised.

Intellectual tradition#

Davis wrote and spoke as a Marxist feminist, a communist and an abolitionist, and resisted labels that would separate those commitments. In Abolition Democracy she told Eduardo Mendieta that although she was no longer a party member, “I still consider myself a communist”, because without the possibility of defeating capitalism she would have “no inspiration to continue with my political work”. She rejected the idea that democracy and capitalism are the same thing, arguing that socialists must “disentangle our notions of capitalism and democracy so to pursue truly egalitarian models of democracy” and that “Communism—or socialism—can still help us to generate new versions of democracy”. These are her stated positions in a published interview, not a description of any existing socialist state.

Her Marxism was joined to the Frankfurt School tradition through Marcuse. In the same interview she said that she had “earned a great deal from Herbert Marcuse about the relationship between philosophy and ideology critique”, singled out his Counterrevolution and Revolt, and said she had been “inspired by critical theory” while insisting that philosophy must enter into conversation with other disciplines. She placed herself in a Black radical tradition that she thought was usually narrated through a false choice between nationalism and integration. In the Frontline interview she said that this way of telling Black political history “forecloses” questions of gender and sexuality, and that the usual male figures crowded out Anna Julia Cooper and Ida B. Wells. She preferred the anti-imperialist pan-Africanism of W. E. B. Du Bois, which she described in Abolition Democracy as a political identification forged in struggle rather than a biological or racial link. James’s introduction records her critical view of the Black Panther Party’s masculinity and of cultural nationalism, and her “permanently ambiguous status” in relation to the Panthers; that quotation is from Davis’s memoir as quoted by James, and is reported here as such.

Major contributions#

Her first distinctive contribution was to insist that racism, sexism and class exploitation are one structure rather than three campaigns to be coordinated later. Her 1971 essay “Reflections on the Black Woman’s Role in the Community of Slaves”, written while she was in custody and published in The Black Scholar, attacked the “matriarch” stereotype and argued that enslaved women’s work for their own communities differed from the work they were forced to perform for enslavers. James describes the essay as one of the earliest analyses of the intersections of racism, sexism and capitalism in the slave economy, the subject also treated historically in Slavery, Race and Capital. In Women, Race and Class (1981) she carried the same method into the history of abolitionism, suffrage, birth control, rape, housework and childcare, arguing that white-led feminism repeatedly advanced its own gains by borrowing the fears of white supremacy. The Penguin Random House description names the same subjects, including the Grimké sisters, Frederick Douglass, Margaret Sanger and the campaign against lynching. She later adopted the word “intersectionality” — a term coined by Kimberlé Crenshaw in 1989 for the way different forms of discrimination overlap and reinforce one another — telling Frontline that “in the latter 1990s we have to recognize the intersectionality, the interconnectedness of all of these institutions and attitudes.” Her use of the word was an adoption of a term with a specific legal genealogy, not its origin.

Her second contribution was a theory of imprisonment that joined political punishment to political economy. In Are Prisons Obsolete? (2003) she argued that the prison had to be understood historically, not as a permanent feature of social life. By “prison-industrial complex” she meant the relationships among prison construction, corporations, guards’ unions, the media and legislative agendas; her own definition notes that prison building and operation attracted “vast amounts of capital” in a way that “recalled the emergence of the military industrial complex”, after which activists “began to refer to a ‘prison industrial complex.’” The University of California’s faculty description says she helped popularise the term. She used “abolition” to mean the political project of ending an institution rather than making its conditions fairer, and compared the campaign to abolish prisons with the campaigns against slavery, lynching and segregation. She was one of twenty-five organisers of the 1998 Berkeley conference “Critical Resistance: Beyond the Prison Industrial Complex”, according to the publisher’s author note; that conference produced the organisation Critical Resistance.

The third contribution was a practice of “non-reformist reform”, developed most clearly in the discussion of imprisonment. Davis did not argue that conditions inside prison were irrelevant. She argued that reforms which stop at making prisons better can legitimise the institution and make decarceration harder to imagine. In Are Prisons Obsolete? she wrote that “frameworks that rely exclusively on reforms help to produce the stultifying idea that nothing lies beyond the prison”, and made decarceration, decriminalisation of drug use and sexual services, and restorative justice the immediate questions. Julia Sudbury, a sociologist and prison abolitionist, describes the same idea as reforms judged by whether they decrease or increase prison budgets and the reach of the criminal justice system. This is a theory of strategy as much as a theory of punishment.

Analysis of capitalism#

Davis treats capitalism as a system that needs racial hierarchy and unpaid or underpaid women’s work, not as a market that merely produces unequal outcomes. In Are Prisons Obsolete? she connects the prison boom of the 1980s and 1990s to deindustrialisation: corporations moved in search of cheap labour, communities lost their economic base, and the people left behind became “perfect candidates for prison”. Her formulation is severe: “The prison has become a black hole into which the detritus of contemporary capitalism is deposited. Mass imprisonment generates profits as it devours social wealth, and thus it tends to reproduce the very conditions that lead people to prison.” She also describes the ideological effect of the prison: it is “an abstract site into which undesirables are deposited, relieving us of the responsibility of thinking about the real issues afflicting those communities from which prisoners are drawn in such disproportionate numbers.” That is a claim about how capitalism governs what becomes visible, not only about who is imprisoned.

On gender, the chapter “The Approaching Obsolescence of Housework” in Women, Race and Class argues that housework is “virtually invisible” and that its socialisation “presupposes an end to the profit-motive’s reign over the economy”. The chapter moves from the drudgery of individual households to the possibility of incorporating domestic tasks into the industrial economy, and then to the history of Black women’s paid domestic labour. The point is that “housewife” was never a universal female condition: Black women worked for wages in white households as well as caring for their own. The Cornell library guide’s note on her 1985 pamphlet Violence Against Women and the Ongoing Challenge to Racism, whose publisher is not verified here, reproduces her claim that rape bears a direct relationship to power structures through “the complex interconnectedness of race, gender, and class oppression”.

In the Frontline interview she tied the same analysis to globalisation. She said that the movement of corporations to low-wage countries eroded the tax base of Black communities, drew poor people into “alternative economies”, and “sends them into the prison industrial complex”. She used the example of a young Black buyer of Nike shoes and the conditions of garment production in Indonesia to argue that consumption and imprisonment belong to one system. In Abolition Democracy she returned to Marx’s analysis of the commodity, saying that the commodity had “penetrated every aspect of people’s lives” and that the task was “to uncover the social relations that are both embodied and concealed by these commodities”. She did not, in these sources, offer a formal model of the value of household labour or a general theory of the capitalist state; those gaps matter when her work is compared with social reproduction theory.

Analysis of socialism#

Davis wrote no blueprint for a socialist economy and did not present a model of planning, ownership or distribution. What she offered was a set of claims about what a socialist or communist politics must do. In Abolition Democracy she said that a version of democracy which assumed capitalism was its only possible form had to be challenged, and that “There must be an alternative to capitalism.” Communism or socialism, in her account, should help people “generate new versions of democracy”. Abolition democracy is the positive side of that claim: the publisher’s description of the 2005 book links the abolition of prisons to the abolition of war, racism and “the social circumstances that lead poor men and women to look toward the military as their only avenue of escape from poverty, homelessness, and lack of opportunities.”

Her critique of actually existing communist organisation is as important as her defence of the idea. Joy James’s introduction reproduces the 1991 “Initiative to Unite and Renew the Party”, in which Davis and other signatories criticised the CPUSA for elitism and for “scant attention” to the women’s movement, argued that the party had “gone backward in attention to the struggle for African-American equality”, and called for the “principle of black and white leadership”. James reports that signatories were refused places on the party slate and that Davis left with most of the eight hundred, later serving briefly on the National Coordinating Committee of the Committees of Correspondence. In the Mendieta interview Davis described herself as a communist without the party. The evidence therefore supports a distinction between her attachment to a socialist horizon and her rejection of the party form as it then existed. It does not support a claim that she advocated a specific economic constitution.

Recommended reading#

These works are starting points for Davis’s argument, not a list of everything she published.

  • Women, Race & Class (1981). Begin here for the claim that racism, sexism and class exploitation are one structure: the book carries that method through abolitionism, suffrage, birth control, rape, housework and childcare, and argues that white-led feminism repeatedly advanced its own gains by borrowing the fears of white supremacy.
  • Are Prisons Obsolete? (2003). This is the clearest entry into her theory of imprisonment, because it treats the prison as a historical institution, names the “prison-industrial complex” and explains why reforms that only make prisons better can make a world beyond prisons harder to imagine.
  • Abolition Democracy: Beyond Empire, Prisons, and Torture (2005). Read this for the positive side of her argument: it is where she says there must be an alternative to capitalism, that communism or socialism can help generate new versions of democracy, and that the campaign against prisons belongs with the campaigns against war and racism.

Selected lectures and interviews#

The records below identify audiovisual material, with host and date where the record supplies them. No audio or video was listened to for this dossier; the statements drawn from these items come from catalogues and transcripts, not from playback.

The KPFA/Pacifica recording “The Liberation of Our People”, made at a Black Panther rally at Bobby Hutton Memorial Park in Oakland on 12 November 1969, is held with transcript and clip by the American Archive of Public Broadcasting, a collaboration of the Library of Congress and GBH. In it Davis says she prefers “Sister” to “professor” and says she would leave her job rather than compromise “the liberation struggle in this country”. The Drum, episode 58 of the Boston University Roxbury Radio Project / National Educational Network, dates from 27 October 1971; it is a biographical programme of her recorded speeches held by the University of Massachusetts Amherst Black Mass Communications Project, with a transcript in the catalogue record. Vibrations for a New People, a KPIX programme hosted by the Reverend Cecil Williams in 1972, is described by the Internet Archive catalogue as the first American broadcast interview after her arrest; the record gives the year and month of publication but not the exact recording date, so no recording date is stated here.

“The Gates to Freedom”, at the Embassy Auditorium in Los Angeles on 9 June 1972, is dated by the transcript hosted by the Almeida Theatre; American RadioWorks independently lists a speech at the Embassy Auditorium on 9 June 1972, so that date is used rather than any alternative. A KUNM public affairs presentation recorded at Popejoy Hall, University of New Mexico, on 19 November 1973 is held by the American Archive of Public Broadcasting, which warns that the transcript was received from a third party or generated by computer and has not been verified. “Women and the Fight Against Racism”, recorded and aired by Minnesota Public Radio at the Sabathani Community Center in Minneapolis in December 1984, has 21 December 1984 as its item date in the MPR archive; this was seen as a search endpoint, not read in full.

The PBS Frontline interview was conducted before 10 February 1998: its transcript is dated “Before February 10, 1998” and the online version was posted in 1998. It is therefore not called a 1998 interview without qualification here, although the existing chapter does. The interviews with Eduardo Mendieta were published as Abolition Democracy in 2005, with the publisher placing them in the context of the Abu Ghraib revelations of early 2004. Democracy Now! interviews with Amy Goodman include the programme of 24 December 2018 on fifty years of freedom struggles, the September 2020 discussion of defunding the police, and the discussion of 2 December 2022 on the UC strike, abolition and Palestine. Rush transcripts are posted for these; they are not verified verbatim records.

Intellectual influences#

Her own statements support a short list. Herbert Marcuse is the first: he taught her at Brandeis and again at UCSD, and in Abolition Democracy she names his Counterrevolution and Revolt as a particular inspiration for joining philosophy to the analysis of political events. The interviewers in that book name Adorno, Habermas and Horkheimer, and she replies that she was “certainly… inspired by critical theory”, while crediting Marcuse with crossing the borders between philosophy, sociology and literature and Adorno with bringing music and philosophy together. This is a direct statement of intellectual debt, not an inference from her topics.

Karl Marx appears in the same interview through the opening pages of Capital: the commodity, its “mysterious” character, and the task of uncovering the social relations concealed in things. Frederick Douglass appears as a source for the racialisation of crime in Are Prisons Obsolete?, where Davis quotes his account of “imput[ing] crime to color” and his example of a white man who blackened his face to escape suspicion. Antonio Gramsci and George Jackson appear together as prison intellectuals whose letters and notebooks she thought should be read side by side. W. E. B. Du Bois supplied the model of anti-imperialist pan-Africanism, and Davis’s critique of the Black nationalist tradition is conducted in his name. Claudia Jones and Lucy Gonzales Parsons are named in James’s introduction as figures whose theoretical and organisational work Davis thought had been made to disappear.

Among contemporaries, the acknowledgments to Are Prisons Obsolete? name Gina Dent, Ruth Gilmore, Cassandra Shaylor, Avery Gordon and the 1998 Critical Resistance organising committee as people whose conversations shaped the book. This collective authorship is worth keeping in view: Davis herself writes that she “should not be listed as the sole author” of that book. Joy James’s editing of The Angela Y. Davis Reader also shaped the way Davis’s earlier essays are now read together.

Influence and legacy#

Davis helped make prison abolition an intelligible position inside academic and activist debates rather than a slogan. The University of California’s faculty description, checked only as a search endpoint, says she “helped to popularize the notion of a ‘prison industrial complex’” and founded Critical Resistance with others. Her acknowledgments to Are Prisons Obsolete? record that the 1998 Berkeley conference attracted over three thousand people. Sudbury’s account in Criminal Justice Matters describes the concept as an organising tool used in courts, budgeting campaigns and criminology textbooks, and notes that by 2009 California abolitionist and reform groups had a share in a state plan to cut prison spending by reducing the population. That is evidence of influence on policy language, not proof that Davis’s analysis caused the change.

Her influence also runs through institutions of knowledge. The Reader collects eighteen essays and interviews from four decades, and Wiley’s description says her work has affected democratic theory, antiracist feminism and critical studies. The 2022 Haymarket book with Dent, Meiners and Richie extends the argument to family policing and gendered state violence; the publisher describes it as tracing the “genealogies of queer, anti-capitalist, internationalist, grassroots, and women-of-color-led feminist movements”. The 2016 collection with Frank Barat connects Ferguson to Palestine and the anti-apartheid movement. Her books have been translated and taught widely; the library guides at Cornell, Loyola Marymount and Shippensburg record the scale of that teaching apparatus, though those guides are finding aids rather than studies of reception. Earlier scholarship on the free-Angela campaign by Daniel Rosenberg and Maxim Matusevich, cited in the existing chapter on Black Women Communists, remains the place to trace how the international campaign itself changed the meanings attached to her case.

Criticisms and controversies#

The most direct scholarly attack on her central penal concept comes from the sociologist Loïc Wacquant. In “Prisoner reentry as myth and ceremony” (Dialectical Anthropology 34, 2010, 605–620) he argues that the prison boom was “state-crafting” rather than profit-seeking, and that scholars should “slay the chimera of the ‘Prison Industrial Complex’”. His objections are concrete. Punishment in the United States is decentralised across thousands of police, prosecutorial, jail and prison agencies, unlike the hierarchical military whose name supplies the analogy; corrections spending was a small fraction of national income, which he puts at under half of one percent of GDP in 2008; fewer than five thousand prisoners were employed by private firms at the peak he identifies in 2002; and private prison firms captured about six percent of the “carceral market” at their peak in 2000. He concludes that the concept “supplies no analytic traction” and that analysis should draw on Durkheim, Weber and Bourdieu rather than Marx and Fanon. These are Wacquant’s arguments and his figures, reported here as his; the source base behind his figures was not independently opened for this profile. They are a serious challenge to the claim that profit is the main driver of imprisonment, but they do not by themselves settle the question of how capital, race and state power interact. His comparison of prison spending to confectionery and soft-drink sales is his rhetorical device, not a finding about prison labour.

Davis’s earlier historical work has also been corrected in detail. Dorothy Roberts, in “Spiritual and Menial Housework” (Yale Journal of Law & Feminism 9, 1997, 51), argues that Davis’s claim that slave women performed “the only labor of the slave community which could not be directly and immediately claimed by the oppressor” must be qualified, because enslavers ultimately profited from the work women did caring for their own families and other enslaved people. Roberts’s footnote records that Davis later amended the statement to acknowledge that men also performed domestic tasks important to the slave community. Roberts’s broader thesis is that domestic work is split ideologically into “spiritual” work associated with privileged white women and “menial” work associated with minority, immigrant and working-class women, a division that Davis’s class analysis did not fully address. Jacqueline Jones, in “‘My Mother Was Much of a Woman’: Black Women, Work, and the Family under Slavery” (Feminist Studies 8, no. 2, Summer 1982, 235–270), acknowledges that Davis “made a crucial distinction between the work that women were forced to perform for a master and the domestic labor that they provided for their own families”, but says Davis’s “emphasis on the political implications of nurturing under slavery has not received the in-depth consideration it deserves”. Jones then supplies the empirical study Davis had called for. Jones’s article text was seen, but its PDF could not be parsed by the fetcher; the citation details were confirmed from Jones’s curriculum vitae. These are respectful corrections from historians working on the same questions.

A criticism from within the socialist feminist tradition concerns the housework debate. Corinna Dengler’s 2024 article, published by Cambridge University Press and seen only as a search endpoint, notes Davis’s “intersectional critique” of the Wages for Housework campaign, including her point that Black women and “their white working-class sisters” rarely had the privilege of being “just housewives”. Dengler replies that the founding texts of that campaign did not target housewives but housework as the “lowest common denominator” of women’s experience, albeit in forms shaped by class, ethnicity and race. The disagreement is over what the campaign claimed, not over whether housework is exploited.

Her party politics generated a different kind of controversy. The 1991 initiative reproduced by James criticised the CPUSA from inside for elitism, racial bias and neglect of women; its signatories were then denied places on the official slate, and Davis left the party. The National Archives notes that she visited Cuba, the USSR and East Germany after the trial and that the Soviet Union awarded her the Lenin Peace Prize in 1979, a fact also recorded by the European Graduate School. Those honours are evidence of how she was received abroad, not evidence about the truth of her claims. No scholarly study opening the FBI case file was read for this dossier, and no claim is made here about the Bureau’s motives beyond the existence of the Ten Most Wanted listing. The full text of the 1983 Race & Class review of Women, Race and Class and the complete JSTOR review by Malini Bhattacharya (Social Scientist 12, no. 11, 1984) were not opened, so no claim is made about their arguments.

Related thinkers#

Claudia Jones is the closest precursor. Her 1949 essay argued that Black women were oppressed as workers, as Black people and as women, and Davis’s 1981 housework chapter works the same combination through paid and unpaid domestic labour. The existing chapter on Black Women Communists covers Jones’s essay in detail, so it is not repeated here. Assata Shakur, whose autobiography Davis introduced, shares the claim that oppressive conditions produce revolutionaries, but writes from underground and exile rather than from the university. George Jackson was a friend and political collaborator whose letters Davis read critically, including their gender politics; her 1971 essay grew partly out of that exchange.

Ruth Wilson Gilmore supplied the analysis of Californian prison expansion that Davis quotes in Are Prisons Obsolete?, and co-founded Critical Resistance. Gina Dent supplied the argument about the visual culture of imprisonment cited in the same book. Joy James edited the Reader and wrote the long introduction that supplies much of the scholarly biographical frame used here. Kimberlé Crenshaw’s 1989 article coined “intersectionality”; Davis’s 1990s use of the word should be read as an adoption of a term with a specific legal genealogy, not as its origin. Loïc Wacquant and Dorothy Roberts appear in this profile as critics, not as allies. The disagreement recorded here is narrow but consequential: Davis and Gilmore treat capital and prison expansion as linked, while Wacquant argues that state-crafting, not profit, explains the prison boom.
