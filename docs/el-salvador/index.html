El Salvador: The War We Were Told Was Not Happening

On 23 March 1980, three days before his death, Óscar Romero, Archbishop of San Salvador, stood in his cathedral and addressed the army, the National Guard and the police on Radio YSAX.

On 23 March 1980, three days before his death, Óscar Romero, Archbishop of San Salvador, stood in his cathedral and addressed the army, the National Guard and the police on Radio YSAX. He told them they were killing their own campesinos, poor rural farmers, and that no soldier had to obey an order against the law of God. He ended with a command:

“I beg you, I beseech you, I order you in the name of God: stop the repression.”

Source: Óscar Romero, homily, 23 March 1980, published English translation, Archbishop Romero Trust (translator not named on the page)

Romero was shot the next afternoon while celebrating mass.

The church and the security forces#

The United Nations Commission on the Truth for El Salvador, set up under the peace accords to investigate rather than prosecute, later identified who had given the order. It described a “death squad”, an unofficial group of killers working with official protection:

“Former Major Roberto DAubuisson gave the order to assassinate the Archbishop and gave precise instructions to members of his security service, acting as a ‘death squad’, to organize and supervise the assassination.”

Source: UN Commission on the Truth, From Madness to Hope, 15 March 1993

The Commission also found that the Supreme Court helped those involved escape punishment.

Jean Donovan, an American lay missionary, a church worker without clerical status, in La Libertad wrote to Senator Edward Kennedy on 30 November 1980 about a twelve-year-old boy she believed had been macheted by ORDEN, a paramilitary force allied to the army. Witnesses had told her that men searching the refugee centre looked and spoke like Americans:

“As a North American, one of the hardest things to see is how the people are suffering here because of actions of our government.”

Source: Jean Donovan, letter to Senator Edward Kennedy, 30 November 1980, quoted in a UPI report of 13 December 1980

She was killed three days later with three other churchwomen. Her account of the men was hearsay, which she flagged; the letter survives in excerpts found with the possessions returned in her coffin.

The river and the village#

In March 1981 families fled towards the Lempa river, pursued by troops and helicopters. María Isidora Leiva remembered the crossing in a sworn statement given decades later:

“They cornered us on the banks of the river and we had to throw ourselves into the water, whether we could swim or not.”

Source: María Isidora Leiva, sworn statement, 2013, published English translation, University of Washington Center for Human Rights (translator not named)

The dead were swept away, so no count is reliable. The Commission recorded twenty to thirty killed and 189 missing against press estimates of 200. Declassified cables show US diplomats privately accepted that Salvadoran forces fired on civilians while disputing the word “massacre”.

At El Mozote in Morazán, soldiers of the Atlacatl battalion, an elite unit trained by American advisers, killed villagers and their neighbours in December 1981. Rufina Amaya, the only adult survivor, told Raymond Bonner of the New York Times, who entered rebel-held territory in January 1982:

“It was a great massacre. They left nothing.”

Source: Rufina Amaya to Raymond Bonner, January 1982, two sentences from one interview joined for brevity, reproduced in the Columbia University case study

Bonner found charred skulls beneath burned roofs and heard thirteen peasants name the battalion. Alma Guillermoprieto of the Washington Post entered Morazán at the same time. In a later testimony Amaya recalled:

“A las doce del mediodía ya habían terminado de matar a todos los hombres.” (“By noon they had already finished killing all the men.” My gloss.)

Source: Rufina Amaya, in Luciérnagas en El Mozote (1996), reproduced by BBC Mundo

The Argentine Forensic Anthropology Unit excavated the convent in 1992. Mark Danner of The New Yorker watched the team recover children’s skulls and recorded her response:

“Didn’t I tell you? All you could hear was that enormous screaming.”

Source: Rufina Amaya to Mark Danner, recorded before 1993, Danner’s English rendering of her Spanish

The team found at least 143 people, 131 under twelve, with bullet fragments in bone and clothing; it ruled out crossfire and a clandestine cemetery. The Commission set out the order:

“First, the men were tortured and executed, then the women were executed and, lastly, the children, in the place where they had been locked up.”

Source: UN Commission on the Truth, From Madness to Hope, 15 March 1993

The death toll is disputed. Villagers gave Bonner 733 names, a church commission 926, the archdiocese’s legal aid office, Tutela Legal, 767 for El Mozote and neighbouring hamlets, the Commission “more than 500 identified victims”. These count different things, and fire and fragmentation prevent precision.

Denial came from Washington too. Bonner and Guillermoprieto were called guerrilla propagandists because they had entered with rebels; the Wall Street Journal attacked them and Bonner was withdrawn. Assistant Secretary of State Thomas Enders told Congress there was no evidence of a systematic massacre, and the embassy never visited El Mozote. Ambassador Deane Hinton wrote privately:

“additional evidence strongly suggests that something happened that should not have happened and that it is quite possible Salvadoran military did commit excesses.”

Source: Ambassador Deane Hinton, cable of 1 February 1982, quoted by Joan Didion

Political officer Todd Greentree doubted the guerrillas could have invented it:

“I knew the guerrillas would never have masqueraded something like this, would never have fabricated it, if they were offering safe-conduct.”

Source: Todd Greentree, US embassy political officer, quoted by Joan Didion from Mark Danner’s book

The guerrillas’ war#

The Frente Farabundo Martí para la Liberación Nacional, or FMLN, the coalition of leftist guerrilla groups formed in 1980, launched the “final offensive” in January 1981, combining a general strike with attacks on garrisons. Celestino, a guerrilla from Arcatao interviewed decades later, thought the name wrong:

“That was the wrong named for it; it was called the final offensive. But that was the wrong name because it was barely the first offensive, yet they named it the final offensive.” (Transcription’s “named” preserved.)

Source: Celestino “Ebaristo”, oral history, University of Washington Unfinished Sentences project

The urban rising did not happen and the units that neared the military quarters in Chalatenango withdrew when their commander was wounded.

Commander Leonel González, also known as Salvador Sánchez Cerén, conceded in 1989 that the FMLN had carried out “massive and forced” recruitment in 1982 and 1983 and that killings of ORDEN members and suspected collaborators had damaged its standing (Marta Harnecker interview with Salvador Sánchez Cerén, 1989). The Commission found separately that the FMLN command had approved the execution of mayors.

In November 1989 a larger offensive forced the government to talk. Comandante Jesús Rojas described the aim:

“La ofensiva del 11 de noviembre no es algo aislado de lo que ha sido siempre nuestra estrategia.” (“The 11 November offensive is not something separate from what has always been our strategy.” My gloss.)

Source: Comandante Jesús Rojas, interview, 1990

Talks at La Palma, Ayagualo and San José led in the end to negotiations in Mexico.

What the accords promised#

On 16 January 1992 the government and the FMLN signed the Chapultepec Peace Agreement in Mexico City. It provided for a ceasefire, separation of forces and demobilisation of the guerrillas under UN verification, redefined the armed forces as subordinate to civilian authority, and abolished two security forces:

“The National Guard and the Treasury Police shall be abolished as public security forces and their members shall be incorporated into the army.”

Source: Chapultepec Peace Agreement, 16 January 1992

A civilian National Civil Police replaced them, a temporary panel vetted officers, and the question of impunity went to the Commission on the Truth.

Its 1993 report attributed roughly 85 per cent of complaints to state agents and death squads and about 5 per cent to the FMLN, figures that describe testimony rather than a count of all deaths. It found that the authorities had denied El Mozote and never investigated it. An amnesty law, which bars prosecution for past offences, closed the road to trials.

How historians have read it#

  1. Mark Danner, The Massacre at El Mozote: A Parable of the Cold War (1994). Source: penguinrandomhouse.com, Expands the 1993 New Yorker article with documents and further interviews; its argument is that the cover-up in Washington matters as much as the massacre. The standard work on the case; broadly accepted on the facts, debated on how far to generalise from it.
  2. UN Commission on the Truth, From Madness to Hope (1993). Source: derechos.org, Official, negotiated account; its method was confidential investigation rather than judicial proof. Later scholars rely on its case findings while criticising its limited time and its silence on many victims.
  3. Elisabeth Jean Wood, Insurgent Collective Action and Civil War in El Salvador (Cambridge University Press, 2003). Cambridge University Press: 70D35F82CEEB47F2F14957EE31B5BFD6, Uses oral histories from peasants who did and did not support the insurgency, plus interviews with commanders on both sides; argues that moral and emotional motives, not selective material benefits, sustained high-risk support. It complicates purely economic accounts and is the main scholarly counterweight to “false consciousness” or “coercion only” explanations.
  4. Leigh Binford, The El Mozote Massacre: Anthropology and Human Rights (University of Arizona Press, 1996). Bibliographic details verified in Wood’s references list; no separate link was opened. An anthropological study of the massacre and the politics of its denial; useful alongside Danner for the local, long-term view.
  5. Alberto Martín Álvarez, From Revolutionary War to Democratic Revolution: The FMLN in El Salvador (Berghof, 2010). Source: berghof-foundation.org, Based on thirty-five interviews with former commanders; traces the FMLN’s conversion into a political party. It is a participant-heavy, transition-studies reading, and should be set against accounts that stress continuity of military power after 1992.
  6. Joan Didion, “‘Something Horrible’ in El Salvador” (1994). Source: nybooks.com, Review essay that presses Danner’s argument about official language and self-deception, printing the embassy cables and officials’ later interviews. Sharp on the machinery of denial, less a work of archival scholarship than a piece of literary journalism.

Broad agreement: El Mozote happened; Atlacatl units carried it out; state forces and allied death squads account for the great majority of documented violations; US officials knew more than they admitted and continued aid. Live disagreement: the total number of dead; how much weight to give scarce versus moral motives for peasant support, where Wood stands against older political-economy accounts; whether the 1981 offensive was a strategic necessity or a miscalculation; and whether the peace accords transformed Salvadoran power or merely regularised it. The evidence above supports the first set firmly and leaves the second open.

Limits of the record#

The record is strongest on the disasters and weakest on ordinary political life. Survivors, foreign reporters and officials dominate it; rank-and-file guerrillas, conscript soldiers, women combatants and the families of FMLN victims appear at the edges. The Spanish-language party documents and commanders’ interviews at CeDeMA are participants’ words but elite and self-justifying, and need checking against rank-and-file testimony and against Wood.

No physical archive was visited. The Commission’s annexes, the forensic and photographic reports, press analysis, statistical analysis and victim lists, remain in Spanish at the UN Dag Hammarskjöld Library. Salvadoran human-rights archives, including Tutela Legal, COMADRES and Pro-Búsqueda, were not consulted. The full text of Romero’s 24 March homily was not opened, and the FMLN’s 1980 founding declaration was not located.

“La Libertad massacre” cannot be matched to a distinct event; the best-documented candidate is San Francisco Guajoyo, twelve executions on 29 May 1980, but the naming needs resolving. No reliable total for the war’s dead exists; the repeated 75,000 is an estimate. Most testimony is retrospective, and translation shapes the wording of nearly every quotation, so the Spanish should be kept beside each.
