Fourier, the Passions and the Phalanstery Experiments

In 1822 Charles Fourier set down the size of the community he wanted built.

In 1822 Charles Fourier set down the size of the community he wanted built. “An association of 1500 or 1600 people requires a site comprising at least one square league of land,” he wrote in Théorie de l’unité universelle (Charles Fourier, “The Establishment of a Trial Phalanx,” first published 1822, translated by Jonathan Beecher and Richard Bienvenu (1972)). He wanted water, hills, a forest, proximity to a city but enough distance to exclude intruders, and seven-eighths of the members to be cultivators and manufacturers. Capital would take the form of transferable shares, with profits divided among capital, labour and talent. The first phalanx should stand in a warm climate. A phalanx was his name for the association, a phalanstery for its building. The numbers fixed the scale that every later attempt had to meet or explain away.

Making work lovable#

Fourier was a commercial clerk in Besançon who published his first book in 1808 and spent his life elaborating a system of social organisation. Work, he argued, had not always been repulsive; it had been made so. “Morality teaches us to love work: let it know, then, how to render work lovable,” he wrote (Charles Fourier, “Attractive Labour,” translated by Julia Franklin (1901)). A rightly arranged association would pay partners by dividends, divide payment among capital, labour and talent, vary sessions about eight times a day, set friends to work with rivalries in clean workshops, divide labour minutely, allow free choice of occupation and guarantee a minimum. This is a design problem, not a description of anything achieved.

The design rested on a theory of the passions, Fourier’s word for the faculties people are born with, including those moralists call vices. Fourier wrote: “My theory confines itself to utilizing the passions now condemned, just as Nature has given them to us and without in any way changing them.” (Charles Fourier, “Of the Role of the Passions,” translated by Julia Franklin (1901)). The theological premise belongs to the argument: the passions are given by God and should be used, not repressed. In a group of about 1,600, contrasting series would form around different tasks and people would move between them. Series and groups were his terms for those teams. The number was a constant in the system, not a size tested by experiment.

His criticism of commerce was sharper than his predictions. Engels, who read Fourier’s unpublished manuscripts in La Phalange in 1845, selected a passage on trade and mocked “the conversion of the sea into lemonade, the couronnes boréale and australe, the anti-lion, and the conjunction of the planets” (Frederick Engels, introducing Fourier’s On Trade, 1845). That list is Engels’s summary of Fourier’s forecasts, not a word-for-word quotation. Engels thought the criticism of society better than the German philosophy he opposed, and omitted Fourier’s positive system. The praise and the mockery belong to a young communist defining his own position.

An experiment that never was#

Condé-sur-Vesgre fell far short of Fourier’s scale. His own explanation protected the theory. “If social experiments have miscarried, it is because some fatality has impelled all speculators to work with bodies of poor people whom they subjected to a monastic-industrial discipline,” he wrote in a posthumous manuscript (Charles Fourier, “The Phalanstery,” from the Manuscrits de Fourier (1851), translated by Julia Franklin (1901)). A trial phalanx, he said, had to contain a graduated range of fortunes.

Louis Reybaud, a journalist writing for an elite review, told the sequence differently in 1837. Baudet-Dulary and the Devay brothers pooled their estates, clearing began, funds ran short and the work stopped. Reybaud was not there and wrote to invite ridicule, but preserved the order of events. His verdict was “Il y avait eu avortement, mais il n’y avait pas eu essai.” In English, there had been a miscarriage but no experiment (Louis Reybaud, “Socialistes modernes. II. Charles Fourier,” 1837). Fourier asked for 1,500 to 1,600 members and a wealthy backer; the French attempt drew about 150 subscribers and two estates.

Failure did not end the movement. Giuseppe Ferrari reviewed Fourierist books in 1845, describing a press a mainstream journal took seriously while arguing that the school was dissolving into the radical party. Victor Considerant, Fourier’s leading disciple, defined the doctrine in 1847: “L’idée exacte ou scientifique de l’Association se compose de la combinaison intime de trois idées, l’idée de l’ordre, l’idée de la liberté, l’idée de la justice.” In English, the exact or scientific idea of Association combines order, liberty and justice (Victor Considerant, Bases de la politique positive, 1847). He distinguished a political reform that moves power between parties from a social reform that changes relations between interests. A manifesto is a self-description, and those words should not be extended to every Fourierist.

Groups and series in America#

Fourier’s categories entered American community constitutions. Brook Farm, at West Roxbury in Massachusetts, adopted them in 1844. Its directors, led by George Ripley, wrote that “we yield an unqualified assent to that doctrine of universal unity which Fourier teaches” (Brook Farm Association, Constitution … With an Introductory Statement, signed 18 January 1844). The constitution provided $100 shares, a five per cent return to capital, labour credits instead of wages, guarantees of food, housing, fuel and clothing, and industry arranged in agricultural, mechanical and domestic series. A later notice reported about 200 acres, roughly $30,000 of property and a 175-by-40-foot section of phalanstery under construction. The document called the association preparatory and was written to attract members and money.

The North American Phalanx near Red Bank in New Jersey met problems the theory did not mention. Charles Sears, a resident writing years later, recorded that the Albany branch formed in 1843, about $7,000 was subscribed and some 700 acres were bought for $14,000 with a mortgage. “three months had not expired before the president and his associate directors came to a dead-lock respecting the business administration,” he wrote (Charles Sears, The North American Phalanx: An Historical and Descriptive Sketch, written 1879, published 1886). The deadlock was resolved by adopting series and groups, with elected chiefs and six departments. Pay was graded by usefulness and repulsiveness of the task, suffrage was universal without distinction of sex, and an internal currency circulated. Sears wrote a quarter-century later for would-be colonists, so his figures are claims, not audited results.

One letter gives the daily texture constitutions omit. Mary Stiles Paul, a former Lowell mill worker staying at the Phalanx for about a year, wrote to her father on 7 May 1854: “I have worked about two hours each day for the Phalanx, three quarters in sweeping, one and a quarter in the dining hall, clearing & laying the tables.” (Mary Stiles Paul, letter of 7 May 1854). She described a fixed meal schedule, orchards outside her window and people remarkably kind; the manner of the meals seemed oddest. It was her first week, and she was reassuring her father. The letter says nothing about debt or governance.

A workers’ union and a woman’s place#

Flora Tristan proposed a different instrument: a mass workers’ union rather than a patron-funded village. Her Union ouvrière appeared in 1843. Publishers refused it, so she solicited subscriptions door to door; she reported that 1,500 copies sold mostly at 25 or 30 centimes and that workers sold copies to other workers. By 15 December she counted 237 letters from 87 workers or societies. The second edition printed responses from public figures, including Considerant, who called the Workers’ Palace a utopia and urged her not to pursue it. Tristan selected the favourable letters, and her arithmetic is her own.

Her case for linking the two emancipations rested on what she said she had seen: girls kept from school for household work, apprenticeship from about twelve, women paid roughly half a man’s wage, and a mother’s illiteracy passed to her children. “La femme (c’est la moitié de l’humanité) a été mise hors l’Église, hors la loi, hors la société,” she wrote. In English, woman, half of humanity, had been put outside the Church, outside the law, outside society (Flora Tristan, L’Union ouvrière, chapter III, 1843/1844). She proposed equal admission of girls and boys to the Workers’ Palaces and a declaration of women’s rights modelled on 1793. Her wage ratio and generalisations about French workers cannot be checked from her book alone.

Engels later wrote of Fourier that “He was the first to declare that in any given society the degree of woman’s emancipation is the natural measure of the general emancipation.” (Frederick Engels, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, 1880, Part I). That is Engels’s attribution, not a Fourier quotation, and Tristan made a similar argument in her own terms. In 1880 Engels placed Fourier, Saint-Simon and Owen together as the three great Utopians whose systems were foredoomed the more completely they were worked out. The label was a polemic inside socialism. The experiments it dismissed had already happened.

How historians have read it#

  • Frederick Engels, Socialism: Utopian and Scientific (1880). Part I places Fourier among the “three great Utopians” while praising his criticism of bourgeois society and his statement on women’s emancipation; the source of the standard Marxist framing. Marxists Internet Archive: soc utop ch01
  • Jonathan Beecher and Richard Bienvenu, translators and editors, The Utopian Vision of Charles Fourier: Selected Texts on Work, Love, and Passionate Attraction (1971; Jonathan Cape edition 1972). The edition behind the “Trial Phalanx” text used above; it selects and introduces texts rather than reprinting the complete works. Marxists Internet Archive: works ch19
  • Marcel van der Linden, editor, The Cambridge History of Socialism, chapter “Early Socialisms” (2022). Its further-reading list is the most convenient guide to the scholarly literature on the early socialisms. Cambridge University Press: A26108B4B0900A5C1AB2DAC17C331697
  • Jonathan Beecher, Charles Fourier: The Visionary and His World (1986). The standard scholarly biography, listed in the Cambridge chapter’s bibliography, and a counterweight to Engels’s dismissive portrait. Cambridge University Press: A26108B4B0900A5C1AB2DAC17C331697
  • Carl J. Guarneri, The Utopian Alternative: Fourierism in Nineteenth-Century America (1991). Listed in the same bibliography; the starting point for testing the American evidence against a wider set of colonies. Cambridge University Press: A26108B4B0900A5C1AB2DAC17C331697
  • Gregory Claeys, Machinery, Money and the Millennium: From Moral Economy to Socialism 1815–1860 (1987). Listed in the same bibliography; it places Fourier among the British and French socialisms of the same decades. Cambridge University Press: A26108B4B0900A5C1AB2DAC17C331697

Fourier’s importance to the history of socialist thought, his first book of 1808 and the collapse of the French Fourierist press around 1848 are not in serious dispute among these sources. How much of the failure came from Fourier’s own specifications and how much from lack of capital remains contested, as does whether Engels’s label describes what the movement was doing. The last three works are standard studies whose arguments are not summarised here; they are listed as guides to the scholarship.

Limits of the record#

Fourier’s own voice is abundant; the voices of those who joined his colonies are thin. No sustained first-hand account from a member of Condé-sur-Vesgre forms part of this record, and no minute book or letter from that colony survives here. The American evidence is richer but partial: Sears wrote a quarter-century later with a promotional purpose, and Paul’s letter covers her first week. Brook Farm’s constitution is an official public document addressed to potential members. The letters of participants such as Curtis and Hawthorne and Marianne Dwight Orvis’s memoir are not part of this record. Both contemporary journalists wrote for an elite review and had an interest in ridicule or in arguing that Fourierism was spent. Fourier’s 1829 Le Nouveau Monde industriel and Tristan’s Le Tour de France were located but could not be consulted; the French texts used here are transcriptions, one still marked as awaiting validation, and the scans behind them were not checked page by page. The English Fourier excerpts are translations, in two cases taken from posthumous manuscript selections whose original chapter and date are not given, so the exact French wording remains unconfirmed. Claims about death rates, wage ratios, member numbers and the date of the Phalanx’s dissolution need corroboration. Whether Fourier’s sexual theories were included or excluded at Condé-sur-Vesgre is raised by a recent local-history account but is not established by any first-hand source here.
