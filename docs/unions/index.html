The Workshop and the Union: Workers' Control from the Clyde to Chile

At the congress of the Confédération générale du travail at Amiens in 1906, the vote on a charter was 830 for, eight against and one abstention.

At the congress of the Confédération générale du travail at Amiens in 1906, the vote on a charter was 830 for, eight against and one abstention. Roughly forty names were appended with their trades. The document declared that the CGT, the French General Confederation of Labour, stood outside “all political schools” and united workers “conscious of the fight to be carried out for the disappearance of the salaried and of employers”. It said that “economic action must be carried out directly against the bosses, the confederated organizations not having to involve themselves, as a union group, with parties and sects” (Charter of Amiens, CGT, 1906, translated by Mitch Abidor. Marxists Internet Archive: charter amiens). It committed the union to immediate improvements and to complete emancipation through the expropriation of the capitalists and the general strike, a strike extended across whole industries rather than one workplace. On the union’s future role it concluded: “it advocates as a method of action the general strike; and it considers that the union, today a resistance group will be, in the future, a group for production and redistribution” (Charter of Amiens, CGT, 1906, translated by Mitch Abidor. Marxists Internet Archive: charter amiens). This was syndicalism: the belief that trade unions, rather than political parties, were the instrument of workers’ emancipation. It was a statement of principle, not a handbook for running production, and the CGT’s later participation in wartime and postwar bodies is a separate question. The English wording is a volunteer translation and would need checking against the French.

“We will act independently”#

In the Clyde region of Scotland during the First World War, shop stewards built a different institution. A shop steward is a worker elected by fellow workers to represent them at the workplace. A strike in February 1915 and the imprisonment of three Govan shipwrights led to the Clyde Workers’ Committee, a body of delegates from all trades. A leaflet signed by its president, William Gallacher, and secretary, J. M. Messer, set out the committee’s position: “We will support the officials just ‘so long as they rightly represent the workers, but we will act independently immediately they misrepresent them.” (William Gallacher, president, and J. M. Messer, secretary, Clyde Workers’ Committee leaflet, issued June–August 1915. Marxists Internet Archive: clyde committee) It listed ten trades, from toolmakers to school teachers, and claimed to represent the rank and file. The officials in question led the ASE, the engineers’ craft union, whose leaders had supported the Munitions Act. “The support given to the Munitions Act by the Officials was an act of Treachery to the Working Class.” (same leaflet, 1915. Marxists Internet Archive: clyde committee)

The dispute centred on dilution, the wartime relaxation of craft rules that let less-trained workers into jobs previously reserved for skilled men. Gallacher wrote that “the workers were left with the impression that the ‘dilution of Labour’ was simply a pretext for the ‘deluding of Labour’” (William Gallacher, “Prepare for Action”, The Worker no. 1, 8 January 1916. Marxists Internet Archive: prepare action). He reported that 20 of 24 or 25 invited unions refused to pack Lloyd George’s meeting at St Andrew’s Hall, and that when the committee put an alternative scheme of industrial organisation to the minister, he replied: “It would be a revolution… and it can’t be done.” (William Gallacher, “Prepare for Action”, The Worker no. 1, 8 January 1916. Marxists Internet Archive: prepare action) In a second article Gallacher stated the positive aim: “the ultimate aim of the Clyde Workers’ Committee is to weld these unions into one powerful organisation that will place the workers in complete control of the industry” (William Gallacher, “A Call to All Workers”, The Worker no. 4, 29 January 1916. Marxists Internet Archive: call workers).

The committee was constituted in October 1915 with 250 to 300 delegates elected directly from workplaces, meeting weekly; by January 1916 it directed workers in 29 Clydeside engineering works. When the Dilution Commission refused to recognise it as a negotiating party, shop stewards at Beardmores were denied access to new dilutees from 14 March 1916. A strike began on 17 March and had spread to three other factories by 21 March. On 24 March the government deported Kirkwood and three other Beardmores stewards, with more on 28 March. The ASE refused them union benefit. The Forty Hours strike of January 1919 brought a confrontation at George Square on 31 January, remembered as Bloody Friday, and a 47-hour week was settled on 10 February. The famous sentence about supporting officials is worded differently in the Marxists Internet Archive leaflet and in the Strathclyde archive’s reproduction, so the leaflet’s text is not stable across versions.

Organs of struggle or organs of the state?#

The same question was argued in Germany after the November 1918 revolution. Workers’ and soldiers’ councils spread across the country. A later account by the Group of International Communists, written in 1938 by people in the council-communist current, recalled that “While this kind of organization was unknown in practice, within four days a vast network of workers and soldiers councils covered Germany.” (Group of International Communists, The Origins of the Movement for Workers’ Councils in Germany, first published 1938, retrospective participant account. Marxists Internet Archive: workers councils) In Hamburg on 16 August 1919, the communist Fritz Wolffheim told a party assembly that the German unions had been built for an expanding capitalism and had become an essential part of the restored state. He distinguished the state-recognised enterprise councils from revolutionary factory councils. “As long as the class State exists, the proletariat is at war, and the factory councils must be organs of the revolutionary struggle.” (Fritz Wolffheim, speech, Hamburg, 16 August 1919, translated primary. Marxists Internet Archive: 08 16) His call was “Get out of the trade unions, Create factory organizations!” (Fritz Wolffheim, same speech, 1919. Marxists Internet Archive: 08 16). Wolffheim belonged to the council left and was later expelled from the KPD; his speech is evidence for what one current argued, not for what most workers did. The GIK pamphlet describes the councils as spontaneous and then captured by parties and unions, and dates their legal emasculation to the law of 4 February 1920. Its account is partisan, and a social-democratic defence of that law is not present here.

At international level the tension took another form. In February 1945, 204 delegates representing 60 million workers met in London and declared: “Organized labour, with so great a part in winning the war, cannot leave to others – however well intentioned they may be – the sole responsibility of making the peace.” (Manifesto of the World Trade Union Conference, London, February 1945, as quoted by the WFTU’s official history. Source: wftucentral.org) The First World Trade Union Congress followed in Paris on 3 to 8 October 1945. Its constitution aimed “to combat war and the causes of war” and it passed a resolution on colonial self-determination. The congress also exposed a fault line. S. A. Dange argued that Indian workers joined the international movement for national independence; Walter Citrine of the British TUC, the Trades Union Congress, replied that the federation was not the “medium” for that and warned that politics would destroy it. Four years later the federation split over the Marshall Plan and the Cold War. The British TUC letter of 19 January 1949, Deakin’s walkout and James B. Carey’s description of the federation as a “corpse” preceded the formation in December 1949 of the ICFTU, the International Confederation of Free Trade Unions. The WFTU’s own history blames “imperialist and reactionary forces” and insists that the organisations which now compose it never intended to divide the world trade union movement. It is a defence of continuity written by one of the parties.

The plan and the cordon#

Yugoslavia tried to answer the council question with a law. The Basic Law of 26 June 1950 provided that state economic enterprises were to be managed by workers’ collectives “in the name of the community, within the scope of the state economic plan”, through elected and recallable workers’ councils and management boards. “The workers’ council, and its individual members, may be recalled before their term is up.” (Basic Law on Management of State Economic Enterprises by the Workers’ Collectives, Yugoslav Federal Assembly, 26 June 1950, translated primary. Marxists Internet Archive: x01) Councils approved plans, elected and recalled boards and distributed remaining accumulation. At least three quarters of management-board members had to be workers directly engaged in production. The director, however, was appointed by a higher association or state organ, sat on the board and could delay decisions he judged contrary to the plan. A law records an intention, not what happened at meetings. Speaking to the First Congress of Workers’ Councils on 25 June 1957, Tito claimed that “This was a period of schooling on a giant scale, when one third of the workers employed, that is to say more than six hundred thousand workers and employees, participated in management.” (Josip Broz Tito, speech to the First Congress of Workers’ Councils, 25 June 1957. Marxists Internet Archive: 06 25) He also conceded: “We are aware that here and there certain people in leading positions in the enterprises deny these rights to the workers councils; and the workers in turn have given in too easily to such improper treatment” (Tito, same speech, 1957. Marxists Internet Archive: 06 25). His figure had no stated method. His admission about enterprise officials is evidence from the system’s own head that formal powers were contested in practice.

In Chile the institutions were built in the factories rather than by statute. During the bosses’ strike of October 1972, workers set up cordons, bodies linking the factories of one industrial district. Luis Angel Fernández Hermana, an observer who stayed in Chile until the coup, wrote that “Each cordon was directed by an executive, elected in the factory assemblies, whose job was to co-ordinate all the tasks in the sector.” (Luis Angel Fernández Hermana, “Chile: Workers’ Struggle Under Allende”, International Socialism 66, February 1974; eyewitness account. Marxists Internet Archive: fernandez) He named Mapocho, Alameda Station, O’Higgins, Macul, Cerrillos and Vicuña MacKenna in Santiago. “Production was directed by the workers, much vaunted ‘commercial secrecy’ was destroyed with the taking over of the accounting books” (Fernández Hermana, same article, 1974. Marxists Internet Archive: fernandez). Goods moved directly from factory to consumer, and neighbourhood price-control juntas were linked to the cordons through co-ordinating committees and Communal Commands. The cordons were later squeezed between the government, the CUT, the Chilean union federation, and the Communist Party, and occupied industries were returned before the coup. This is one partisan eyewitness, written from the International Socialist opposition a few months after the events. It names no rank-and-file worker and gives no assembly minutes, so neither the scale nor the duration of any single cordon is established.

How historians have read it#

  • Dario Azzellini, ed., An Alternative Labour History: worker control and workplace democracy (2015). Source: openlibrary.org A comparative collection that sets workplace self-management against both state socialism and union bureaucracy; its chapter on Chile places the cordones in a global frame.
  • Branko Horvat, Political Economy of Socialism: A Marxist View (1982). Source: openlibrary.org A Yugoslav economist’s defence of self-management as a distinct socialist political economy, written from inside the system he analyses. It is the clearest scholarly statement of the claim Tito made politically; compare Ward’s 1958 critique of the labour-managed firm, which argues that such firms tend to under-employ labour.
  • World Federation of Trade Unions, The World Federation of Trade Unions, 1945–1985 (Prague, 1985; issued 1989). Internet Archive: dudeman5685 yahoo WFTU An institutional history with a constitution, a congress list and descriptions of the trade union internationals. It is a primary source for what the WFTU wanted remembered, not an independent account of why it split.
  • Group of International Communists, The Origins of the Movement for Workers’ Councils in Germany (1938; English editions 1968, 1994). Marxists Internet Archive: workers councils Both a participant document and a historiographical position: it argues that councils were a mass, non-leadership form destroyed by social democracy and the unions, and rejects any account that treats the 1920 Works Councils Act as a step forward.
  • “The Charter of Amiens” (1906) and the WFTU constitution (1945, as amended). Marxists Internet Archive: charter amiens and Source: wftucentral.org Primary statements of principle rather than scholarship. They define the two poles, union independence from parties and international class-based unity, against which historians measure what unions actually did.

Broad agreement covers the dates and texts: the 1906 charter, the 1915 to 1916 Clyde leaflets, the German council laws of 1918 to 1920, the 1950 Yugoslav law and the WFTU split of 1949 are not in serious dispute. Live disagreement concerns interpretation: whether workplace councils were a revolutionary alternative or a management tool, whether the Yugoslav law transferred power or institutionalised a managerial class, and whether the Chilean cordones were a viable embryo of workers’ power or a short-lived response to a bosses’ strike. The sources above do not settle these questions, and the position of each writer should be kept visible.

Limits of the record#

The most serious gap is a genuine rank-and-file voice. Nearly every surviving source was written by a leader, an organiser or a state figure. The Clyde leaflets claim to speak for shop stewards but are signed by the committee’s president and secretary; Tito addresses delegates rather than speaking from the shop floor; the Chilean account names no worker. The Pact of Rome of June 1944, the founding text of the Italian CGIL, is reproduced on Italian Wikipedia in an article flagged as unsourced, and the 1994 edition to which it points has not been consulted. No CGIL congress proceedings or text by Di Vittorio is available here. Postwar German co-determination is another blank: the German evidence stops at 1920, with nothing from the 1951 coal and steel law or the 1972 Works Constitution Act. The WFTU’s founding proceedings are held at Cornell but access is restricted, so only the finding aid is available. The CGT charter and the Wolffheim speech were read in English by unnamed or volunteer translators, and the Yugoslav law was translated by the transcriber rather than by its author. Originals in Italian, French, German and Serbo-Croatian have not been checked. The Strathclyde archive holds images of The Worker and the 1919 leaflets that have not been transcribed, while the originals are held at the National Archives, the TUC Library and the Gallacher Memorial Library. Several Chilean items, among them the 1973 “Workers’ Control: Its Structure Under Allende”, are PDFs that could not be read. Closing these gaps would need a funded archive visit and a translator.
