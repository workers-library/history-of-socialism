Nasser and the Revolution from Above: Egypt, 1952–1970

The broadcast of 23 July 1952 was careful to sound like an instruction rather than a coup.

The broadcast of 23 July 1952 was careful to sound like an instruction rather than a coup. The Free Officers said in the name of the army: “Egypt has recently been going through a period marked by corruption, bribery, and governmental instability.” (Statement of the Revolution, 23 July 1952, English translation on the Bibliotheca Alexandrina site). The Free Officers were a group of army officers who took power in the name of reform. They assured Egyptians that detained officers were unharmed, warned against violence and promised foreign residents that the army would protect them. The statement shows how deliberately they presented themselves as disciplined guardians. Within months their chairman, Gamal Abdel Nasser, was writing an explanation of why they had acted.

Two revolutions#

Nasser wrote The Philosophy of the Revolution in 1953 and 1954, after the officers had consolidated power. He treated the Abdeen Palace incident of 1942, the war of 1948 and the scandal of defective arms as accelerants rather than causes. The old parties, in his telling, had forfeited any right to lead. “I can now say that we are at present in the throes of two revolutions and not one.” (The Philosophy of the Revolution, written 1953–54, translator unnamed in the scanned edition). Political liberation and social revolution were to be carried out together by the same officers in the same decade. He recorded his shock that crowds did not rise behind the vanguard after Farouk’s abdication. In letters to his friend Hassan El-Nashar written before the revolution, the young officer said the army had stopped talking about “women and amusement” and had begun talking about “sacrifice and dignity” (letters, including that of 16 February 1942). This is a participant’s retrospective and a set of edited extracts from a family-linked archive. The claim that the masses were absent is contested.

The canal and the invasion#

On 26 July 1956 Nasser told a crowd in Manshiya Square, Alexandria, that the Suez Canal Company had always been an Egyptian company. The canal was the waterway linking the Mediterranean to the Red Sea, and the company that operated it was the focus of Egyptian grievance. “Everything which was stolen from us by that imperialist company, that state within a state, when we were dying of hunger, we are going to take back” (nationalisation speech, 26 July 1956, retranslated from the Arabic by Anne Alexander, 2006). The name de Lesseps was the signal that started the seizure of the company’s offices. A decree read that evening set up an Egyptian authority under the Ministry of Commerce, ordered employees to stay at their posts and promised compensation at the previous day’s Paris Bourse price. The US State Department’s record of the same day notes that officials took over the administration while Nasser spoke (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1955–1957, vol. XVI, document 1). In September, with invasion still ahead, he said: “Egypt insists her sovereignty must remain intact and refuses to give up any part of that sovereignty for the sake of money.” (speech of 15 September 1956, official US State Department translation).

Israel attacked Egypt on 29 October 1956, Anglo-French bombing began on 31 October, and British and French troops landed on 5 November. The voices that survive from the resistance are not only those of the president. Fathallah Mahrus, a factory worker and trade unionist, recalled: “So the popular resistance against the invasion was led by the people of Port Said – women and children as well – armed with cooking pans, kitchen knives, walking sticks and anything they could find.” (interviewed in Cairo, 25 March 2006, translated in Anne Alexander’s article). Layla al-Shal, a Cairo University student and activist, said: “We set up women’s resistance committees across the capital. We had military-style training; how to defuse a bomb, how to shoot a rifle and so on.” (interviewed 31 March 2006). Communist activists trained workers in a vacant lot beside the Alexandria textile mills and set aside their opposition to Nasser in the face of the invasion. Yusuf Darwish later said Nasser “used the Communists as guard-dogs”. Recorded fifty years later by a writer of the same political tendency, they are valuable but not representative.

Socialism defined in Cairo#

The July 1961 laws transferred what the state called the major part of production equipment to the public sector, that part of the economy owned and run by the state. The Charter of 1962 gave the experiment a name. “Socialism has become both a means and an end, namely sufficiency and justice.” (The Charter, 1962, official English translation). The Charter restated the six principles of the 1952 revolution, set the triad “Freedom, Socialism, Unity” and created the Arab Socialist Union, the party through which the working forces were to be organised. It said explicitly that public control of the means of production did not require nationalising everything, or abolishing private ownership or inheritance. That qualification matters: Nasser’s socialism was not a simple doctrine of total state ownership. The Charter blamed the failed revolution of 1919 on a leadership that ignored social change, the regime’s own account of its past.

The Dam and the setback#

The Aswan High Dam was the regime’s proof that the seizure of the canal had paid for development. Speaking beside Habib Bourguiba in February 1965, Nasser said: “The High Dam is a symbol of great significance; it is a symbol of moral struggle, a symbol of the abolition of imperialism, a symbol of determination.” (Aswan, 18 February 1965, official UAR Information Department translation). His figures were 1.5 million feddans gained, a local measure of area, 700,000 converted to perennial irrigation and 10 billion kilowatt hours of electricity. Of a cost of £E400 million, he said, £E100 million came from two Soviet loans and £E300 million from the Egyptian budget. He called the workforce “an army” and “a register of technical experience”. The workers do not speak in this speech, and the displacement of Nubian communities by the reservoir is absent from it. Interviews with dam workers were recorded in Aswan in 2009 and 2010, but none of their words appears in this record.

In June 1967, after the war with Israel, Nasser went on radio and television: “We cannot hide from ourselves that we have faced a serious setback in recent days” (resignation broadcast, 9 June 1967, translated from the original Arabic audio in 2024). He took personal responsibility, invoked Article 110 of the 1964 provisional constitution and handed the presidency to Zakaria Mohieddin. It was here that the war is attributed by scholarship to Mohamed Hassanein Heikal, the setback. He said Israel had attacked with American and British assistance; that remains his allegation under defeat. In September the Arab heads of state at Khartoum answered with “no peace with Israel, no recognition of Israel, no negotiations with it” (Khartoum Resolutions, 1 September 1967), while agreeing to resume oil pumping and to create an economic and social development fund. The formula was agreed; its implementation is another question.

Limits of the record#

Presidents, officers, a party charter and a state information department supply most of the words, all from people who made decisions and later defended them. Every Nasser speech here is a translation, in two cases made decades after the event, and the Arabic originals of the 1956 speech and the 1967 broadcast were not read in a usable form. The exceptions are the interviews of 2006 with a lawyer, a worker, a student and a journalist, all left-wing activists questioned by a writer of similar politics. They give Port Said a human scale but are memories half a century old. Farmers whose land was reformed, Nubians moved for the reservoir, Soviet specialists on the site and communists imprisoned after 1954 do not speak for themselves. The 1953 to 1955 trial transcripts, the 1961 nationalisation decrees, the Revolutionary Command Council minutes and the Arab Socialist Union committee sessions remain outside this record. No source says how many died building the Dam, how the £E400 million was raised, or what became of the workers and peasants promised half the seats in the People’s Assembly. Inference is no substitute.

How historians have read it#

  • John Waterbury, The Egypt of Nasser and Sadat: The Political Economy of Two Regimes (Princeton University Press, 1983/84). The standard political-economy account of what the public sector actually did; it supplies institutional detail the Charter lacks and is more sceptical about the regime’s claimed results. Internet Archive: egyptofnassersad0000wate
  • Joel Beinin and Zachary Lockman, Workers on the Nile: Nationalism, Communism, Islam and the Egyptian Working Class, 1882–1954 (I.B. Tauris, 1987). Establishes the independent labour movement the Free Officers inherited and then dismantled; its evidence is the main counterweight to Nasser’s claim that the masses were absent.
  • Anne Alexander, “Suez and the high tide of Arab nationalism”, International Socialism 2:112 (Autumn 2006). Argues that resistance in 1956 came from below, through Communist networks and popular committees, and that Nasser’s revolution from above weakened the state’s own capacity by demobilising those networks. Its Trotskyist framing should be kept in view. Marxists Internet Archive: suez
  • Alia Mossallam, “‘We are the ones who made this dam High!’: A builders’ history of the Aswan High Dam”, Water History 6 (2014), 297–314. Uses interviews with the dam’s workers, technicians and engineers and their own idioms and poetry to set pride against loss; its endnotes record interviews in Aswan in 2009–10. Only the abstract and references were available for this account. Source: link.springer.com
  • Tom Little, High Dam at Aswan: The Subjugation of the Nile (John Day, New York, 1965). A contemporary account written as the project neared completion, useful as a near-contemporary description, though its framing predates later environmental and Nubian-displacement criticism. Internet Archive: highdamataswansu01litt
  • Mohamed Hassanein Heikal, Cutting the Lion’s Tail: Suez through Egyptian Eyes (André Deutsch, 1986; Arbor House, 1987). A participant-adviser’s retrospective on Egyptian decision-making in 1956; Alexander relies on it for the dispute between Amer and Nasser. Internet Archive: cuttinglionstail00hayk

Broad agreement: the officers of 1952 dismantled independent organisations before building state ones; the 1956 nationalisation was a decisive political victory; the Dam was the central development project; 1967 was a genuine military catastrophe. Live disagreement: whether the revolution from above expressed or displaced popular politics, and how far the socialist decrees changed ownership rather than merely renaming it.
