Epeli Hauʻofa: Oceania, Development and the Sea of Islands

A research profile of the Tongan-Fijian writer's critique of development discourse and his account of Pacific identity and resistance.

Epeli Hauʻofa (1939–2009) wrote against two habits of thought that he saw as connected. The first was the habit of describing the islands of the Pacific as small, remote and dependent. The second was what this profile calls development discourse: the language and assumptions through which aid workers, planners, academics and governments described those islands and prescribed what should be done to them. Hauʻofa used the word Oceania for the region as he understood it, a connected world of ocean, kinship and movement rather than a collection of isolated pieces of land. In 1998 he wrote that “anyone who has lived in our region and is committed to Oceania is an Oceanian”, and he called the official categories “Pacific Islands Region” and “Pacific Islanders” as redundant as “South Seas” and “South Sea Islanders” (Hauʻofa 1998). His best-known essay argued that “There is a world of difference between viewing the Pacific as ‘islands in a far sea’ and as ‘a sea of islands’” (Hauʻofa, 1994 text). This profile sets out that argument, the institutions he built, and the objections other Pacific scholars made against it.

Biography#

Epeli Toʻimuli Hauʻofa was born in 1939 at Somona in the Louisiades, in Milne Bay province of what was then the Territory of Papua. Thomas Malm, who knew him and quotes his own writing, records that he was the second of five children of the Wesleyan missionary Reverend ʻIsikeli (1908–1995) and Mele (1909–1974), and that he grew up among Dobu and Mekeo people far from his parents’ Tongan homeland (Malm 2026). The University of Hawaiʻi Press gives the years 1939–2009 and says he was born in Papua New Guinea and educated in Papua New Guinea, Tonga, Fiji, Australia and Canada (UH Press). Colleagues at the University of the South Pacific later described him as “Tongan by name, Papuan by birth, Tongan, Fijian, Canadian and Australian by upbringing and education, and Fijian by citizenship” (USP memorial). The more precise birthday of 7 December 1939 could not be verified from a primary or institutional record opened for this profile.

Hauʻofa gave his own account of this beginning to Nicholas Thomas in 2006. His parents went to Papua New Guinea in 1937 or 1938, and he did not know he was Tongan until he was seven or eight: “I grew up as a Papuan.” The family left in September 1947, travelled through Madang, Townsville, Magnetic Island, Sydney and New Caledonia, and reached Tonga in January 1949. “While we were traveling, we all spoke Dobuan. We were still Papuans.” He spent seven years in Tonga, then four years at Lelean Memorial School in Fiji, then studied history at the University of New England in Armidale from 1960. A scholarship took him to McGill University in Montreal, where sociology and anthropology were in one department; he took a master’s degree in 1968 after fieldwork in Trinidad. He worked as a senior tutor at the University of Papua New Guinea, then took a PhD scholarship at the Australian National University and did fieldwork among the Mekeo. When his parents retired to Tonga he followed them, intending to stay two years; he stayed seven, revised his thesis for publication and began writing fiction. Afterwards he moved to Suva (Thomas’s 2006 interview).

At the University of the South Pacific he became professor of sociology and head of the School of Social and Economic Development, a position from which he commissioned responses from colleagues to his 1993 essay. Ratu Joni Madraiwiwi recalled that Hauʻofa spent a few years in the late 1970s as deputy to Tonga’s King Tāufaʻāhau Tupou IV, and that he lived at Wainadoi outside Suva (Wesley-Smith, editor, 2010). He married Barbara Brown, an Australian, in 1966 (Malm 2026). In 1997 the university appointed him first director of the new Oceania Centre for Arts and Culture. USP’s own history says the Centre was set up that year as a special project of the University Council and that Hauʻofa was appointed first Director “supported by a Programme Assistant and a Cleaner” (USP Centre history). In his own account, the university had intended a tourist-oriented centre modelled on the Polynesian Cultural Center in Hawaiʻi; he used the post instead to build a regional arts project, and said that literature was left out because the language and literature department had that remit (Thomas’s 2006 interview). No institution called a “Pacific Writers’ Centre” appears in any record opened for this profile: the verified foundation was the Oceania Centre for Arts and Culture, an arts and performance centre whose founder said literature was outside its remit. He died in Suva on 11 January 2009 (UH Press memoriam; Wesley-Smith 2010).

Intellectual tradition#

Hauʻofa was an anthropologist by training who progressively turned away from anthropology’s professional forms. In the 2006 interview he said that at the Australian National University he found the discipline “too far above the things that I was interested in, the ordinary life of people”, that what lasted was fieldwork and ethnography, and that a 1975 paper called “Anthropology and Pacific Islanders” was a critique of what anthropologists were doing in the Pacific. A senior colleague’s remark that he was “biting the hand that feeds you” marked the cost of that position (Thomas’s 2006 interview). He told Juniper Ellis in 1999 that “many in these fields are careless in writing and use a great deal of obfuscation. My writing is for the people.” He described his audience as “the people of Oceania, talking to them. Not excluding others, but that’s the kind of conversation- or monologue- that I’m addressing to people” (Ellis 2001).

He resisted the labels available to him. In “The Ocean in Us” he argued that “anyone who has lived in our region and is committed to Oceania is an Oceanian”, and called “Pacific Islands Region” and “Pacific Islanders” as redundant as “South Seas” and “South Sea Islanders” (Hauʻofa 1998). Madraiwiwi reported that Hauʻofa would have preferred “Oceanian” to “Pacific Islander” (Wesley-Smith 2010). He was equally explicit that a regional identity must not erase difference: “I am not in any way suggesting cultural homogeneity for our region. Such a thing is neither possible nor desirable” (Hauʻofa 1998). Sudesh Mishra called his stance “celebratory resistance”, a phrase Teaiwa repeated when reviewing the collection of responses to his essay (Teaiwa 1996).

His satire was part of the same method rather than a separate pastime. He told Ellis that every one of the Tales of the Tikongs was written in anger except “The Wages of Sin”, and that “it’s a very Tongan thing to use that medium to put through your critique of society. You never criticize people directly. It has to be devious” (Ellis 2001).

Major contributions#

His best-known contribution was to reverse the image of the Pacific. In the essay first published in 1993 and reprinted in 1994 he wrote: “There is a world of difference between viewing the Pacific as ‘islands in a far sea’ and as ‘a sea of islands’.” The first view stressed “dry surfaces in a vast ocean far from the centres of power” and therefore smallness and remoteness; the second was “a more holistic perspective in which things are seen in the totality of their relationships” (Hauʻofa, 1994 text). He rooted the contrast in Tongan usage: people from the outer islands were called kakai mei tahi, “people from the sea”, which for him showed that the sea was understood as home. He rejected the idea that islanders were naturally confined: “Smallness is a state of mind.” Nineteenth-century imperialism, he argued, drew boundaries that contracted a formerly open Oceania; post-war migration was enlarging it again through kinship networks, trade in goods such as kava, and remittances that economists counted as dependency (Ibid.).

The second contribution was a critique of the language of development. He described how teaching students that their countries were hopeless forced him to ask: “Is this not what neocolonialism is all about? To make people believe that they have no choice but to depend?” He confessed that he had been “actively participating in our own belittlement” (Ibid.). The target was not only outside the region. He argued that aid workers, planners, academics and governments were “involved directly or indirectly in the fields of aided development and Pacific rim geopolitics, for whose purposes it is necessary to portray our huge world in tiny, needy bits” (Ibid.). The publisher’s description of We Are the Ocean summarises the range: writing “over the past three decades has consistently challenged prevailing notions about Oceania and prescriptions for its development” (UH Press).

A third contribution was the proposal for a regional identity grounded in the ocean rather than in shared ancestry. “The sea is our single common heritage”, he wrote in 1998, because land had been divided and disputed while open water remained common; the role of Oceanians was to be “custodians of the ocean” (Hauʻofa 1998). A fourth was institutional. He built the Oceania Centre as a place where young artists, musicians and dancers could work without the constraints either of village conformity or of a Western fine-arts curriculum. Its principles, in his 2005 address, were reciprocity, cooperation, openness to community, transmission by participation, locally made criteria of value, and constant experiment (Hauʻofa 2005).

Analysis of capitalism#

Hauʻofa did not write a theory of capitalist exploitation in the Marxist sense. His analysis was of development discourse, colonial extraction and the political economy of smallness. In “Our Sea of Islands” he held out the possibility that a deterministic view of small island states would consign people “to a perpetual state of wardship wherein they and their surrounding lands and seas would be at the mercy of the manipulators of the global economy and ‘world orders’ of one kind or another” (Hauʻofa, 1994 text). He insisted that remittances were not a one-way flow of dependence: migrants earned the money by hard labour in the countries that needed them, while relatives at home reciprocated with goods, land and homes. “This is not dependence but interdependence”, he wrote (Ibid.).

In “The Ocean in Us” he set the regional identity he wanted against the market. “In an age when our societies are preoccupied with the pursuit of material wealth, when the rampant market economy brings out unquenchable greed and amorality in us”, he wrote, institutions of learning had to build corrective mechanisms (Hauʻofa 1998). He traced how outside naming had remade the region: “South Seas” paradise, then the Cold War “South Pacific” of Western security, then the “Pacific Islands Region” of “naked, neocolonial dependency”, and finally exclusion from APEC, where the islands “do not exist”. He used Banaba and Bikini as evidence that powerful states could use the region with minimal political cost, and warned that toxic waste and nuclear testing would continue for the same reason (Ibid.). In the 1999 interview he returned to the environmental point: the big powers “have used us in ways that they cannot use places elsewhere because of political consequences” (Ellis 2001).

His 2005 address gives the sharpest statement of domination. “Today, the main pressure that we confront is globalisation, the rules for which are set so far away that they remain beyond the reach of our influence.” Superior standards had been imposed on Pacific societies for two centuries; tourism turned culture into a commodity; the university concentrated on “harnessing our responses to the demands of global capitalism” and sought external approval against Western “international standards”. The alternative he proposed was not a state plan but a space in which Oceanian artists set their own rules: “In them we enter other people’s fields, other people’s rules, other people’s control. But we do not have to always play in other people’s fields” (Hauʻofa 2005).

Analysis of socialism#

Hauʻofa wrote no socialist blueprint and should be described as having none. What he offered was an argument about agency, community and the limits of class categories imported from elsewhere. “Our Sea of Islands” distinguishes the level of governments, diplomats, banks and consultants from the level of “ordinary people, peasants and proletarians”, and declares that the vision in the essay rests on grassroots behaviour (Hauʻofa, 1994 text). Yet the mechanism of liberation he describes is kinship and reciprocity rather than class organisation. He calls reciprocity “the core of all Oceanic cultures”, and treats the circulation of goods through family networks as a form of interdependence that economists miss (Ibid.). Vijay Naidu, a colleague and development sociologist, wrote that Hauʻofa’s 1987 paper “The New South Pacific Society” had reinforced his own understanding of elites and classes in the region and noted that ordinary islanders were taking matters into their own hands rather than waiting for leaders (Wesley-Smith 2010).

His relation to Marxism was distant and specific. In “The Ocean in Us” he wrote that “the introduction in the early 1980s of neo-Marxism” at USP was hostile to localism and regionalism: “Nationalism and regionalism were bourgeois attempts to prevent the international unity of the working classes.” He added that neo-Marxism disappeared from campus after the 1987 military coups in Fiji, along with the Pacific Way (Hauʻofa 1998). This is a report of an intellectual climate, not an endorsement of the doctrine. Douglas Borer read the 1993 essay in the opposite way, arguing that Hauʻofa had revived “Marx’s false dream of an international class consciousness” as a pan-Pacific cultural consciousness (Teaiwa 1996, quoting Borer). The disagreement should be left open: Hauʻofa set community, reciprocity and cultural creativity against the market, but he did not design socialist institutions.

Recommended reading#

These five selections are starting points rather than a complete account of the work.

  • “Our Sea of Islands” (1993/1994) — the short text that sets out the “sea of islands” image and the critique of development discourse; the argument in miniature.
  • “The Ocean in Us” (1998) — explains how the ocean could become a shared basis for regional identity without demanding cultural sameness.
  • We Are the Ocean: Selected Works (2008) — the publisher’s collection brings the essays, fiction and poetry together and shows how the arguments connect.
  • Tales of the Tikongs (1983) — the satire that Hauʻofa said he wrote in anger, showing the “development game” through local characters rather than policy language.
  • “The Development of Contemporary Oceanic Arts” (2005) — his clearest account of what the Oceania Centre was for and why cultural creativity was, for him, a form of resistance.

Selected lectures and interviews#

One audiovisual item is verified by an institutional record: Our Sea of Islands: Epeli Hau`ofa, 53 minutes, colour, half-inch VHS, released in 2003 and produced by the East-West Center and the University of Hawaiʻi’s Center for Pacific Islands Studies. The record gives a distributor but no playable online file (Center for Pacific Islands Studies). Several lectures are documented in print rather than by a recording. The footnote to “Our Sea of Islands” says the paper grew out of lectures at the University of Hawaiʻi at Hilo and the East-West Center in Honolulu in March and April 1993, and Hauʻofa told Thomas that the Kona talk went to the annual meeting of the Association of Social Anthropologists of Oceania and the Hilo talk was written after the drive between them (Hauʻofa, 1994 text; Thomas’s 2006 interview). “The Ocean in Us” is dated 12 March 1997 as an Oceania Lecture at USP, with an earlier keynote in Copenhagen on 13–15 December 1996 (Hauʻofa 1998). Two print interviews are primary material: Juniper Ellis’s conversation at the Oceania Centre on 9 July 1999, with composer Sailasa Tora joining for part of it, and Nicholas Thomas’s recorded conversation in London in April–May 2006, published in 2012 (Ellis 2001; Thomas, 2012).

Intellectual influences#

The clearest evidence of influence is Hauʻofa’s own attribution of the turn in his thinking to Marshall Sahlins. He told Thomas that Sahlins, over lunch, said “Not all is lost”; “Right then I decided to see what kind of perspective I should look for. I said to myself: ‘We must do something about this dominant view of the Islands.’” The published chapter’s acknowledgement thanks Sahlins “for convincing me in the end that not all is lost” (Thomas’s 2006 interview; 1993 chapter). The phrase “islands in a far sea” he credited to Eric Waddell in a footnote, so the central contrast was a shared formulation rather than a wholly solitary one (Hauʻofa, 1994 text).

He named Albert Wendt’s 1976 paper “Towards a New Oceania” as a “landmark paper” and quoted it at length in “The Ocean in Us”, taking from it the idea that Oceania is made by the imagination as well as by fact (Hauʻofa 1998). Teresia Teaiwa’s line “We sweat and cry salt water, so we know that the ocean is really in our blood” stands as the epigraph to the same essay (Ibid.). For the Centre’s theme he drew on Sylvia Earle’s account of the ocean in Time magazine in 1996 (Ibid.).

His literary formation was equally explicit. In 2006 he described reading Conrad and Melville at school in Fiji, wanting to be a sailor and then a writer, and discovering V. S. Naipaul’s early novels during fieldwork in Trinidad: “His early novels were the funniest I ever read, and that had an influence later on, in the way that I wrote.” He named Ruth Benedict and Margaret Mead as anthropologists whose narrative writing attracted him, while rejecting the theoretical fashions of the ANU department (Thomas’s 2006 interview). Ulli and Georgina Beier’s work at the University of Papua New Guinea influenced the model of artist support he later built at USP, and he cited their writing on colonial art education in his 2005 address (Ibid.; Hauʻofa 2005). The institutional “Pacific Way” and dependency theory were foils rather than influences: Teaiwa notes that he wrote against a university climate in which dependency theories predominated (Teaiwa 1996).

Influence and legacy#

The Oceania Centre is the most concrete institutional legacy. USP says it grew from a director, a programme assistant and a cleaner into a leading regional creative centre, and that in 2008 the Pacific Studies Programme was merged into it to form the Oceania Centre for Arts, Culture and Pacific Studies (USP Centre history). The Centre’s Red Wave artists exhibited in London with Hauʻofa in April–May 2006; a journal article about them was in press when he died (Thomas, 2012; Wesley-Smith 2010). USP’s own memorial says he was a staff member for 37 years and an exemplary mentor to several cohorts of artists, and records the 2024 launch of Remembering Epeli Hauʻofa (USP memorial). The University of Auckland awarded him a posthumous honorary doctorate in 2023; this was seen in an institutional news page but the full text was not read closely enough here to quote from it, so it is stated only as a verified headline fact (University of Auckland).

The essay’s reception is documented. Teaiwa recorded in 1996 that it had been cited by James Clifford, solicited for republication by Arif Dirlik and Marshall Sahlins, used as the title of an ocean resources newsletter and made the theme of a Pacific Islands studies conference (Teaiwa 1996). The 2010 tribute volume documents its continuing use in teaching and in the work of writers, artists and activists across Oceania (Wesley-Smith 2010). Reception is not proof: Malm notes that the essay is still a “beautiful rhetorical device” more than three decades on, while arguing that its account of size, distance and migration cannot carry the explanatory weight his followers have given it (Malm 2026).

Criticisms and controversies#

Criticism began inside USP at the same time as the essay was published. Teaiwa’s review divides the responses in A New Oceania into the celebratory, the cautionary and the critical. Douglas Borer called the essay an “old and tired” claim to a “natural destiny of greatness”, compared its pan-Pacific cultural consciousness to Marx’s “false dream of an international class consciousness”, and concluded that “as a social scientist I see no hope for Epeli’s Oceania”. Joeli Veitayaki accused Hauʻofa of pandering to students after years of telling them “the painful truth”; the new view was “mostly superficial and unrealistic, certainly severed from the situation in the Pacific…. Epeli is romanticizing the past”, and myths and oral tradition could not solve present problems (Teaiwa 1996). Malm independently quotes the same essays and adds the Fijian sociologist Sitiveni Ratuva’s class objection: for Pacific elites the world is a big network of interrelationships, while for underpaid workers and poor villagers it is small, so “redefining the size, as Hauʻofa suggests, is a meaningless exercise to most” (Malm 2026, quoting Ratuva 1993).

Teaiwa’s own reservations were about the politics of the essay’s fame and its silences. She wrote that its “trans-Pacific currency” owed something to “the west’s” burgeoning market for scholarship on diaspora and postcoloniality, and argued that the volume neglected or merely paid obligations to Pacific women’s “physical and conceptual relationships”, which called into question “the ‘our-ness’ and the ‘new-ness’ of such spaces” (Teaiwa 1996). Other contributors, she reported, objected to his retention of the hierarchical categories Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia, to the suppression of rural poverty and urban deprivation, and to the risk of reviving the “Pacific Way” (Ibid.).

Pacific scholars have continued the argument. Tarcisius Kabutaulaka, writing in 2010, valued the essay for offering islanders “alternative ways of thinking about ourselves” but warned that its idealism had to be tested: interactions between islanders include superiority-inferiority complexes, and “focusing on the ocean as the element that connects us immediately marginalizes the millions of people who live inland, in places like the highlands of Papua New Guinea, for whom the ocean has little significance” (Wesley-Smith 2010). Malm’s 2026 book questions the historical claim that isolation was mainly a product of nineteenth-century imperialism, arguing that smallness and remoteness mattered before colonial rule and still matter for income, migration options and exposure to global harm. He also notes Margaret Jolly’s objection that the vision of a “world traveller” does not fit people who cannot afford to move: “because of border patrols by nation states, the exclusionary policies of migration, and sheer poverty”, few people in the southwest Pacific can travel overseas (Malm 2026). Kabutaulaka and Jolly contest the universal reach of the framing rather than rejecting Hauʻofa’s project. Hauʻofa himself had conceded part of the ground in 1998, writing that the sea “is the one physical entity that all of us in Oceania share” even though many people no longer depend on it directly (Hauʻofa 1998).

Related thinkers#

  • Walter Rodney — the nearest comparison inside this collection. How Europe Underdeveloped Africa made underdevelopment a relation of exploitation produced by capitalism. Hauʻofa shared the objection to outside definitions of island societies but shifted the mechanism from surplus extraction to discourse, mobility and cultural confidence. Borer’s reading of him as a disguised Marxian universalist should not be confused with Rodney’s actual Marxism.
  • Albert Wendt — supplied the phrase “a new Oceania” and a model of cultural reconstruction that Hauʻofa explicitly acknowledged.
  • Eric Waddell and Vijay Naidu — co-editors and interlocutors; Naidu, a critic who found Hauʻofa’s account of local agency more useful than dependency theory.
  • Teresia Teaiwa — both a critic and a beneficiary of his patronage; she later taught at USP and remained a close friend.
  • Marshall Sahlins — gave the decisive encouragement at the turn in Hauʻofa’s thinking.
  • Margaret Jolly, Ron Crocombe, Sitiveni Ratuva, Joeli Veitayaki and Thomas Malm — different versions of the objection that region-wide identity can hide class, gender, inland populations and the material costs of smallness.
