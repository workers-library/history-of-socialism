Socialism with a Human Face: The Prague Spring and the Invasion of 1968

In January 1968 the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia replaced Antonín Novotný with Alexander Dubček.

In January 1968 the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia replaced Antonín Novotný with Alexander Dubček. In April its Central Committee adopted the Action Programme, which named the “personality cult”, the exaggerated praise of one leader, and directive planning, in which officials fixed targets for every enterprise, as obstacles. It promised free speech and assembly, an end to censorship, equal standing for Czechs and Slovaks in a federal state, and a new constitution. Western journalists called the project “socialism with a human face”, a phrase the sociologist Radovan Richta had used, according to Zdeněk Mlynář, one of its principal drafters. The programme insisted the party kept its leading role, its sole right to govern: “The Communist Party enjoys the voluntary support of the people; it does not practice its leading role by ruling the society but by most devotedly serving its free, progressive socialist development.” (Central Committee of the KSČ, Action Programme, adopted 5 April 1968, translated by Zdravko Saveski) This was a statement of intent, not a record of change.

A programme from within#

The reforms began inside the party. Nine weeks after the programme, on 27 June, the writer Ludvík Vaculík published a manifesto, “Two Thousand Words”, in four Prague newspapers. He attacked the apparatus of officials who ran the farms and factories, called for workers’ councils and warned that their adversaries would not take a summer holiday: “The spring is over and will never return. By winter we will know all.” (Ludvík Vaculík, “Two Thousand Words”, 27 June 1968) The five allied parties that later intervened read this one intellectual’s intervention as proof of counter-revolution, a return to the old order. That was their reading, not a neutral description.

The party pressed from both sides#

Moscow and four allies answered in a letter of 15 July, released by the Soviet news agency TASS on 18 July. The Warsaw Letter told the Czechoslovak leaders the reforms were no longer their own affair: “This is something more than your cause. It is the common cause of our countries, which have joined in the Warsaw Pact …” (Warsaw Letter, TASS release 18 July 1968). It demanded that the party retake the press, radio and television.

The pressure was not only external. In August five senior Czechoslovak officials, Indra, Kolder, Kapek, Švestka and Bil’ak, wrote secretly to Brezhnev in Russian: “The very existence of socialism in our country is under threat.” (Indra, Kolder, Kapek, Švestka and Bil’ak, secret letter to Brezhnev, August 1968, translated by Mark Kramer). They said the press was in right-wing hands and the leadership paralysed. The letter gave the invasion domestic sponsors, though its authors had reason to exaggerate.

On 13 August Brezhnev telephoned Dubček. A KGB transcript, released in 1994, brackets places where the transcriber summarised Dubček’s replies. Brezhnev accused him of deceit, demanded the dismissal of named reformers and warned of new, independent measures. Dubček answered: “If you believe that we’re deceiving you, then take the measures you regard as appropriate.” (Alexander Dubček in the KGB-recorded telephone conversation with Brezhnev, 13 August 1968). He argued that decisions needed a plenum and time; the editors leave open whether Moscow took this as permission.

The public case came in a Soviet newspaper article whose author and date the two holding repositories give differently: “The sovereignty of each socialist country cannot be opposed to the interests of the world of socialism, of the world revolutionary movement.” (official Soviet doctrinal article, 1968, translated by Novosti). The name “Brezhnev Doctrine” was attached later; Brezhnev denied a formal doctrine existed.

The night the transmitters went silent#

On the night of 20–21 August 1968, Warsaw Pact troops entered Czechoslovakia. Zdeněk Mlynář, inside the party leadership, wrote later: “When Alexander Dubček formally adjourned the presidium meeting in the early morning of August 21, 1968, the corridors around the chambers were a hive of activity.” (Zdeněk Mlynář, Nightfrost in Prague, written in exile, English translation by Paul Wilson, 1980). The leaders were seized and the radio transmitters went silent. Czechoslovak Radio said the borders had been broken against the will of the state and the party; Radio Moscow called the invasion fraternal assistance.

In Moscow, eight people sat down on Red Square on 25 August. Natalya Gorbanevskaya recorded a banner: “Stop Soviet interference in Czechoslovakia.” (banner text recorded by Natalya Gorbanevskaya, Red Square, 25 August 1968, translated by Alexander Lieven). Plainclothes agents beat them and dragged them into cars as vehicles carrying Dubček to the talks passed. The protocol signed on 26 August annulled the party congress at Vysoká, restored censorship, banned “anti-socialist” groups and named reformers to be dismissed, with no fixed date for withdrawal. Its editors record that the Czechoslovaks won small changes and signed under threat of military dictatorship, so it cannot be read as free agreement.

What grew from the ruins#

The occupation bred an opposition that did not need to seize power. In January 1977 citizens issued Charter 77, a petition based on human-rights covenants Czechoslovakia had ratified in 1975 and the Helsinki Final Act. It listed barred employment, denied education, controlled media and police surveillance. Its spokespeople, Jan Patočka, Jiří Hájek and Václav Havel, wrote to the Federal Assembly: “We ourselves then – on behalf of these citizens – are prepared to talk with the competent authorities so that we can further clarify this position and put forth other suggestions and proposals” (spokespersons’ letter transmitting the Charter 77 Declaration, 5 January 1977, translated by Derek Paton). Couriers were detained, but the text reached journalists abroad.

Havel’s agenda for the first meeting shows the organising work: interrogations, substitute spokespeople, a file of abuses. He proposed: “I suggest not reacting to journalistic attacks unless it is to refute matter-of-fact lies” (Václav Havel, draft agenda, 3 January 1977). The state opened criminal proceedings for subversion against an unidentified group. Havel had earlier explained why a regime that stopped mass repression still bred opposition: “The man who resists humiliation can quickly forget it, but the man who can long tolerate it must long remember it.” (Václav Havel, “Dear Dr. Husák”, April 1975). In his 1978 essay “The Power of the Powerless”, circulated in samizdat, writing copied and passed outside the official presses, he described the greengrocer who displays a slogan: “That poster was delivered to our greengrocer from the enterprise headquarters along with the onions and carrots.” (Václav Havel, “The Power of the Powerless”, October 1978). The system, he argued, rested on daily consent. “Normalization”, the restoration of strict control after 1968, reversed the programme but not the argument.

What the record cannot establish#

The documents are almost all the words of leaders, writers and officials. Reformers, dissident intellectuals and Soviet leaders dominate; ordinary citizens, Slovak activists, invading soldiers and those who faced the tanks speak mainly through records written about them. The Charter 77 list is broad, including workers, students and priests, but gives only names and occupations. Havel’s essays are philosophical arguments, Mlynář’s memoir retrospective and personally aggrieved, Gorbanevskaya’s account a participant’s record. No Prague Spring diary was securely identified, and no Warsaw Pact operational orders for Operation Danube were located: the political decision is documented, the military chain of command is not. The KGB transcript contains editorial summaries and the Moscow Protocol exists only as a noisy scan. Numbers of dead, arrested or expelled are not answered. Language narrows the record further, since much Czech and Slovak material was reached only in English. The original transcripts, an intact protocol and any diaries would need a physical archive visit. The works below are known mainly from catalogue records.

How historians have read it#

  • Jaromír Navrátil et al., eds., The Prague Spring ’68 (Central European University Press, 1998). The 140-document reader compiled by the Czech commission on 1967–70, with a foreword by H. Gordon Skilling; its method is to let Soviet, Czechoslovak and American records confront one another. Source: nsarchive.gwu.edu
  • H. Gordon Skilling, Czechoslovakia’s Interrupted Revolution (Princeton University Press, 1976). Based on unpublished party documents and interviews conducted in Czechoslovakia in 1967–69; argues that the Prague Spring was a movement of revolutionary proportions, a stronger claim than reading the reforms as elite liberalisation. Internet Archive snapshot: czechoslovakias interrupted revolution
  • Z. A. B. Zeman, Prague Spring: A Report on Czechoslovakia 1968 (Hill and Wang / Penguin, 1969). A near-contemporary narrative organised around the writers’ union, the Slovaks and students, the April programme and the summer crisis; useful for sequence, written before the archives opened. Internet Archive: praguespring0000zema
  • Vojtech Mastny, Czechoslovakia: Crisis in World Communism (Facts on File, 1972). Places 1968 inside the international politics of the communist world rather than solely in Czechoslovak domestic history; a Cold War international frame that complements Zeman’s domestic one. Internet Archive: czechoslovakiacr0000mast
  • Mark Kramer, “The Kremlin, the Prague Spring, and the Brezhnev Doctrine” (2009). A scholarly interpretation of the newly available Soviet record; his translations and headnotes to the National Security Archive documents are also the working basis for several sources above. Internet Archive: TheKremlinThePragueSpringandtheBrezhnevDoctrinebyMarkKramer2009 09 01

Broad agreement holds that the Action Programme was a party-led reform, that the invasion was decided in Moscow with the help of Czechoslovak hardliners, and that normalization reversed the reforms. Live disagreement concerns how radical the movement was, how decisive the letter from Bil’ak and his colleagues was, and what Dubček’s remarks on 13 August meant. These are interpretive disputes over an evidence base that is not equally distributed, and they should not be presented as three equally supported answers.
