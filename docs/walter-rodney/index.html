Walter Rodney: How Europe Underdeveloped Africa

A research profile of the Guyanese historian's account of colonial extraction, underdevelopment and Black Power, with scholarly debate over his work.

Walter Rodney argued that Africa’s poverty was made, not inherited. By underdevelopment he meant neither a stage of backwardness nor a plain absence of development: every people had developed in some way, and the word described a comparison produced by uneven development and, more importantly, a relationship in which the surplus of one country was transferred to another. Dependency, in his usage, was the resulting reliance of the poorer economy on the metropolitan capitalist economies that drew that surplus away. This profile sets out his argument in the sources opened for it, the method behind it, and the objections made to it. It does not convert his claims into settled fact.

Biography#

The chapter on Cabral and Rodney already tells the story of Rodney’s life, his exclusion from Jamaica, the writing of How Europe Underdeveloped Africa, his organising in Guyana and the disputed circumstances of his death; this profile keeps the biography to what bears on the work and adds an account of his arguments, his method and the scholarly debate around them.

Walter Anthony Rodney was born in Georgetown, British Guiana, on 23 March 1942, the second of six children of Percival Edward Rodney, a tailor, and Pauline Worrell Rodney, a seamstress and home-maker. The Atlanta University Center finding aid records that his father was involved in the People’s Progressive Party, the multi-racial anti-colonial party that included Marxists and socialists. This is an institutional record, not Rodney’s own retrospective account (AUC finding aid). He won a scholarship to Queen’s College in Georgetown and then to the University of the West Indies at Mona, Jamaica, where he took a first-class bachelor’s degree in history in 1963. A further scholarship took him to the School of Oriental and African Studies in London. He married Patricia Henry in London in January 1965; they had three children, Shaka, Kanini and Asha. He received his doctorate in African history in 1966, at twenty-four, with a dissertation on the Upper Guinea Coast that became his first book (AUC finding aid).

His first teaching post was at University College, Dar es Salaam, from 1966 to 1968. He returned to the University of the West Indies in Jamaica in 1968, taught African history and spoke outside the university to students, workers and people in poor districts of Kingston, making contact with Rastafarians. While he was in Montreal for the Congress of Black Writers in October 1968, the Jamaican government of Hugh Shearer barred him from re-entering the country. The finding aid records student demonstrations, the closure of the university for two weeks, unrest that spread beyond the campus, deaths and injuries, extensive debate in Parliament and heavy property damage. Those are the archive’s words; the scale is not independently measured here. The talks he gave in Jamaica and Montreal were published in 1969 as The Groundings with My Brothers (AUC finding aid; 2019 Verso edition).

Rodney went back to Dar es Salaam, where he was senior lecturer from 1969 to 1972 and associate professor from 1972 to 1974. He spent February to June 1972 as a visiting professor at the University of Michigan’s Center for Afro-American and African Studies, and the first months of 1975 teaching at Cornell University’s African Studies and Research Center. In 1974 the University of Guyana offered him the chair of history; the government rescinded the appointment after he returned. He remained in Guyana, worked in the leadership of the Working People’s Alliance, founded in 1974 and constituted as a party in 1979, and became the main national figure opposing Forbes Burnham’s People’s National Congress government. He and seven others were charged with arson in July 1979 after two government offices burned. His trial began on 2 June 1980 and was adjourned a week later (AUC finding aid).

He was killed in Georgetown on 13 June 1980, a week after his last public speech, at the age of thirty-eight. The finding aid describes a car bomb; it also records that repeated petitions for an official investigation had not produced one, that more than 35,000 people took part in his funeral procession on 23 June, and that the Guyanese government awarded him the Order of Excellence in 1992. The political circumstances are set out under “Criticisms and controversies” below, with the conflicting date left visible. The archive holds his papers, donated by the family in the early 2000s: 20 linear feet, 1,025 described records, covering 1960 to 1987 with the bulk from 1970 to 1979 (AUC finding aid; Walter Rodney Foundation).

Intellectual tradition#

Rodney was a Marxist historian in the anti-colonial and Pan-African current, but the labels need care. The finding aid calls him a Marxist historian who interpreted Marxist theory within the context of African people’s history and circumstances. His own writing preferred the phrase “scientific socialism”, which he treated as a method for uncovering how production and political power worked rather than a fixed European blueprint. In the 1975 speech Marxism and African Liberation he argued that Marx’s method was independent of time and place, and that Lenin, Mao and above all Amílcar Cabral had shown how it had to be applied to different social formations. He described Cabral starting from people in the process of production rather than looking for classes that did not yet exist. Marxism was a “weapon of theory” against the capitalist-imperialist structure, not a set of conclusions to be repeated (Rodney, Marxism and African Liberation).

He rejected the label “African socialism” when it meant a third way between capitalism and socialism. In Tanzanian Ujamaa and Scientific Socialism he separated Nyerere’s Ujamaa from the “African socialism” of Léopold Senghor and Tom Mboya, which he said left capitalism and imperialism unchallenged. He argued that Ujamaa, with its nationalisations, peasant villages and break with private property in the major means of production, was in substance compatible with scientific socialism even though Tanzanian leaders would not use the name. He warned that the comparison was “an exercise in theory”, that Ujamaa had not yet been tested, and that the article was “no paean of praise” (Rodney, Tanzanian Ujamaa and Scientific Socialism). In the 1975 speech he attacked Nkrumah for refusing to admit classes in Ghana and for mixing Marxism with Protestantism, Pan-Africanism, Consciencism and Nkrumahism. He treated Nkrumah’s Class Struggle in Africa as an honest self-critique, written after the coup, not as a coherent theory. Rodney also rejected racial essentialism: in his 1972 talk on the African Revolution he warned against the “grand singular” that treated all whites as enemies and all blacks as friends (Rodney, The African Revolution; Adeleke 2000).

His intellectual home was therefore a disputed border. He wrote as a Pan-Africanist who insisted on class, as a Marxist who insisted on race and colonial history, and as a university historian who argued that the historian’s duty was to the working people he studied. Tunde Adeleke calls this “guerrilla intellectualism”: knowledge treated as a weapon in a struggle rather than as a detached occupation. Adeleke is a scholarly interpreter, and his account of Rodney’s motives is an interpretation, but his citations point back to Rodney’s own texts (Adeleke 2000).

Major contributions#

Rodney’s central contribution was to define underdevelopment as a historical relation rather than a stage of backwardness. In How Europe Underdeveloped Africa he wrote that underdevelopment was not the absence of development, since every people had developed in some way, but a comparison produced by uneven development. Its “second and even more indispensable component” was that it expressed “a particular relationship of exploitation: namely, the exploitation of one country by another”. He called the export of surplus, which deprived African societies of the benefit of their own resources and labour, “an integral part of underdevelopment in the contemporary sense” (Rodney, How Europe Underdeveloped Africa, 2018 Verso text). The book’s preface fixed the enquiry from the fifteenth century to the end of colonial rule, and said the facts and interpretation were offered in support of the conclusion that African development was possible “only on the basis of a radical break with the international capitalist system, which has been the principal agency of underdevelopment of Africa over the last five centuries”. Its stated purpose was to reach Africans who wished “to explore further the nature of their exploitation, rather than to satisfy the ‘standards’ set by our oppressors and their spokesmen in the academic world” (Ibid.). The related history of how this argument was received in the anti-colonial movement is told in Colonialism and Underdevelopment.

A second contribution was the claim that Africa had helped to create European wealth at the same time as Europe was underdeveloping Africa. He put this as a proportional formula: Africa helped to develop Western Europe in the same proportion as Western Europe helped to underdevelop Africa, while taking care to say that it was “extremely simple-minded” to claim that colonialism alone caused Europe’s science and technology. His more precise claim was that colonisation formed “an indispensable link in a chain of events” that made possible the technological transformation of European capitalism (Wallerstein 1986, quoting Rodney).

A third was his theory of the revolutionary intellectual. In the 1971 review of Issa Shivji’s The Silent Class Struggle, Rodney accepted “petty bourgeoisie” — the educated and salaried middle stratum — as a neutral analytical description of a privileged local group, drew on Cabral’s idea of a “revolutionary petty bourgeoisie”, and located the decisive ideological battle inside that stratum rather than directly between peasants and imperialists. Progressive intellectuals had to go beyond the limits capitalism imposed on their knowledge, and revolutionaries had to show peasants and workers how socialist ideas bore on daily life. He wrote that the revolution required “that the millions who have been gagged throughout history should speak and choose” (Rodney, Some Implications of the Question of Disengagement from Imperialism). A fourth contribution, developed in Guyana, was a theory of mass power: withdrawal of labour as the ultimate weapon against a regime that still needed workers to produce, non-cooperation as refusal to cooperate in one’s own oppression, and a Government of National Unity across race and class (Rodney, People’s Power, No Dictator).

Analysis of capitalism#

Rodney treated capitalism as a single world system whose centres and peripheries were formed together. In How Europe Underdeveloped Africa he described a “relationship of exploitation” between underdeveloped and developed countries, the dependency of the former on metropolitan capitalist economies, and “multiplier effects” that were positive for Western Europe and negative for Africa. Colonialism’s “central purpose”, in his formulation, was a great intensification of exploitation (Wallerstein 1986, summarising and quoting Rodney). The mechanism was not only trade. The slave trade drained labour; colonial rule removed African control over political and cultural goals; cash crops and mining created “growth without development” in which profits went abroad and the economy became more dependent; and manufacturing was blocked so that African societies supplied raw material and labour while the industrial complex grew in Europe. In a passage published as an excerpt by his publisher he wrote that what colonialists called “the development of Africa” was a “cynical shorthand expression for ‘the intensification of colonial exploitation in Africa to develop capitalist Europe’” (Rodney, publisher’s excerpt). The Upper Guinea Coast research behind the book’s account of slavery is used as evidence in Slavery, Race and Capital.

His analysis of what capitalism gave workers is unusually precise for a polemical book. The peasants and workers of Europe “paid a huge price so that the capitalists could make their profits from the human labor that always lies behind the machines”, and there had been a period when the system increased the well-being of many people “as a byproduct of seeking out profits for a few”. In the present, he argued, the quest for profits had come into conflict with people’s demands for material and social needs. The general principle was stated at the end of the colonial chapter: “The exploitation and the comparative disadvantage are the ingredients of underdevelopment.” (Rodney, How Europe Underdeveloped Africa).

In The Imperialist Partition of Africa he defended Lenin against the charge of crude economic determinism. Lenin’s pamphlet dealt with the expansion of the capitalist economy; political, military and psychological factors existed but were secondary and had to be related to the political economy of monopoly capital. Imperialism and political partition were not interchangeable. Imperialism arose from the expansion of capitalist production; the particular form of the scramble for Africa was shaped by African social formations, racism in the capitalist superstructure, and African resistance. He illustrated this with the palm-oil trade, the Royal Niger Company, the Congo Free State and German competition with Britain and France, and concluded that by the 1970s imperialism had almost changed its form of political partition in order to pursue economic exploitation more effectively (Rodney, The Imperialist Partition of Africa).

Racism was not an afterthought. He wrote that a group of African “sell-outs” was part of the definition of underdevelopment, and that Fanon had analysed the local minority that served as a transmission line between metropolitan capital and African dependencies (Rodney, How Europe Underdeveloped Africa). In the 1971 essay he agreed with Shivji that nationalisation was not automatically beneficial: joint ventures, management contracts and a bureaucracy directing surplus towards consumer goods could hollow out a policy of disengagement. The problem was internal as well as external, and had to be named before it could be solved (Rodney, Some Implications).

Analysis of socialism#

Rodney did not write a blueprint for a socialist society, and this profile makes no claim that he did. What he did write was a defence of scientific socialism as a method and a set of institutions to be built from existing social relations. In Marxism and African Liberation he defined a scientific socialist position as one that consciously undermined the system of production and the political relations flowing from it, and argued that African people had a vested interest in socialism because it offered a tool for dismantling capitalist and imperialist power. He treated nationalism and socialism as inseparable: the petty bourgeois nationalist who sought only the recovery of national independence could not deliver participation or liberation (Rodney, Marxism and African Liberation).

His most concrete institutional discussion is the 1972 essay on Tanzanian Ujamaa. He defended village collectivisation, joint production, egalitarian distribution and the universal obligation to work as aims, and cited the Arusha Declaration’s nationalisation of the major means of production through government and cooperatives. He accepted a transitional role for private enterprise but denied that permanent coexistence of capitalist and socialist relations was socialism. He argued that communalism was not itself socialism, that technological weakness had to be overcome, and that the possibility of moving from commune to socialism depended on socialist breakthroughs elsewhere providing models, technology and a changed world balance. He thought the peasants were the key class in Tanzania because of the stage of its productive forces — the tools, skills and labour available to produce what a society needs — but he also criticised the actual building of Ujamaa villages for failing to identify points of strength and weakness scientifically, and ended by saying that the theory would be realised only through ideological and political struggle (Rodney, Tanzanian Ujamaa and Scientific Socialism).

In the 1971 essay he endorsed the structures Shivji had described: national corporations in place of private firms, worker self-management in place of elitist management, Ujamaa villages in place of individualist farming, and self-reliant schools in place of institutions that alienated students from peasant parents. He did not treat nationalisation as an end. In Guyana he described the nationalisation of sugar and bauxite as positive, while arguing that the industries had suffered from what he called the Burnham Touch; the alternative he proposed was a Government of National Reconstruction and National Unity led by workers but including patriotic private business and the middle class (Rodney, Some Implications; Rodney, People’s Power, No Dictator).

His late speeches present socialism as a practical question of power and daily life. In Sign of the Times of 6 June 1980 he joined the absence of milk to the absence of a free constitution, and said the two could not be grumbled about separately. He described a young man from the “street force” becoming a political cadre, and insisted that revolution was made by ordinary people, “not by angels” (Rodney, Sign of the Times).

Recommended reading#

These works are starting points into Rodney’s argument, not a measure of his whole output; the complete bibliography stands above.

  • How Europe Underdeveloped Africa (1972) — This is the essential starting point because it defines underdevelopment as a relationship of exploitation between countries and traces how surplus was drained from Africa while its manufacturing was blocked.
  • “The Imperialist Partition of Africa” (1970) — It is the best place to see how he separates imperialism from political partition, arguing that the scramble for Africa was shaped by African social formations, racism and resistance rather than by economics alone.
  • “Some Implications of the Question of Disengagement from Imperialism” (1971) — This is the clearest entry to his theory of the revolutionary intellectual and to his warning that nationalisation alone does not transfer power, since joint ventures and a bureaucracy directing surplus toward consumer goods can hollow it out.
  • “Tanzanian Ujamaa and Scientific Socialism” (1972) — This is the clearest place to see his most concrete institutional argument: village collectivisation, joint production, egalitarian distribution and a universal obligation to work, offered without treating Ujamaa as a finished socialist society.
  • People’s Power, No Dictator (1979) — This is the best starting point for his theory of mass power in Guyana: withdrawal of labour, non-cooperation and a government of national unity across race and class.

Selected lectures and interviews#

The public finding aid describes three audio recordings from 1980 in the Rodney Papers: a media interview, a Working People’s Alliance conference, and his last public speech at a WPA rally. The Walter Rodney Foundation repeats that the papers contain recordings of Rodney lecturing at universities, conferences and rallies, including the last public speech. Both state or imply access limits: the AUC finding aid says audio recordings are for research and study and may not be copied, and neither source offers a playable online file (AUC finding aid; Walter Rodney Foundation). The recordings exist but cannot be checked remotely here; no unattributed upload is used in their place.

The last public speech is the one item with a firm date and a published transcript. Sign of the Times was delivered on 6 June 1980 and printed in the WPA memorial booklet of 1981. The transcript names Rupert Roopnaraine and Omawale as speakers at the same meeting and records Rodney’s jokes about the proposed constitution, his account of Edward Dublin’s killing, and his appeal for a combined struggle over food, rights and political freedom (Rodney, Sign of the Times).

The 1968 Montreal lectures are documented by the 2019 edition of The Groundings. Carole Boyce Davies reports that “Statement of the Jamaican Situation”, co-authored with Robert Hill, and “African History in the Service of Black Liberation” were delivered during the Congress of Black Writers of 11–14 October 1968, and that “The Grounding with My Brothers” was delivered after the Congress. She also notes that Rodney was not on the initial programme and was added at the last minute (Davies’s introduction). The 1975 Institute of the Black World roundtable with Vincent Harding and Robert Hill, held in Amherst on 30 April–1 May 1975, is documented by the archive finding aid; it became Walter Rodney Speaks (AUC finding aid). An Internet Archive audio item titled “Walter Rodney on Race and Class in Guyanese Politics (1978)” describes itself as a lecture reposted by a podcast in 2019; because the original host, date and source are not stated, it is not used as evidence here (catalog endpoint checked, not used as evidence).

Intellectual influences#

Rodney’s own “Guide to Further Reading” in How Europe Underdeveloped Africa is the best primary evidence for influences. For development and underdevelopment he listed Engels’s Origin of the Family, Marx’s Preface to a Contribution to a Critique of Political Economy and Pre-Capitalist Economic Foundations, and, from a different standpoint, Celso Furtado, A. G. Frank, Tamas Szentes and Samir Amin. For Africa’s contribution to European development he singled out Eric Williams’s Capitalism and Slavery, Oliver Cox, Du Bois, Philip Curtin and Winthrop Jordan, noting that Williams was precise and detailed on the connection between British capitalism and the enslavement of Africans. For imperialism he listed George Padmore, Nkrumah, Jalée and a Soviet Academy history; for colonialism, Abdou Moumouni and the writings of Fanon, whom he called unequalled on the psychological aspects of enslavement and on the end of colonialism (Rodney, How Europe Underdeveloped Africa).

C. L. R. James mattered in two ways. Rodney’s 1972 talk on the African Revolution is a long tribute to James’s A History of Negro Revolt and The Black Jacobins, to his recognition of African resistance before later historians accepted it, and to his combination of committed scholarship and activism. Rodney also defended James against both Stalinist and Trotskyist orthodoxy (Rodney, The African Revolution). Adeleke reports Rodney saying that James’s London study group gave him “a knowledge of Marxism, a more precise understanding of the Russian revolution, and of historical formulation”; this is a secondary author quoting Rodney, so the words are attributed through Adeleke unless the original is located (Adeleke 2000). The AUC finding aid independently confirms that Rodney joined a West Indian study group that met with James in London (AUC finding aid).

Cabral was the decisive contemporary. Rodney’s 1971 essay adopts Cabral’s categories of nationalist capacity, revolutionary capacity and revolutionary petty bourgeoisie; his 1975 speech quotes Cabral on revolutionary theory; and his 1972 Ujamaa essay cites Cabral on the possibility of a historical jump made possible by existing productive forces and socialist states. The direction of influence is documented from Cabral to Rodney, not the reverse (Rodney, Some Implications; Rodney, Marxism and African Liberation). Prashad’s foreword adds that Rodney worked in debate with Issa Shivji, John Saul, A. M. Babu and the student radicals around Cheche, and that Nyerere’s Arusha Declaration and the Ugandan writer Yoweri Museveni’s student front formed the setting in which his Marxism took its later shape (Prashad, foreword excerpt).

Influence and legacy#

The AUC finding aid records that How Europe Underdeveloped Africa was translated into Portuguese, German and Japanese and used as a text in colleges and universities in the United States, Europe, Africa and the Caribbean. The Walter Rodney Foundation maintains a bibliography of works by and about him, an annual symposium, a speakers series and the journal Groundings (AUC finding aid; Walter Rodney Foundation). The 2019 edition of The Groundings documents a Walter Rodney Speaker Series begun in 2013 at the University of the West Indies (The Groundings). Guyana’s posthumous honours, including the Order of Excellence, the Walter Rodney Memorial Park, and the naming of the National Archives building, are recorded in the same finding aid.

Scholarly reassessment has continued. African Economic History devoted its 50, no. 2 issue of May 2022 to fifty years of the book, with Richard Drayton on its Caribbean origins and Ana Lucia Araujo testing one of its supporting claims; only the records and titles were accessible here, not the full articles (Drayton 2022 and Araujo 2022, DOI records). Small Axe 27, no. 3 (2023) carried Adom Getachew on Rodney’s image of the historian as diagnostician and participant, and Peter James Hudson on his pedagogy and method. The abstracts available through Crossref describe Getachew’s argument that the medical analogy generates a critique of romantic pictures of the African past, and Hudson’s account of Rodney’s engagements with UCLA within the geographies of Black radicalism; the full texts were not opened (Getachew 2023, abstract only; Hudson 2023, abstract only).

The book’s reach should not be confused with proof of its conclusions. Wallerstein called it influential and one of the first to propagate a standpoint that was new around 1972, while noting that it was published cheaply for students and educated Africans rather than as an archival monograph (Wallerstein 1986). This profile follows the project’s editorial source policy in separating that judgement from the evidence needed to establish the book’s claims.

Criticisms and controversies#

Wallerstein’s 1986 review is the most substantial scholarly assessment opened here. He described How Europe Underdeveloped Africa as unlike Rodney’s other two major books: not based on archival sources, without footnotes, with short annotated reading notes instead of a bibliography, and openly didactic. He reported that some critics thought the early work implied a docile Africa moulded by an aggressive Europe, and that African historians debated whether Africans were agents of their own history or merely reacted to others. Wallerstein’s own judgement was more favourable: he argued that Rodney refused the verbal trap by distinguishing temporal initiative from analytical structure, insisted that African rulers still made decisions before 1885, and spent much of the book showing how the two zones formed a single analytical entity. He also drew attention to Rodney’s care in qualifying his own claims, including the warning that it would be simple-minded to attribute European technology to colonialism alone (Wallerstein 1986).

A second line of criticism is empirical. Ana Lucia Araujo’s 2022 article, “Did Rodney Get It Wrong? Europe Underdeveloped Africa but Enslaved People Were Not Always Purchased with Rubbish”, tests the book’s supporting claim about the goods exchanged for enslaved people against later research. The journal page returned a 403 response and the full text was not opened, so this profile records the title and the existence of the dispute but does not summarise Araujo’s findings (Araujo 2022, record only). The same limitation applies to Drayton’s article, whose full text could not be retrieved (Drayton 2022, record only).

Rodney also faced criticism from inside the revolutionary tradition. C. L. R. James’s memorial lecture of 30 January 1981 credited him with an extraordinary political intelligence and with building the Working People’s Alliance, but argued that he had failed to study the question of the seizure of power, had moved before the popular upsurge was ready, and should never have been in the car with a member of Burnham’s army handling an explosive device. James called this a political, not merely a personal, mistake and said the organisation had failed to protect its leader. He also judged that Burnham’s cancellation of Rodney’s university appointment was a gross blunder that helped create the dangerous situation (James 1981). This is a criticism by an ally and teacher, not an official finding.

The circumstances of the death remain contested in the opened sources. The AUC finding aid gives 13 June 1980 and a car bomb, and says no official investigation was realised despite repeated petitions. Tim Hector, writing in 1997, recalled the phone call from Conrad Luke on the night of the killing and treated 13 June as the anniversary; his article is a participant’s retrospective memory, not an investigation (Hector 1997). James’s lecture gives the circumstances in more detail but dates the killing to 13 October 1980; that date conflicts with the archive, with Hector and with the date of Rodney’s last speech. This profile uses 13 June and leaves the conflict visible. Verso’s publisher copy says Rodney was assassinated in 1980 “reportedly at the behest of the government”; a publisher’s summary is not evidence of responsibility (Verso, publisher description). No commission report, court record or police file establishing responsibility was opened, and no conclusion about who was responsible is drawn here.

Related thinkers#

The connection to Cabral is the closest and the best documented. Rodney took over Cabral’s categories of nationalist capacity, revolutionary capacity and revolutionary petty bourgeoisie, and used Cabral’s dictum on revolutionary theory as his authority for joining national liberation to scientific socialism. There is no evidence in the documents opened here that Cabral read Rodney. The shared history is told in Cabral and Rodney, while this profile is concerned with what Rodney made of Cabral’s categories. C. L. R. James was both a model and a critic: Rodney defended his method against orthodox Marxists and used his history of popular resistance as a model, while James later faulted Rodney’s strategy in Guyana. Eric Williams’s Capitalism and Slavery was, for Rodney, the precise demonstration of the link between British capitalism and African enslavement, and the two appear together in the history of slavery, race and capital.

Two other relationships ran through argument rather than agreement. Rodney criticised Nkrumah for refusing to name class contradictions in Ghana while treating Nkrumah’s later self-critique as historically important; the wider history is covered in Nkrumah and Lumumba. He defended Nyerere’s Ujamaa against the “African socialism” of Senghor and Mboya while refusing to call Ujamaa a finished socialist society. He cited Frank and Amin in his reading guide but did not simply repeat dependency theory: his distinct claim was that the relationship of exploitation and the loss of development opportunity had to be traced through African social formations and African resistance, not read off from trade statistics. Issa Shivji, John Saul, A. M. Babu and the students around Cheche were partners in debate, as Prashad’s foreword records. Fanon supplied his analysis of the local stratum that carries metropolitan capital into African dependencies. None of these figures has a profile in this collection yet, so they are named here without links to pages that do not exist.
