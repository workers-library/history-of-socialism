Empire and Underdevelopment: Colonialism as an Economic and Political System

Rodney, Cabral, Fanon, the Bengal famine, the Congo and the forms of imperial control that survived decolonisation.

MarxismPart II: Capitalism, Imperialism and the Social Question

By the fourth Pan-African Congress in New York in 1927, Du Bois was printing resolutions that demanded land, self-rule in Africa and the withdrawal of American troops from Haiti. One stated: “The attitude of all the imperial powers who own Africa is fundamentally wrong. They are seeking profit, not men; they want trade and industry more than civilization and spiritual uplift” (resolutions reproduced by Du Bois, October 1927). It was an organiser’s claim, not a neutral finding, but it joins the economic and political. Imperialism here means a system in which states and firms extend power beyond their borders to secure markets, raw materials and investment outlets; colonialism means direct political rule over another people; neo-colonialism means control through trade, finance and aligned local interests rather than formal rule. Underdevelopment here is a condition produced by the relation between societies, not merely a stage not yet reached.

The argument about causes#

Lenin’s Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism, written in 1916, defined the epoch as “the monopoly stage of capitalism,” monopoly meaning a branch of production dominated by a few large firms. He stressed that monopoly “does not eliminate” free competition “but exist[s] above it and alongside it,” producing “acute, intense antagonisms, frictions and conflicts” (Imperialism, chapter VII). It moved through concentration of production, banks, finance capital, the export of capital and the division of the world (chapter I). It was written to explain the collapse of the Second International in 1914.

The economic critique of empire was not confined to Marxists. J. A. Hobson’s Imperialism: A Study of 1902 examined how mercantile interests in the home country could manipulate state power to secure benefits in the colony (Hobson). Placing Hobson beside Lenin matters: it prevents the chapter from treating Marxism as the only source of the argument that imperial expansion served particular economic interests. Hobson’s explanation and Lenin’s are not identical, and neither is a neutral account of colonial rule.

Lenin’s draft theses of June 1920 called for a temporary alliance with bourgeois democracy in colonial countries while keeping communist organisations independent (Lenin, 5 June 1920). M. N. Roy’s supplementary theses of July 1920 put the economic claim sharply: “The super-profits made in the colonies form one of the main sources of the resources of contemporary capitalism” (Roy, 25 July 1920). Roy wanted communists to build workers’ and peasants’ organisations rather than follow the national middle class. The two texts record a disagreement and reach English through translations of differing provenance.

Rodney refused a single-cause account. In 1970 he held that partition was shaped by three factors beyond capitalist expansion: African social formations, racism within the capitalist superstructure, and African opposition (Rodney, The Imperialist Partition of Africa). In 1971 he endorsed the claim that wealth drained towards the developed capitalist nations and that political independence did not halt economic exploitation (Rodney, 1971). Cabral defined imperialism as piracy moved from the sea to dry land, reorganised to exploit colonised peoples’ resources. He distinguished direct rule from indirect rule through local agents, which he called neo-colonialism, and held that the essence was the seizure of a people’s right to develop its productive forces (Cabral, 1966). These writers share a framework but differ in emphasis; their claims are related, not interchangeable.

Different rulers and different controls#

The Treaty of Nanking of 29 August 1842 shows the commercial terms behind military victory. It opened five ports to British residence and trade, ceded Hong Kong “to be possessed in perpetuity”, abolished the licensed Cohong monopoly, and required China to pay a total of twenty-one million dollars: six for the opium surrendered at Canton, three for debts to British merchants, and twelve for the cost of the expedition (Treaty of Nanking, articles 2–7). The treaty is a legal record of what was imposed, not a measurement of its effects on Chinese society. It does, however, connect the opium trade, commercial access and territorial cession in a single document, which the theoretical accounts above treat separately.

The record describes several imperial systems rather than one. In British India the Bengal famine of 1943 killed between one and two million people according to the official inquiry, which found that high prices and a failure of distribution, not a single failed harvest, produced the starvation (Famine Inquiry Commission, 1945). T. G. Narayanan of The Hindu wrote of an old woman near Ballygunge station: “Hundreds of people passed by her. Not one cared enough to telephone for an ambulance” (Narayanan, Famine Over Bengal, 1944). The commission’s finding supports Amartya Sen’s later claim that particular groups lose the ability to command food (Sen, 1981). Whether policy was deliberately lethal remains unsettled.

The statutory form of that system can be read in the Natives Land Act of 1913. Section 1 barred a Black person from acquiring land outside scheduled native areas from anyone other than a Black person, and barred anyone else from acquiring land within those areas, except with the Governor-General’s approval. Section 5 made a contravening transaction an offence punishable by a fine or up to six months’ imprisonment, and section 6 protected farmers’ claims to resident labour while placing the burden of proving farm-labourer status on the accused (Natives Land Act, Act No. 27 of 1913). This is the law itself; what it did to particular communities is a separate question, and the transcribed text may contain errors.

In South Africa the Native Land Act of 1913, pass laws and the colour bar structured a labour market along racial lines. Padmore marshalled these measures in 1931 and quoted East African governors on taxation as a means of forcing Africans into wage work (Padmore, 1931). The ANC’s claims of 1943 joined suffrage, repeal of the pass laws, land, equal pay and “the statutory recognition of the right of the African worker to collective bargaining under the Industrial Conciliation Act” (ANC, 16 December 1943). French rule in Vietnam was described by Ho Chi Minh in 1924 through plantations of 20,000 to 25,000 hectares, forced labour and a land tax raised after floods (Ho Chi Minh, 1924). Belgian rule in the Congo was recalled by Lumumba on independence day as forced labour paid too little to feed a family, land seized under laws of might, and towns divided between white mansions and Black huts (Lumumba, 1960). Nkrumah used the Congo as his case study of neo-colonialism, naming international financial control and the danger of states too small to develop independently (Nkrumah, 1965).

Portugal’s territories differed again. Cabral told the United Nations in 1962 that Guinea was a non-self-governing country occupied by force, without elected representatives or any means to remove officials (Cabral, 1962). In 1972 he put those assimilated at no more than 0.3 percent, a figure from a political speech (Cabral, 1972). Villagers in Tombali told visitors later: “We worked hard but the Portuguese took all we had and treated us worse than animals” (villagers of Tchugue). The wars ended through a Lisbon coup and negotiations. At Alvor in January 1975 the Portuguese state and three Angolan movements fixed 11 November for independence and a three-member transitional presidency (Alvor Agreement, Article 4). Fighting resumed within weeks. Algeria showed the settler variant: Henri Maillot, a European born in Algeria who defected to the FLN, wrote in 1956 that “I’m not Muslim, but I am Algerian of European origin. I consider Algeria to be my homeland” (Maillot, 1956). American power appeared in the 1927 demand for withdrawal from Haiti and in Ford’s arrest in 1929 while protesting the shooting of Haitians by Marines (Ford, 1938). The Brussels manifesto named Netherlands India among recent outrages without analysing Dutch rule (Brussels manifesto, 1927). These cases differ in who ruled, who settled, what was extracted and how resistance was organised.

What independence did not end#

Fanon argued that formal sovereignty could leave economic control intact. The national middle class, he wrote in 1961, becomes an intermediary: “its mission has nothing to do with transforming the nation; it consists, prosaically, of being the transmission line between the nation and a capitalism, rampant though camouflaged, which today puts on the masque of neocolonialism” (Fanon, “The Pitfalls of National Consciousness”). Nkrumah defined the condition in 1965: “The State which is subject to it is, in theory, independent and has all the outward trappings of international sovereignty. In reality its economic system and thus its political policy is directed from outside” (Nkrumah, Introduction). He listed mechanisms: obligations to take the former power’s manufactures, civil servants dictating policy, and foreign exchange controlled through outside banks. Sankara called debt “the second pillage” in 1987 and said “debt is neo-colonialism, in which colonizers have transformed themselves into ‘technical assistants.’ We should rather say ‘technical assassins’” (Sankara, 1987). These are arguments by leaders and theorists, not measurements of capital flows.

At Bandung in 1955 Sukarno spoke of colonialism in “a modern dress of economic, intellectual and physical control,” while Nehru rejected both Cold War blocs (Sukarno; Nehru). Cabral’s answer was to build institutions in the liberated zones before a state existed: universal suffrage and a secret vote chose a National Popular Assembly of 120 members before independence in September 1973 (Cabral, January 1973; Proclamation of Guinea-Bissau). They agreed that a flag was not the end of the question and disagreed over which institution could carry it forward.

Limits of the record#

There is no dedicated Congo chapter; the material here consists mainly of speeches by Nkrumah and Lumumba in movement publications. No colonial office file, concession contract or customs series has been opened. The Bengal material includes a reporter, an official inquiry and a later theory, not the voices of those who died in their villages. The Portuguese evidence is weighted toward liberation movements, with one retrospective collection from settlers. Fanon’s examples were his own selections, and the 0.3 percent figure was Cabral’s. None of these sources can rank the causes of underdevelopment or measure how much of a colony’s wealth moved elsewhere.

Research priorities are comparative trade and investment statistics, land and labour ordinances, concession records, and testimony from peasants and workers in the relevant languages. The aim would be to establish where different mechanisms operated and with what weight.
