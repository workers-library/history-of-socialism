Nyerere, Neto, Machel and Mandela

Ujamaa and the Arusha Declaration, the MPLA and FRELIMO, and the ANC Freedom Charter tradition.

Julius Nyerere, Agostinho Neto, Samora Machel and Nelson Mandela led four different movements in four different countries. Nyerere governed Tanganyika after independence and then the United Republic of Tanzania. Neto led the Movement for the Liberation of Angola, known as the MPLA, through war and into power. Machel commanded the Mozambique Liberation Front, known as FRELIMO, and became the first president of independent Mozambique. Mandela became the public face of the struggle against apartheid in South Africa and its first democratically elected president. Each used the language of socialism, but each meant something specific by it, and each defined the power of the people in a different way. Their own texts remain the best evidence for what they intended. The work of later historians shows how far those intentions diverged from what their governments did.

Nyerere’s early life and the idea of ujamaa#

Julius Kambarage Nyerere was born on 13 April 1922 at Butiama, on the eastern shore of Lake Victoria in north-west Tanganyika, the son of the chief of a small Zanaki community. He began school at twelve, walking the twenty-six miles to Musoma, went on to Tabora Government Secondary School and trained as a teacher at Makerere University in Kampala with help from the Roman Catholic priests who taught him. After three years of teaching he took a government scholarship to study history and political economy at the University of Edinburgh. There, partly through the gradual socialism of the British Fabian Society, he began to connect socialist ideas with African communal life (MIA biography of Nyerere).

Nyerere described himself as a schoolmaster by choice and a politician by accident. In 1954 he brought the nationalist factions together in the Tanganyika African National Union, or TANU, and led it until 1977. He entered the Legislative Council in 1958, became chief minister in 1960 and premier under internal self-government. Tanganyika became independent in December 1961, and he was elected president in 1962. After the Zanzibar revolution of 1964 he negotiated the union of Tanganyika and Zanzibar that created Tanzania. He retired from the presidency in 1985, remained chair of Chama Cha Mapinduzi, or CCM, the party formed from TANU and the Zanzibari party, until 1990, and served as chief mediator in the Burundi conflict in 1996. He died of leukaemia in a London hospital on 14 October 1999 (MIA biography).

His founding text, “Ujamaa: The Basis of African Socialism,” appeared in 1962. The Swahili word ujamaa is usually rendered as familyhood, and Nyerere used it for the extended-family principle of shared responsibility, which he argued should be extended from kinship to the nation as a whole. The idea was to make the values and obligations of the family the basis of a national community. Nyerere held that humanness rather than the creation of wealth must come first, and that societies improved through the development of people rather than through the expansion of output. He collected the essays in Ujamaa: Essays on Socialism, published by Oxford University Press in 1968, and reprinted much of the material in Freedom and Unity and Freedom and Socialism (Ujamaa: Essays on Socialism, 1968; Freedom and Unity; Freedom and Socialism).

The Arusha Declaration of 5 February 1967#

Nyerere wrote the Arusha Declaration and TANU’s Policy on Socialism and Self-Reliance for his party, and TANU published it on 5 February 1967. Its creed stated that all human beings were equal, that every citizen had a right to take an equal part in government at local, regional and national level, that citizens held the natural resources of the country in trust for their descendants, and that the state must control the principal means of production in order to prevent exploitation and the accumulation of wealth inconsistent with a classless society. Its stated objects included a democratic socialist government, the elimination of poverty, ignorance and disease, support for co-operatives, equal opportunity for men and women of every race and religion, and collective ownership of the country’s resources (The Arusha Declaration).

The document defined a socialist state by work and by the absence of classes. Everyone who was physically able should work, every worker should receive a just return, and incomes from different kinds of work should not be grossly divergent. Only children, the elderly, the disabled and those for whom the state could not provide work should be supported by others. Nyerere conceded that Tanzania was a nation of peasants but not yet a socialist society, because it still contained elements of feudalism and capitalism, with the temptations that went with them (Ibid.).

On ownership, the Declaration required that the major means of production and exchange be controlled and owned by peasants through their government and co-operatives, and that the ruling party be a party of peasants and workers. It listed land, forests, minerals, water, oil, electricity, the news media, communications, banks, insurance, import and export trade, wholesale trade, the iron, steel, machine tool, armament, motor car, cement, fertilizer and textile industries, and any large factory on which many people depended (Ibid.).

Nyerere rejected the idea that state ownership alone made a country socialist. A state was not socialist simply because its government owned or controlled the means of production, he wrote. If the minority governments of Rhodesia or South Africa owned their entire economies, the result would be stronger oppression, not socialism. “True socialism cannot exist without democracy also existing in the society,” he concluded, and the government had to be chosen and led by peasants and workers themselves (Ibid.).

This was more than an institutional rule. Socialism was a way of life and could be built only by people who believed in it and practised it. A committed member of TANU would be a socialist, and his fellow socialists, meaning all who fought for the rights of peasants and workers, were to be found in Africa and elsewhere. The first duty of a leader was to accept these principles and live by them. A genuine TANU leader would not live off another person’s sweat or act in a feudal or capitalist manner. The point returned later in the document: a country that believed in socialism must believe in equality and work for it (Ibid.). Nyerere took the personal conduct of leaders seriously. The MIA biography says he did not amass a fortune through his position, unlike many politicians.

The third part of the Declaration turned to self-reliance. Nyerere described TANU as waging war on poverty and oppression and spoke of a revolution that would end the weakness that had allowed Tanzania to be exploited and disregarded. He said the country had chosen the wrong weapon in money, trying to overcome economic weakness with the weapons of the economically strong, which it did not possess. Calling on the government to spend more without raising taxes was like asking for more milk while refusing to milk the cow. External gifts could be useful if they strengthened local effort, but a nation that depended on gifts and loans for its development could not be truly independent. He warned that loans spent mainly in towns would be repaid mainly by farmers, and that if the country was not careful the real exploitation in Tanzania would be town dwellers exploiting peasants.

The conclusion returned to work, and here Nyerere paid attention to women. He said that village women worked harder than anyone else in Tanzania, sometimes for twelve or fourteen hours a day, even on Sundays and public holidays. “The people and their hard work are the foundation of development,” he wrote, and money was one of the fruits of that work. Industries and money would come, but their foundation was the people and their labour, especially in agriculture. That was his meaning of self-reliance (Ibid.). The observation about women’s hours is one of the few passages in the Declaration where a named social group appears through its daily labour.

His 1967 policy paper “Education for Self-Reliance” applied the same principle to schools. The Arusha Declaration had made more food, more education and better health the objects of the war on poverty, and had insisted that intelligence had to be joined to hard work. The Julius Nyerere Foundation describes the paper as a foundational text on progressive education in a postcolonial setting (Julius Nyerere Foundation, “Nyerere on Education”). Machel’s FRELIMO later gave the idea a wartime form. Studying, Machel wrote in 1970, was a revolutionary task to be combined with production and fighting, and the student should be like a spark lighting the flame of the people (Machel, “Educate Man to Win the War,” 1970). The two formulations are parallel, but they are separate texts written in different circumstances.

Ujamaa villages and the argument over villagisation#

The village programme, known as ujamaa vijijini, asked people to live and work co-operatively in organised villages. The MIA biography says people were encouraged and sometimes forced to do so, that nearly ten million peasants were moved, that many were effectively compelled to give up their land, and that productivity fell. It says too that the focus on human development, health, education and political identity brought some gains (MIA biography). The admission of coercion matters because later historians have made it central to their judgements.

The scholarly dispute is substantial. Zaki Ergas concluded that thirteen years after 1967 the ujamaa village policy had failed in its stated aim of building socialism in the rural areas where more than ninety percent of Tanzanians lived (Zaki Ergas, “Why Did the Ujamaa Village Policy Fail? Towards a Global Analysis,” Journal of Modern African Studies). Michael Lofchie found that collective production had ceased by 1975 and that the 1975 Villages and Ujamaa Villages Act reduced the term to block farming, leaving villagisation without socialism (Michael F. Lofchie, “Agrarian Crisis and Economic Liberalisation in Tanzania,” Journal of Modern African Studies 16, no. 3, September 1978, pp. 451 to 475). Leander Schneider argues that Nyerere bore primary responsibility for turning rural development towards coercion, a path he had publicly denounced, and that the years 1973 and 1974 saw forced resettlement on a large scale (Leander Schneider, “Freedom and Unfreedom in Rural Development: Julius Nyerere, Ujamaa Vijijini, and Villagization,” Canadian Journal of African Studies 38, no. 2, 2004, pp. 344 to 392). John Saul, writing in 2012, accepts that Nyerere spoke of empowering the people but argues that when workers, peasants and students actually acted, the state and the party cut them back, and that the language of participation collapsed into enforced villagisation from above (John S. Saul, “Tanzania fifty years on: rethinking ujamaa, Nyerere and socialism in Africa,” Review of African Political Economy, 2012). Paul Bjerk’s survey of the historiography describes a field torn between the theoretical implications of the Tanzanian experiment and the pragmatic question of sovereign authority (Paul K. Bjerk, “Sovereignty and Socialism in Tanzania: The Historiography of an African State,” History in Africa, 2014). Cranford Pratt took a more favourable view of Nyerere’s democracy, a position Saul and Schneider reject. The disagreement is not over whether the policy failed but over what its failure reveals about Nyerere’s politics. A later study of village land politics argues that ujamaa left a dual legacy: continuing centralised authority and a real capacity for democratisation from below in village institutions (“Village land politics and the legacy of ujamaa,” Review of African Political Economy, 2016).

Nyerere on democracy and the party#

Nyerere’s “Democracy and the Party System” dates from 1963. It defends the one-party system as the democratic form appropriate to Tanganyika and criticises the imported two-party model. Democracy, he wrote, was often spoken of as if it were alien to Africans and had to be taught to them. In his view it was as familiar as the tropical sun, because the traditional method of conducting affairs in an African village was free discussion until agreement was reached. He quoted the writer Guy Clutton-Brock on elders sitting under a big tree and talking until they agreed (Nyerere, “Democracy and the Party System,” 1963).

He turned the question back on the advocates of the Westminster model. Critics asked how democracy could exist under one party. Nyerere, who called himself one of the heretics who also claimed to be democrats, asked how democracy could exist under two parties. He said the people of Tanganyika had rejected the idea that an official opposition was essential, and the one-party system had resulted from a choice freely expressed at the polls. He identified a practical defect in the inherited electoral machinery: the party unity rule meant that most TANU candidates were returned unopposed and were in effect chosen by a party committee. Against that, he set the intra-party meeting, where leaders and backbenchers were on an equal footing and only the value of an opinion counted, against parliamentary discipline, where a party line approved by the leaders had to be followed (Ibid.).

In 1998 he wrote “Good Governance for Africa,” directed at the way donor countries defined and imposed multi-party democracy as a condition of aid. He recalled a meeting of the Global Coalition for Africa in Harare where the issue arose as a condition of aid, and said the manner of the exchange between African heads of state and officials from rich countries made him angry. In that usage, good governance meant multi-party elections, private ownership, free trade and a human rights record as defined by the industrialised countries of the North. It sounded to him like a tool of neo-colonialism (Nyerere, “Good Governance for Africa,” 13 October 1998).

His own definition refused to reduce democracy to elections. Democracy was government by the people, and meant more than voting every few years. It required attitudes of tolerance, a willingness to cooperate on equal terms, equality of all people within the country’s boundaries and laws that applied to all adults without exception. He criticised African presidents who amended constitutions in their own favour and sought to lengthen their terms, warning that disregard for a constitution destroyed the basis for the rule of law. He added that persistent poverty was an obstacle to good governance because democratic institutions cost money to establish and run (Ibid.).

Agostinho Neto: doctor, poet and nationalist#

António Agostinho Neto was born on 17 September 1922 in the village of Kaxicane in the region of Icolo e Bengo, the son of schoolteachers. The date and place appear in the official biography printed in the special 1979 edition of his poetry collection Sagrada Esperança, published by the Angolan Writers’ Union (Sagrada Esperança, special edition, 1979). Neto trained as a physician. His student and political life ran through the Faculty of Medicine in Lisbon, repeated arrests and deportations to Lisbon and Cape Verde, and the clandestine organising that produced the MPLA. He was repeatedly detained by the colonial security police.

He was also a poet, and the same volume’s preface describes him as a man of action respected as the mouthpiece of a people struggling for liberty, and says that the keys to everything else are to be found in his poetry. The title poem ends with images of ordinary life improving after return: more grain in the granaries, more pupils in the schools, more light from the sun, and certainty where there had been hope. Its last lines speak of the arms of men, the courage of soldiers and the sighs of poets all striving to raise the flag of independence. Another well-known poem, “Havemos de voltar,” was written in Aljube prison in October 1960 and repeats the promise of return to a liberated and independent Angola (Ibid.). These are the words of a leader in confinement, not the testimony of the civilians whose lives the poems imagine.

The biographical literature on Neto has its own history. Helder Adegar Fonseca found that writing about him was dominated for a long period by celebratory biography based on weak research, produced by internationally aligned and by Lusophone nationalist writers, many of them literary figures with political commitments. A critical historical biography with better documentary resources emerged later, but Fonseca argues that it was driven by political controversy and swung towards denigration (Helder Adegar Fonseca, “Agostinho Neto e a historiografia biográfica,” 2018). Neither tone should be mistaken for a neutral account.

Complete independence and the MPLA#

Neto’s message to the liberation movements was broadcast over Radio Tanzania in December 1972 and published by the Liberation Support Movement as Messages to Companions in the Struggle. He addressed his words not only to Angolans and comrades in the MPLA but to fighters in Mozambique under FRELIMO, and he spoke of the peoples of Guinea-Bissau, Mozambique and Angola as united in the same combat. Guerrilla-held areas were growing, he said, and a new life was opening roads towards the restoration of independence and dignity. He set out the political premise plainly. Real control of the country, whether political, economic or social, had to be in the hands of people devoted to the struggle and not in the fists of dishonest bureaucrats. Foreigners must not continue to exploit the people or extend neo-colonial rule. “We have to fight for complete independence!” he wrote, meaning political, economic and social independence together (Neto, Messages to Companions in the Struggle, December 1972).

He was equally specific about how that independence was to be protected. The struggle had to be directed by an independent party with clearly defined ideas, whose members were disciplined and had absorbed its doctrine. Leaders had to be honest, modest and active, and always to be found at the side of their people, sharing their suffering and daily sacrifices (Ibid.). This is a leader’s standard for leadership, and it is the closest Neto comes in these texts to a definition of democratic power.

Neto issued a statement on the Portuguese military coup of 25 April 1974, and in January 1975 he spoke on behalf of the three Angolan liberation movements at the signing of the Alvor Agreement (Statement on the military coup; Neto’s Alvor speech). Independence was proclaimed on 11 November 1975, and his message on that occasion was titled “Victory is Certain!” (Neto, “Victory is Certain!”).

The MPLA as a party of the working class#

The organisational change of Neto’s presidency was the transformation of the MPLA into a Marxist-Leninist party of the working class. The movement’s discussion document explained that the decision had to be taken consciously by the militants and not out of loyalty to the central committee. The organisation had to evolve so as to reflect the principal contradiction at each moment. Its membership and strategy depended on the definition of the main enemy, because its role was to unite all classes and social forces that opposed that enemy. In Angola, as the enemy changed from the slave trade to colonial capitalism and then to imperialism, the forms of opposition had changed with it (The Constitution of MPLA into a Party of the Working Class). The document recorded internal complaints about routine, elitism and factionalists presenting petty-bourgeois ideas as ultra-revolutionary. Those were the movement’s own words about itself, and they are evidence of an internal argument rather than an outside description.

The first congress, in December 1977, consecrated the MPLA as a workers’ vanguard party on Marxist-Leninist lines, and a new constitution in February 1978 stated for the first time that the party’s objective was the construction of a socialist society. Neto published a book-length statement of the new line, Avancemos na revolução com o Partido da Classe Operária, in 1977 (text).

Historians have challenged the assumption that the MPLA was socialist from the start. Nuno de Fragoso Vidal notes that as late as August 1975 Neto said the MPLA was not a Marxist-Leninist organisation and that its leadership was not Marxist-Leninist either, describing it as a heterogeneous body containing Marxist and other points of view. Vidal argues that the movement acquired its socialist label through foreign political and academic classification, through the Cold War alignments of the civil war, and through its own later adoption of the language, while its governing practice in political economy, management and foreign policy moved in a different direction (Nuno de Fragoso Vidal, “Agostinho Neto: The MPLA’s alchemist, between a rock and a hard place,” Cadernos de Estudos Africanos 47, 2024, pp. 11 to 42; Vidal, “The international and domestic fabrics of an ideological illusion: the Socialist MPLA,” Tempo e Argumento 13, no. 34, 2021). This does not cancel the 1976 and 1977 documents. It means that a statement of doctrine and a record of government are different kinds of evidence, and that the date at which a movement called itself Marxist-Leninist is a matter for investigation.

Neto’s death#

Neto died on 10 September 1979, aged fifty-six, a week before his fifty-seventh birthday. The Political Bureau of the MPLA issued a statement on his death, and Lúcio Lara delivered a funeral oration on 17 September before the urn in the Salão Nobre do Palácio do Povo. The oration was printed at the front of the special 1979 edition of Sagrada Esperança and ended with the words: the light of your example will illuminate the Angolan fatherland for ever; farewell, Neto friend; farewell, comrade president; the struggle continues; victory is certain (Sagrada Esperança, 1979; MPLA statement on Neto’s death).

Machel’s early life#

Samora Moisés Machel was born in September 1933, the third son of a peasant farmer. He succeeded Eduardo Mondlane as president of FRELIMO after Mondlane’s assassination in 1969. The introduction to his collected volume describes him as the architect of the guerrilla struggle against Portuguese colonial rule and says that after independence in 1975 his vision helped push Mozambique away from its colonial past. He died in 1986 (Samora Machel: An African Revolutionary, Selected Speeches and Writings, Zed Books, 1985). FRELIMO had been founded on 25 June 1962 through the merger of three nationalist organisations and had begun armed struggle in September 1964.

Production, study and combat#

Machel’s wartime speeches show how FRELIMO joined fighting, production and political education. In “Sowing the Seeds of Revolution,” directives for the 1971 to 1972 productive cycle, he argued that production was not only a way to eat and clothe oneself. It was also a way to overcome poverty, understand and use nature and educate people politically. The same hoe in enemy territory produced exploitation, he wrote, while in the liberated zones it produced liberation. The difference was not in the tool but in who held it and for what purpose. He used the G3 rifle to make the same point: in enemy hands it was an instrument of oppression, and captured by the movement it became a weapon for liberating the people. He described a Nyanja cultivating alongside an Ngoni, sweating with him and learning with him, and thereby breaking down tribal, religious and linguistic prejudice. Unity, he wrote, grew with the growing plant (Machel, “Sowing the Seeds of Revolution,” 1971 to 1972).

The Centre for Political and Military Training distilled this into a watchword in 1972: production, study and combat. Production supplied the material needs of war, political study gave the fighter his identity, and scientific study improved both production and combat. The same document said that the principal contradiction was whether a handful of exploiters, old or new, or the masses would control the means of production, and that production in the army was a school of independence (Machel, “Leadership is Collective, Responsibility is Collective,” 1972).

In “Educate Man to Win the War” of September 1970 Machel rejected education that merely created literate graduates detached from the revolution. Just as one could wage an armed struggle without making a revolution, he said, one could learn without being educated in a revolutionary way. Books and study were tools at the service of the masses, and the student should be a spark lighting the people’s flame (Machel, “Educate Man to Win the War,” 1970). He applied the same standard to health services, arguing in 1971 that a hospital must cleanse itself of incorrect ideas and complexes as a surgeon disinfects before an operation, so that it became a centre of revolutionary propaganda and a true FRELIMO area (Machel, “Our Health Service’s Role in the Revolution,” 1971).

The liberation of women#

Machel opened the First Conference of Mozambican Women on 4 March 1973 with a speech titled “The Liberation of Women is a Fundamental Necessity for the Revolution.” He reported that some militants wanted to postpone women’s emancipation until after independence, arguing that it would dissipate the movement’s forces and that there were too few schools and too many traditional constraints. Others claimed that local traditions had to be respected and that emancipation imposed by FRELIMO would be artificial. Machel’s reply was that women’s liberation was not an act of charity but a necessity for the revolution, its guarantee of continuity and the precondition of victory. A revolution that left more than half of the exploited and oppressed on the fringe of the struggle could not succeed. Mothers were the first educators, and they could not transmit a revolutionary education while they themselves were marginal to the process (Machel, “The Liberation of Women is a Fundamental Necessity for the Revolution,” 4 March 1973).

He explained women’s oppression in terms of property and class. Once early humans produced more than they consumed, a group could appropriate the labour of the majority. To possess women was to possess workers whose labour a husband could appropriate, and women’s oppression began with private ownership of the means of production. He warned against delay with an image of a person who refrains from fighting an alligator on the bank only to fight it in the middle of the river (Ibid.). The speech was printed in Mozambique: Sowing the Seeds of Revolution, pages 21 to 36, and FRELIMO’s own documents, including the 1968 Women’s Detachment paper and the 1969 Women’s Section brochure, show how the line was put into practice (1968 document; 1969 brochure).

The historiography of women in the liberation war complicates the official story. Allan and Barbara Isaacman found that women were central to the rural support network, recruiting, spying, carrying supplies, producing food and helping to run rudimentary social services, but were initially barred from military training and from positions of authority. They judged the Mozambican Women’s Organisation’s later record harshly (Allan and Barbara Isaacman, “The Role of Women in the Liberation of Mozambique,” Ufahamu 13, 1984). Harry West studied the experience of women in FRELIMO’s Female Detachment through their own narratives of war (Harry G. West, “Girls with Guns: Narrating the Experience of War of Frelimo’s Female Detachment,” Anthropological Quarterly 73, no. 4, 2000, pp. 180 to 194). Maria Paula Meneses used in-depth interviews with three women and further conversations with relatives and neighbours to recover the experiences of those who fought the war from home rather than in the bush. She argues that nationalist historiography acknowledged women who left home and joined the armed struggle but rendered invisible those who resisted from the household, and that official archives are patriarchal in what they choose to record (Maria Paula Meneses, “Silent Agents of Nationalist Struggle? Women of Mozambique, Fighting a War Within a War,” African Studies 81, nos. 3 and 4, 2022, pp. 324 to 339). Paolo Israel reconstructed a different kind of evidence from the same period: after the war, Makonde women guerrillas invented a flamboyant feminist version of the mapiko mask, a form previously reserved for men, and the dance spread across the plateau before eventually giving way to a less provocative genre (Paolo Israel, “Lingundumbwe: Feminist Masquerades and Women’s Liberation, Nangade, Mueda, Muidumbe, 1950s to 2005,” Kronos 39, no. 1). That invention is an act by ordinary women that the political speeches do not mention, and it shows both the reach of the revolution and the limits of the archive.

People’s power and the FRELIMO party#

Independence came on 25 June 1975. In a chapter later collected as “Establishing People’s Power to Serve the Masses,” Machel stated what the revolution was for. The aim was not to hoist a different flag, hold elections in which Black faces replaced White ones, or put a Black president in the governor’s palace. It was complete independence, people’s power and a new society without exploitation. He compared the war to plunging lice-infested clothing into boiling water, and said the new power was not abstract: it was the people themselves, with all their shortcomings, who had to wield it. Because the old society left its mark in ideas, habits and customs, the movement had to apply the scalpel of criticism and self-criticism to its own practices (Machel, “Establishing People’s Power to Serve the Masses,” in Samora Machel: An African Revolutionary, 1985).

The Third Congress of 1977 constituted FRELIMO as a Marxist-Leninist vanguard party (CC report, 1977; Third Congress document). Machel later said that the party was born from its embryo at that congress (1985 volume). In 1976 he declared that Mozambique had to defend itself against attack and support the just cause of the Zimbabwean people (Machel, 1976). The following year’s Central Committee report placed the Mozambican revolution within the world revolutionary process. John Saul’s comparison of Tanzania and Mozambique argues that FRELIMO’s leaders, like Nyerere’s, often treated workers and peasants as categories to be led rather than as people to be empowered from below, a criticism that sits uneasily beside Machel’s own language about the masses wielding power (Saul, “Tanzania fifty years on”).

Mandela, the Freedom Charter and the turn to arms#

Nelson Rolihlahla Mandela was born in 1918 in the Transkei. He joined the African National Congress, or ANC, in 1944 and helped found its Youth League. In the Defiance Campaign of 1952 he served as volunteer-in-chief and was arrested and convicted. His 1953 speech “No Easy Walk to Freedom” took its title from a passage in an article by Nehru, and described the daily forms in which ordinary Africans had discussed their grievances for decades: in homes, on trains and buses, in factories, on farms, in schools and in prison. He ended by warning that there was no easy walk to freedom anywhere and that many would pass through the valley of the shadow of death before reaching the mountain tops of their desires (Mandela, “No Easy Walk to Freedom,” in No Easy Walk to Freedom).

The Freedom Charter was adopted at the Congress of the People at Kliptown on 26 June 1955. Its preamble declared that South Africa belonged to all who lived in it, black and white, and that no government could claim authority unless it rested on the will of all the people. Its economic clauses called for the mineral wealth beneath the soil, the banks and monopoly industry to be transferred to the ownership of the people as a whole, for land to be shared among those who worked it, and for equal pay, a forty-hour week, a minimum wage, paid leave and maternity leave. It demanded an end to child labour, compound labour and contract labour (The Freedom Charter, 1955). The document joined a liberal promise of equal rights to a programme of public ownership, and that combination is why later generations could read it as both a constitutional and a socialist text.

Mandela was among 156 Congress Alliance members arrested in 1956 and charged with high treason. He recalled that the state put the ANC’s non-violent policy in issue, but the court found after about five years that the ANC had no policy of violence and acquitted the defendants on all counts, including the charge that the alliance sought to establish a communist state (O’Malley Archives, “I am Prepared to Die”). The ANC was banned after the Sharpeville shooting of 1960 and went underground. Mandela went underground inside South Africa in 1961 and left the country on 11 January 1962, and Umkhonto we Sizwe, or MK, the armed wing associated with the ANC, was founded that year. He was arrested on 5 August 1962.

In his statement from the dock at the Rivonia Trial on 20 April 1964, Mandela denied that the struggle was directed by foreigners or communists and said that everything he had done arose from his own experience in South Africa and his African background. He admitted planning sabotage, but said it followed a calm assessment of a situation in which all lawful means of opposing white supremacy had been closed by legislation. The violence chosen was not terrorism, he argued, because those who formed MK came from the ANC’s tradition of non-violence and negotiation and did not want an interracial war. He said that for thirty-seven years, until 1949, the ANC had adhered strictly to constitutional struggle, sending delegations and petitions while white governments remained unmoved. He pointed to more than 8,500 people who had defied apartheid laws and gone to jail without a single act of violence by a defier. On social and economic demands, he said Africans wanted a living wage, work suited to their abilities, the right to live where they worked, the right to own land, an end to ghettoes and a just share of the country. Above all they wanted equal political rights, because without them their disabilities would be permanent. He acknowledged that majority suffrage sounded revolutionary to whites, but argued that political division by colour was artificial. The statement closed with the ideal of a democratic and free society in which all people lived together in harmony and equal opportunity, an ideal for which he was prepared to die (Ibid.). He and seven others were convicted on 11 June 1964 and sentenced to life imprisonment.

Mandela, Marxism and the Communist Party#

In Long Walk to Freedom Mandela described how his friendships with members of the South African Communist Party, or SACP, changed his view of them. He had found it hard to justify his prejudice against the party after observing the sacrifices of people such as Moses Kotane, Ismail Meer and Ruth First. He then read Marx and Engels, Lenin, Stalin and Mao, and found himself drawn to the idea of a classless society, which he thought resembled the shared life of traditional African culture. He was stimulated by the Communist Manifesto and exhausted by Das Kapital. Dialectical materialism, the Marxist account of change through contradictions in material conditions, seemed to him both a searchlight on racial oppression and a tool for ending it. He insisted that he was first and foremost an African nationalist and did not need to become a communist to work with communists, adding that African nationalists and African communists had more in common than divided them (Mandela, Long Walk to Freedom).

Reflecting on his 1964 statement, he wrote that theoretical differences among people fighting oppression were a luxury they could not afford, and that for decades communists had been the only political group willing to treat Africans as equals, to eat, talk, live and work with them. He told the court he was not a communist and had always regarded himself as an African patriot, while admitting that he was attracted by a classless society and influenced by Marxist thought, as were many leaders of newly independent African states (Ibid.).

The SACP’s own programme, The Road to South African Freedom of 1962, set out the party’s position. It described the winning of national freedom as the condition for advance towards a socialist South Africa and a classless communist society, and defined the strategy as a national democratic revolution, its term for the alliance-based struggle to destroy white domination as the stage before socialism (SACP, The Road to South African Freedom, 1962). The programme is evidence of what the party taught. It is not evidence that Mandela and his comrades read and debated it, and no separate document here establishes that.

Whether Mandela was a member of the SACP has become a major scholarly dispute. Stephen Ellis argued in Cold War History in 2016 that Mandela joined the underground party and that the party won the strategic argument for armed struggle after Sharpeville (Stephen Ellis, “Nelson Mandela, the South African Communist Party and the origins of Umkhonto we Sizwe,” Cold War History 16, no. 1, 2016, pp. 1 to 18). Tom Lodge accepts that the evidence for membership around 1961 is strong but stresses that Mandela’s deepest loyalty remained with the ANC, and that his relationship with the left began and ended in friendship and political alliance (Tom Lodge, “Mandela and the Left,” Journal of Southern African Studies, 2019). Thula Simpson surveyed the arguments over whether the ANC had authorised the turn to arms and whether Mandela had been honest about the party’s role, concluding that the surviving evidence sets firm limits on what can be established (Thula Simpson, “Nelson Mandela and the Genesis of the ANC’s Armed Struggle: Notes on Method,” Journal of Southern African Studies 44, no. 1, 2018, pp. 133 to 148). A collective critique of Ellis’s wider account warns that his claims about SACP control of the ANC are tendentious and selectively sourced, and notes that Mandela consistently denied party membership from the Treason Trial through his autobiography (“Debating the ANC’s external links during the struggle against apartheid”). A 2019 editorial on the rewriting of Mandela’s life records the range of positions, from Ellis’s picture of manipulation by white Stalinists to Paul Landau’s argument that African communists inside the ANC took the initiative and that the SACP was the more hesitant body on the use of force (Editorial, “The Challenge of Rethinking Mandela,” Journal of Southern African Studies, 2019). Mandela’s own account, the party programme and the historians’ arguments are three distinct kinds of evidence, and none of them cancels the others.

From prison in 1975 Mandela wrote a memorandum on divisions in the movement, smuggled off Robben Island and preserved as “Clear the Obstacles and Confront the Enemy.” He argued that the Communist Party had worked with the ANC throughout its history and had been among the first organisations to take up armed struggle. He quoted with approval the view that while the imperialist powers stood with the enemy, the socialist countries supported the liberation movement, and that having two socialist countries on South Africa’s borders would affect its policies. His practical conclusion was that only a united movement could unite the country, and that the vast majority of people, including a significant section of whites, were at war with apartheid (Mandela, “Clear the Obstacles and Confront the Enemy,” 1975).

The transition of 1990 to 1994#

Mandela was released in February 1990 after the unbanning of the ANC, the SACP and other organisations. In Long Walk to Freedom he described the task as transforming an illegal underground movement into a legal mass political party (Long Walk to Freedom). Documents of the transition include the Harare Declaration of 1989 and the record of the unbanning (O’Malley archive, 1989 documents). His address to the Constitutional Assembly on the adoption of the new Constitution appears on the South African History Online site (address record).

The demands of the 1955 Charter, universal suffrage, a bill of rights, equality before the law and the transfer of mineral wealth and monopoly industry to the ownership of the people as a whole, were carried into the constitutional negotiations by the movement. The connection is visible in the Charter’s own language and in the movement’s later documents, including the ANC’s 1997 National Question in post-1994 South Africa and its 1997 Strategy and Tactics (ANC documents index). Mandela was elected president in 1994, the year his autobiography appeared.

Four meanings of democratic power#

The four leaders used similar words for different things. Putting their definitions side by side shows how much the word democracy carried in African socialist politics.

For Nyerere, democratic power meant government by the people expressed through a single party that organised discussion from below. In 1963 he argued that the one-party system was the authentic African form because village affairs were settled by open discussion until agreement was reached, and he doubted that competing parties could be democratic. By 1998 he had widened the definition. Democracy was not just voting every few years but tolerance, equality before the law, respect for constitutional limits and freedom from poverty. He applied that standard to donors and to African presidents alike. His meaning of democratic power was therefore as much about conduct and conditions as about institutions, and his practice was criticised by Saul and Schneider for allowing the party’s leadership to override the participation it proclaimed.

For Neto, democratic power meant control of the country by those who had fought for it. The 1972 message placed political, economic and social control in the hands of people devoted to the struggle and warned against dishonest bureaucrats and foreign exploitation. Power was to be exercised through an independent party with defined ideas, disciplined members and honest leaders who remained with their people in hardship. This is a vanguard conception: the party carries the revolution and is answerable to the struggle rather than to competing electoral programmes. Vidal’s work shows that the socialist language adopted in 1976 and 1977 sat alongside a governing practice that did not always match it, so the definition describes an intention and a standard of legitimacy rather than a recorded outcome.

For Machel, democratic power was people’s power in the specific FRELIMO sense: the masses holding and exercising power through their own organs, with control over the means of production. He rejected elections that merely replaced a White elite with a Black one and said the new power was the people themselves, wielding it imperfectly but with a correct line and mass support. Criticism and self-criticism were the tools for keeping that power from being captured by the habits of the old society. Women’s emancipation was part of the same definition, because a revolution that left women subordinate could not be a revolution of the people. The studies by the Isaacmans, Meneses and Israel show that the experiences of ordinary women both confirmed and exceeded this official account.

For Mandela, democratic power meant a state based on the will of all the people, expressed through universal suffrage, a bill of rights and equality before the law. The Freedom Charter joined that political programme to public ownership of mineral wealth and monopoly industry, and the 1964 statement argued that equal political rights were the necessary condition for every other gain. Unlike Nyerere, Mandela came to accept competitive multi-party elections as the route to power in 1994. Unlike Neto and Machel, he located democratic legitimacy in constitutional rules and individual rights rather than in a vanguard party controlling the economy. The scholarly dispute over his Communist Party membership does not change this public definition, which he stated in the dock and repeated in his autobiography.

The comparison should not be flattened into a single African socialist position. Nyerere distrusted party competition but insisted that socialism was impossible without democracy. Neto and Machel placed the organised movement, not the ballot, at the centre of popular power, though Machel’s language of mass control was more radical than Neto’s language of honest leadership. Mandela treated elections and rights as the foundation, with public ownership as their economic counterpart. Each definition was made in a specific struggle, and each was later measured against what its author’s government actually did. That gap between definition and record is where the historians have done their most useful work.
