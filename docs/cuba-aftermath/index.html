After the Victory: Ordinary Cubans and the Social Revolution, 1959–1971

In 1961 Felo Rojas was thirteen years old.

MarxismPart VI: Revolutionary Lives and Movements in the Americas

In 1961 Felo Rojas was thirteen years old. He slept in a hammock in a peasant house in the Cuban countryside, beneath a roof of leaves, on an earth floor, with water drawn from a well. He had been sent there to teach adults to read. “I was sleeping in a hammock. The house had a dirt floor, no running water — water came from a well — and the roof was made of leaves.” He recalled it when a radio producer interviewed him in Havana fifty-four years later (Felo Rojas, interviewed by Brian Kahn, broadcast 22 March 2015; English wording as published by Montana Public Radio, translator not stated). That hut, and thousands like it, was where the revolution’s most durable claim was lived out: that it had carried literacy and a new dignity to people the old order had left behind. At a May Day rally in Havana that year, Fidel Castro pointed to the same effort from a platform. He told the crowd that “the volunteer teachers paraded and also representatives of the 100,000 young people on their way to the interior to wipe out illiteracy” (Fidel Castro, May Day speech, 1 May 1961; English transcription in the Castro Internet Archive). The parade measured the campaign in tens of thousands. Rojas’s hammock measures what it felt like to the young people who did the work.

Teaching by lantern#

The volunteers were often children. Rosa Hernández Acosta was ten in 1961. “Well, imagine! I was born in 1951 and the Campaign started 1961, so I was 10 years old.” That was how she introduced herself in an interview sixty years later (Rosa Hernández Acosta, interview by Kian Seara Rey, 6 February 2021; translated from Spanish by the interviewer). When the assigned teacher could not stay in her village, she took on three or four adult pupils herself. She taught by kerosene lantern and waited until eight or nine at night for a pupil who worked in the fields to return. Afterwards the adults called at her house to show her that they could read the newspaper. Marietta Biosca, nineteen in 1961, kept a stricter timetable: “We arrived at their houses at four in the afternoon and taught them till ten at night.” (Marietta Biosca, interviewed by Brian Kahn, 22 March 2015; English wording as published by Montana Public Radio, translator not stated).

Not everyone wanted to learn. Pamela Rios was ten, and she met adults who turned her away. “Some said, ‘I’m too old to learn to read and write.’ I explained that reading would allow them to understand what was happening in the entire country, not just in their towns.” She remembered the conversation (Pamela Rios, interviewed by Brian Kahn, 22 March 2015; English wording as published by Montana Public Radio, translator not stated). The material conditions were as bare as the houses. A volunteer interviewed by Timoteo Delgado remembered that “where we were there was not potable water, there was not light, there were not sanitary services.” (unnamed brigadista, quoted by Timoteo Delgado, 6 June 2019). Another told Delgado she had received a death threat from an anti-revolutionary group, knowing that another volunteer had been killed, and that supervisors advised her to go home, but she refused; the government then issued her a gun for self-protection. Joanne Elvy’s oral histories record the names of two volunteers who were killed, Conrado Benítez and Manuel Ascunce Domenech (Joanne C. Elvy, “Photos from a Cuban Diary,” 10 September 2005). The work was a schoolroom and a political front line at once. Angelica Martiz gave the reason simply: “I wanted to help spread literacy because it was something the country needed.” (Angelica Martiz, quoted by Timoteo Delgado, 6 June 2019).

A change in themselves#

More than half the volunteers were young women, and many had never previously left home without an escort. In their own accounts the campaign is remembered less as a teaching exercise than as a change in how they understood their own lives. Dora, fifty-seven, from Santiago de Cuba, put it in one sentence: “Becoming a brigadista, this was the moment I took charge of my life.” (Dora, in Joanne C. Elvy, 2005; translated in the article, translator not stated). Humbelina, sixty-six, from Pinar del Río, defined the condition the campaign was meant to end: “An illiterate woman is chained to all of her responsibilities in the household.” (Humbelina, in Elvy, 2005; translated in the article). Zeida, also of Pinar del Río, spoke of what the women had proved about themselves: “Those of us who were part of it proved that together we could overcome obstacles, despite all the challenges we would undoubtedly face, socially or economically, in the future.” (Zeida, in Joanne C. Elvy, “Women in the Cuban Literacy Campaign,” 2007; translated in the article). Rosa Hernández Acosta, who went on to become a professor of pedagogy, said directly that as a poor Black woman she could not have reached university before the revolution. She believed the campaign had redirected other lives too: “All of them were no doubt motivated by the experience they had during the Campaign. It was a tremendous experience for everyone involved.” (Rosa Hernández Acosta, interview by Kian Seara Rey, 6 February 2021; translated by the interviewer).

These are retrospective accounts, recorded four decades or more after 1961, collected by researchers sympathetic to the campaign, and translated without the translator always named. Several phrases echo official campaign language, and the selection favours women who stayed in Cuba and spoke positively. What the accounts can establish is how the volunteers later understood their own biographies, and on that point they agree with one another: the campaign enlarged what women thought they were entitled to do. They cannot reconstruct exactly what was said in a hut in 1961.

The new man and the cane fields#

The leadership set out its own account of the same period in March 1965, when Che Guevara wrote a letter to Carlos Quijano, editor of the Uruguayan weekly Marcha, under the title “From Algiers, for Marcha. The Cuban Revolution Today.” The original editor’s note said the piece was meant to provoke debate. Guevara named the making of a new kind of person as one of the two pillars of socialist construction: “To build communism it is necessary, simultaneous with the new material foundations, to build the new man and woman.” (Ernesto “Che” Guevara, letter of March 1965, first published 12 March 1965; English translation from The Che Reader, Ocean Press, 2005). He argued that work should become a social duty rather than a commodity and that material incentives should be subordinated to moral ones. He placed his hope in the young: “The basic clay of our work is the youth; we place our hope in it and prepare it to take the banner from our hands.” (Guevara, same text and translation). The essay was a statement of ambition, written abroad by a leading actor defending a policy line, not a measure of what had been achieved.

The same mobilising impulse reappeared five years later in the 1970 sugar harvest. The state’s posters urged people towards the cane. One asked “And where will we be on January 2? In the cane fields!” and another carried the title “To celebrate the biggest harvest in history” (Cuban Revolution Poster Collection, posters dated 1970; catalogue titles translated by the Archive). Rafael Ocasio records that writers and editors, including Reinaldo Arenas, were conscripted into that harvest, so the mobilisation reached well beyond the sugar workforce (Rafael Ocasio, “Gays and the Cuban Revolution: The Case of Reinaldo Arenas,” March 2002). Posters are official messaging. They show what the state urged people to do, not what workers in the mills thought or did.

The other side of moral mobilisation#

The project of moral remaking had a coercive edge that the literacy stories do not carry. Ocasio documents the 1961 raids known as the “Night of the Three P’s,” the moral campaigns of 1965, and the UMAP labour camps, in which homosexuals, religious believers and political nonconformists were confined. He reproduces Fidel Castro’s 1965 answer to the interviewer Lee Lockwood that a homosexual “should not be considered politically negative” but could not be “a true Revolutionary, a true Communist militant” (reported at second hand by Ocasio). Pablo de Llano Neira, writing in El País in 2022, reported the historian Abel Sierra Madero’s argument that about 30,000 people passed through the camps, while Ocasio quotes a visitor who heard of 60,000 “felons” from Virgilio Piñera (Pablo de Llano Neira, El País, 7 November 2022). The dates conflict by several years: Gutiérrez Alea placed UMAP in 1965 to 1967, Arenas from 1963, and a college thesis described creation in 1964 and closure by 1969. Ocasio notes that no systematic account of the persecution existed before 1984. Exile witnesses such as Arenas describe systematic persecution; Argüelles, Rich and Leiner argue that the camps were a brief episode and that conditions later improved; Gutiérrez Alea accepted that UMAP was “a mistake and a scandal” while attacking the accuracy of the film Improper Conduct. Cuba legalised same-sex marriage in September 2022, which is the present-day counterweight to the 1960s record.

For two other episodes the first-hand record is visible only as an inventory. The National Security Archive lists a “Transcription of a conversation between imprisoned invaders and foreign reporters in the Naval Hospital, June 15, 1961,” an account by prisoners elected as delegates, a cablegram from the prisoners’ commission, and the agreement of 21 December 1962 between the revolutionary government and the Committee for the Liberation of the War Prisoners (National Security Archive, “Index of Declassified Cuban Documents”). A conference in Havana in March 2001 brought Brigade 2506 veterans together with Cuban officers and former Kennedy officials, but no transcript of what was said was located (National Security Archive, “Bay of Pigs: 40 Years After,” 21 March 2001). No ordinary Cuban witness of the October 1962 missile crisis appears in the material. What exists here is a catalogue of testimony, and it should be described as such rather than filled in.

How historians have read it#

  • Ada Ferrer, Cuba: An American History (Simon & Schuster, 2021). A broad narrative that places unnamed Cubans alongside presidents and generals; it argues that the revolution became socialist and anti-American after taking power rather than being so from the start. Source: adaferrer.net
  • Samuel Farber, Cuba Since the Revolution of 1959: A Critical Assessment (2011). A socialist critique that treats bureaucratic centralisation, the record on Black Cubans, homosexuals and dissidents, and the economic failures as connected rather than incidental. Source: foreignaffairs.com
  • Rafael Ocasio, “Gays and the Cuban Revolution: The Case of Reinaldo Arenas” (2002). A source-critical survey that sets exile testimony beside the arguments against it; the best single guide to the dispute over UMAP. Source: library.fes.de
  • Joanne C. Elvy, “Photos from a Cuban Diary” (2005) and “Women in the Cuban Literacy Campaign” (2007). Oral histories arguing that the campaign changed women’s sense of civic possibility; they share Ferrer’s interest in ordinary people but focus on gender rather than economics. Source: acontracorriente.chass.ncsu.edu ; Source: literacies.ca
  • Abel Sierra Madero, El cuerpo nunca olvida. A study of Cuba’s labour camps that treats memory as a site of justice and calls the state’s handling of the past a “washing of the national memory”; his argument was reported in El País in 2022. Source: english.elpais.com
  • Kimberly Waller, The Traveling Memories Project (Antioch University New England, 2022). An oral-history dissertation of brigadista recollections whose participants themselves disputed the campaign’s published statistics. Internet Archive snapshot: viewcontent.cgi

There is broad agreement that the literacy campaign sharply reduced illiteracy, that women formed a large share of the volunteers, that UMAP existed and targeted homosexuals, religious believers and political nonconformists before being closed in the later 1960s, and that the 1970 harvest was a major mobilisation. Disagreement continues over how much weight to give the social gains against the repression, whether UMAP was an aberration or part of the “new man” project, how many people it held, and whether exile testimony should be preferred to the accounts of visitors who stayed sympathetic to the revolution. The first two claims are much better documented than the precise figures.

Limits of the record#

The largest gap is first-hand testimony from Bay of Pigs prisoners. Such documents are inventoried, a 1961 hospital conversation with foreign reporters, an account by prisoners’ elected delegates, a prisoners’ commission cable, but the index offers no links and the documents could not be opened. The 2001 Havana conference brought Brigade veterans and Cuban officers together, but no transcript was located. Prisoners’ words cannot be invented; the honest treatment is to say that the voices exist in the catalogue and are not yet accessible. No ordinary Cuban witness to the missile crisis appears in the material either. The campaign’s first-hand record is lopsided: strong on young women volunteers, weak on the adults who learned, the men who taught, and the sugar workers. There is no rank-and-file mill-worker oral history here, and the official art on which the harvest account relies says nothing about conditions on the ground. The LGBT evidence reaches us through two secondary publications; neither Ocasio’s quoted memoir passages nor El País’s reported survivor account was checked against the primary memoir, film or testimony. Figures for UMAP’s population and dates of operation conflict by a factor of two and by several years. Much Spanish-language material remains out of reach: the museum and archive collections used by Elvy and Waller, the Mount Holyoke thesis, and Catherine Murphy’s documentary. The interpreter’s role matters throughout, since most English quotations are translations whose translator is not named. Finally, the surviving testimony is politically shaped. Interviewees stayed in Cuba or left it, and both groups had reasons to emphasise certain things. That should be reported rather than averaged away.
