Domenico Losurdo: Liberalism, Class Struggle and the Communist Question

A research profile of the Italian Marxist historian's counter-history of liberalism, his defence of existing socialism and the criticism it drew.

Domenico Losurdo (1941–2018) was an Italian Marxist philosopher and historian of ideas who built his work by turning the history of liberalism against itself. Liberalism, the political tradition that puts individual liberty, rights and limits on government at the centre of public life, was for him not a set of ideas to admire in the abstract but a set of institutions to examine in practice. His best-known book, Liberalism: A Counter-History, is a counter-history: an account written against the way a movement describes itself, showing how liberal societies actually worked and who was left outside them. Losurdo argued that the “community of the free” was defined against slaves, colonial peoples, wage workers and women. He also widened class struggle, the conflict between social groups over wealth and power, to cover colonial, racial and gender conflicts, and he defended the communist states of the twentieth century against what he saw as a one-sided verdict. Those arguments won him a wide readership and drew sharp objections from historians and other socialists. This profile sets out what he claimed, what can be checked in the texts, and where his critics disagreed.

Biography#

Verso’s author page gives Losurdo’s dates as 14 November 1941 to 28 June 2018 and describes him as Professor of Philosophy at the University of Urbino (Verso author page). The translators of the English edition of his Stalin book add that he was born in Sannicandro di Bari and that a tumour took his life in 2018 (Iskra translation foreword, in the Stalin PDF). Angelo d’Orsi, writing in La Repubblica and reprinted by Verso, gives the same birthplace and year and says he died of a brain tumour after a short illness; David Broder’s obituary in Jacobin also reports a brain tumour (Verso obituary by d’Orsi; Broder, Jacobin). Broder calls him seventy-seven, which does not sit with the 1941–2018 dates; the dates on the Verso page are the safer record.

His university training and teaching career are only partly documented. Guido Liguori, in an obituary published by Verso, says he studied under Pasquale Salvucci at Urbino and then taught the history of philosophy there for many years (Verso obituary by Liguori). Springer’s author note for Class Struggle calls him Emeritus Professor at Urbino, and Verso confirms the professorship (Springer book record; Verso author page). The University of Urbino keeps a personal page listing him as Professore Emerito and an institutional CV. Both were located in search results, but the CV downloaded as a fifteen-page scan with no text layer and the personal page was not opened directly. The often-repeated details that he graduated in 1963, wrote a thesis on Johann Karl Rodbertus and led the Internationale Gesellschaft Hegel-Marx from 1988 could not be independently verified from those records and remain unconfirmed here.

His political development is described consistently in three obituaries. Liguori records that he was first a militant and leader in the Italian Communist Party (PCI), then in Rifondazione Comunista (PRC), then in the PdCI and the effort to refound a PCI after 2016, and that he was president of the Associazione Marx XXI (Verso obituary by Liguori). Broder, writing in New Left Review in 2017, gives a fuller sequence: as a young militant in the 1960s Losurdo sided with the Chinese position in the Sino-Soviet split and welcomed the Cultural Revolution; in the 1980s he wrote for the PCI daily l’Unità and joined the party; after the PCI changed its name in 1991 he went with those who formed Rifondazione Comunista; from 2016 he joined the attempt to recreate a PCI (Broder, New Left Review 107). d’Orsi confirms that Losurdo was an “unrepentant communist” who joined the small party that became the PdCI and helped launch a new Italian Communist Party, and says the two men disagreed significantly about Stalin (Verso obituary by d’Orsi). A 2025 People’s World review also says he left the PCI over the Sino-Soviet split, but that is a political newspaper’s retrospective claim rather than an institutional record; Broder’s account is the firmer of the two.

Intellectual tradition#

Losurdo wrote as a Marxist philosopher and historian of ideas who refused to separate philosophy from the political conflicts in which it was produced. d’Orsi describes him as a convinced Marxist and a historical materialist who looked behind ideas for their social basis and ideological context, and whose standing as a scholar rested on the history of philosophy (Verso obituary by d’Orsi). Liguori places his early work in an Hegelian-Marxist line running from Kant and Hegel through Marx, Lukács and Gramsci (Verso obituary by Liguori). In the 2013 interview republished by Verso, Losurdo described himself as “a communist – and not only that, but also as a philosopher and historian” fighting dominant ideology (Verso republication of the l’Humanité interview).

He wrote from within the communist movement but against several currents of the left. The title and argument of Western Marxism set “Eastern” and “Western” Marxism against one another, and he used the distinction as a political and historical judgment rather than a merely geographical one (Monthly Review Press, Western Marxism; Ponce de León and Rockhill’s 2026 introduction). He rejected the “withering away of the state” as a messianic inheritance and argued that Marxism had more to learn from liberalism about limiting power than liberals had learned from Marxism (Red Sails transcript of the 2012 interview). He also rejected the Cold War category of “totalitarianism” when it placed Stalin and Hitler on the same moral plane. In the l’Humanité interview he said the term derived from “total mobilisation” and “total war”, and compared it to a “huge historical comparison” that collapses unlike cases (Verso republication). Critics from several directions therefore describe him as a Marxist-Leninist or a pro-Stalin writer, while his translators insisted that his Stalin book was “not a rehabilitation” but an attempt to contextualise and end demonisation (Iskra translation foreword).

Major contributions#

Losurdo’s best-known contribution is the method and vocabulary of Liberalism: A Counter-History. He called the book a “counter history” not because it abandoned historical evidence but because it set out to write the history of the liberal movement and liberal society “in their concrete reality” rather than “liberal thought in its abstract purity” (Liberalism, “A Short Methodological Introduction”). He borrowed Tocqueville’s complaint that histories of the old regime describe its “surface glitter” while saying little about those who could neither make themselves heard nor seen, and applied it to liberalism itself. The book’s central concept is the “community of the free”: a group whose members enjoyed rights and whose freedom was defined against populations kept outside it. Its counterpart is the “sacred” and “profane” space of liberal society, and the “exclusion clauses” that marked slaves, colonial peoples, servants, wage workers and women as outside the promised universality (Liberalism, chapters 1, 4, 7 and conclusion).

The term he made most influential was “Herrenvolk democracy”, a democracy of the master race. In chapter 4 of Liberalism he writes that students of the United States and South Africa should “speak of a ‘Herrenvolk democracy’ that is, a democracy which applied exclusively to the ‘master race’”, and that equality inside the white community was “primarily a clear line of demarcation from the excluded” (printed p. 107). He used the same category in the 2013 interview to describe Israel’s treatment of Palestinians and the claim that the West may wage war without United Nations authorisation (Verso republication). A second concept is the “tangle” or dialectic of “emancipation and dis-emancipation”: rights won by one group could be tied to the further subjection of another, and rights once won could be withdrawn. In the conclusion he gives the examples of the French Revolution’s male suffrage, abolished two years later, and the losses of Black Americans after the 1877 compromise (printed pp. 341–342).

His second major contribution is a widening of the concept of class struggle. Springer’s description of Class Struggle: A Political and Philosophical History states his claim that class struggle is not merely the poor against the rich but “a general theory of social conflict” in which conflicts are intertwined and each situation has its own characteristics (Springer book record). The publisher’s chapter list confirms that he treated struggles for recognition, the family, the “South-East passage”, Lenin in 1919, the period after the revolution and the “end of history” as parts of one argument. In the 2013 interview he named three fronts: peoples shaking off the colonial yoke, subaltern classes fighting capitalist exploitation, and women refusing the “domestic slavery” of the patriarchal family (Verso republication). Scholarly admirers describe this as a genus with several species, including national liberation and women’s emancipation (Ponce de León and Rockhill, 2026 introduction).

His third contribution is the East-West account of Marxism and the comparative method used in the Stalin book. In its introduction he asks whether the vilification of Stalin should be explained by “the initiative of a single personality or a narrow ruling class”, and argues instead for an “all-round comparative approach” that keeps in view the history of Russia, the Western countries in what he calls the “Second Thirty Years’ War”, and comparable institutions elsewhere, including liberal states (printed pp. 9–11). This produces the claim that Stalin’s gulag cannot be understood by comparing it only with Nazi camps; it must also be compared with tsarist Siberia, British penal transportation and the plantation (Stalin, table of contents and chapter 4).

Analysis of capitalism#

Losurdo did not write a conventional account of the labour process or of value. His analysis of capitalism is historical and institutional: he asks which social relations a given regime of freedom requires, and who is excluded from them. The clearest statement is in the conclusion of Liberalism. He grants that liberal thought “vigorously insisted on the need for competition between individuals in the market, in order to develop social wealth and the productive forces”, and calls this “a further, major historical merit”. He immediately adds that the liberal market was for centuries “a site of exclusion, de humanization and even terror”: enslaved people were commodities rather than buyers and sellers, white indentured servants were bought and sold, workers’ coalitions were repressed, and essential aspects of human dignity were commodified (printed pp. 343–344). The threat of being sold away from family members, he writes, imposed obedience more effectively than the lash (Liberalism, conclusion).

For Losurdo, chattel slavery and colonialism are not deviations from capitalism’s liberal history but part of its formation. Chapter 2 of Liberalism is titled “Liberalism and Racial Slavery: A Unique Twin Birth”; in chapter 1 he quotes John C. Calhoun calling slavery “a positive good” while presenting himself as a defender of liberty and minority rights against absolutism (printed pp. 1, 35). The English text traces the wealth of the British Empire to the slave trade and the asiento, and notes that people who profited from slavery sat in Parliament (printed pp. 106–107). In the final chapter he cites other historians on the deportation of Indigenous Americans, the slave trade as “the largest involuntary movement of human beings in all history”, lynching, eugenics and racial segregation, and asks whether the twentieth-century catastrophe can still be described as an invasion of a previously peaceful liberal world (printed pp. 336–340). This is an argument by juxtaposition and comparison; it depends heavily on the work of historians such as Eric Williams, C. Vann Woodward, George Frederickson and Mike Davis, whom he cites by name (Liberalism).

His later work adds an international political economy. Sympathetic editors write that, following Lenin, he located the roots of Western left Eurocentrism and national chauvinism in the political economy of imperialism, visible in the racial and exclusionary structures of “Herrenvolk imperial liberalism”, and that he treated the labour aristocracy as a material basis for reformism in the imperial core (Ponce de León and Rockhill, 2026 introduction). That is a summary by editors of his work, not a verbatim statement from one of his books. Losurdo’s own words in the 2013 interview are more direct: China’s struggle to end the Western monopoly on high technology and refuse a place in “inferior segments of the international labour market” must itself count as class struggle (Verso republication). Capitalism, in this account, is held together by coerced divisions inside the working people of the world as much as by the wage relation alone.

Analysis of socialism#

Losurdo wrote no institutional blueprint for a future society. His preferences have to be recovered from scattered arguments in interviews, the late books on class struggle and Western Marxism, and his historical studies of Stalin and China. The most consistent preference is for strong public power. In the 2012 Platypus interview he called the Marxist idea that the state would simply disappear “totally misguided” and said that he did not believe society could exist without regulation and laws. He distinguished a naive “withering away of the state” from a “withering away of the state in terms of today’s political form”, and credited Gramsci with the claim that civil society is itself a form of domination (Red Sails transcript). In the 2013 interview he repeated that Gramsci’s warning against the “myth of the withering-away of the state” helped overcome the messianism he believed damaged the building of a postcapitalist society (Verso republication).

He defended markets and competition as instruments rather than as ends. In 2012 he said that “we cannot develop a post-capitalist society, at least for a long time to come, without some form of competition”, and that Gramsci’s phrase “determinate market” was preferable to an abstract market (Red Sails transcript). In 2013 he argued that Deng Xiaoping’s turn to incentives was continuous with Mao’s goals: moral exhortation could not sustain development over a long period, and access to Western technology required opening to the world market. He treated the Chinese claim to have lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty as an achievement while acknowledging “stark contradictions” (Verso republication). His article “Has China Turned to Capitalism?”, published in International Critical Thought 7(1) in 2017, reaches a similar conclusion through a comparison with Soviet Russia: the New Economic Policy, Stalinist collectivisation and late Soviet welfare each had distinct logic, and China’s post-Mao market socialism is defended as a success (abstract and journal details).

His defence of historical communist projects rests on comparison and on the argument that judgement must use coeval standards. The translators of the Stalin book write that he sought to contextualise the material conditions of the Soviet leadership and to compare its institutions with those of other states, not to deny its “mistakes and terrible outcomes” (Iskra translation foreword). In the book itself he shows how respected non-communist figures, from Isaac Deutscher and Benedetto Croce to Thomas Mann and Harold Laski, judged Stalin and the USSR favourably in the 1940s and early 1950s, and he treats Khrushchev’s 1956 speech as a political act to be historicised rather than as the final disclosure of truth (Stalin, introduction). The positive institutional vision behind this is one of parties and states capable of defending national independence, developing productive forces, limiting power by law, maintaining a public sphere in the economy, and participating in a democratic international order that refuses the “right” of powerful states to wage war (Verso republication of the 2013 interview).

Two things should be kept apart here. The first is what Losurdo claimed about those states. The second is what the evidence establishes about them. This book treats achievements and coercion in separate chapters, including the collectivisation, gulag and workplace chapters (the Stalin chapter; what history establishes). His book is a polemic in a debate about how that record should be weighed; it is not a substitute for the record itself.

Recommended reading#

These are starting points rather than a survey of everything Losurdo wrote; the bibliography above gives the fuller record.

  • Liberalism: A Counter-History (2011). Start here for the method that made his name: he writes the history of liberal institutions in their concrete workings rather than as abstract ideas, and argues that the “community of the free” was defined against slaves, colonial peoples, servants, wage workers and women.
  • Class Struggle: A Political and Philosophical History (2016). This is the clearest statement of his widened class struggle, which treats colonial, racial and gender conflicts as intertwined parts of one general theory of social conflict.
  • Stalin: History and Critique of a Black Legend (2023). Read this to see his comparative method at work: he argues that Stalin’s gulag cannot be understood by comparison with Nazi camps alone, and must also be set beside tsarist Siberia, penal transportation and the plantation.
  • “Has China Turned to Capitalism? — Reflections on the Transition from Capitalism to Socialism” (2017). Begin here for his socialism argument in brief, because he uses the comparison with Soviet Russia and the New Economic Policy to defend China’s post-Mao market socialism as a success.
  • Western Marxism: How It Was Born, How It Died, How It Can Be Reborn (2024). This is the best entry into the East-West account of Marxism, the third of his major contributions, set out alongside the comparative method he used in the Stalin book.

Selected lectures and interviews#

The only interview read in full transcript form is the 2012 conversation with Ross Wolfe and Pam Nogales for the Platypus Affiliated Society, conducted around the English translation of Liberalism: A Counter-History. The transcript is hosted at Red Sails, which states that the original was on platypus1917.org and that a video recording exists (Red Sails transcript; video endpoint listed but not opened). In it Losurdo explains the emancipation/de-emancipation dialectic, defends his comparison of Locke and Bodin, rejects the “withering away of the state”, and describes liberalism’s legacy as the limitation of power and competition. A hostile reply to this interview by Henry Flynt appeared in Platypus Review 49 in September 2012 (Flynt, Platypus Review 49).

A 2013 interview for l’Humanité was republished by Verso on 16 July 2018 under the title “Liberalism, the most dogged enemy of freedom” (Verso republication; the original humanite.fr link was listed but not opened). It covers Herrenvolk democracy, totalitarianism, class struggle, Gramsci and China. Verso’s introduction also mentions a talk Losurdo gave at the 2007 Fête de l’Humanité at the invitation of the Fondation Gabriel-Péri; this is a reported event, not material examined for this profile. Monthly Review Press states that the English edition of Western Marxism includes “the unprecedented publication of a major lecture” on Western Marxism, but gives no date or title on the page opened (Monthly Review Press). That date and title remain unverified.

Intellectual influences#

Losurdo’s own statements name Marx, Engels, Lenin, Mao and Gramsci as sources for his widened theory of class struggle, and he repeatedly appeals to Marx’s authority while arguing that Marx himself was one-sided about the bourgeois revolutions (Verso 2013 interview; Red Sails 2012 transcript). Gramsci is especially important: he took from him the claim that civil society can be a form of domination, the idea of a “determinate market”, and a non-messianic communism understood as an “integral humanism” completing the unity of the human race (2012 and 2013 interviews; Ponce de León and Rockhill). Liguori lists Kant, Hegel, Marx, Lukács and Gramsci as the objects of his earliest studies (Verso obituary by Liguori).

In the 2013 interview Losurdo also names Rousseau and Hegel as thinkers who understood the link between economics and politics, and treats Tocqueville’s method in The Ancien Régime as a model for his own “counter history” (Verso 2013 interview; Liberalism, methodological introduction). Hegel is a continuing presence rather than a simple influence. Liguori calls Losurdo one of the great contemporary authorities on Hegel, and the 2012 interview shows him using Hegelian categories of recognition while criticising idealist and messianic readings of Marx (Verso obituary; Red Sails transcript). The Chinese revolution is an empirical and political influence: Broder records his youthful Maoist commitment, and the 2013 interview treats Mao and Deng as continuous (Broder, New Left Review 107; Verso 2013 interview).

Influence and legacy#

Losurdo published a large body of work in Italian, German, French and Spanish, but much of the English-language reception came after his death. Verso’s author page lists only a few English titles, while Monthly Review Press describes him as the author of over fifty books (Verso author page; Monthly Review Press). Broder counted some thirty-five books in Italian in 2017 and called him one of the most prolific Italian thinkers of his generation (Broder, New Left Review 107). Posthumous English editions include Liberalism (Verso, 2011 and 2014), War and Revolution (Verso, 2015 and 2020), Non-Violence (2017), Class Struggle (2016 and 2018), Stalin (Iskra, 2023), Democracy or Bonapartism (Verso, 2024), Western Marxism (Monthly Review Press, 2024) and The Communist Question (Iskra, 2026). These are publisher records opened for the research dossier.

His institutional legacy is visible in Italy and internationally. The journal Materialismo Storico, published at Urbino, devoted its volume 10, issue 1 (2021) to “Domenico Losurdo, three years after”, including an account of the first international prize named for him (Urbino journal issue page). International Critical Thought published a memorial by Stefano G. Azzarà of Urbino in volume 9, issue 2 (2019) and a special issue on Losurdo in 2026; the 2026 introduction by Jennifer Ponce de León and Gabriel Rockhill argues for the continuing relevance of his analysis of imperialism and emancipation (Ponce de León and Rockhill, 2026 introduction). The 2019 memorial record was seen as a search result only, not opened. Roland Boer reported on a seminar series in China on Losurdo’s books on liberalism and Hegel, attended by members of the Communist Party of China and teachers of Marxism (Boer, Materialismo Storico 10(1), 2021). Ross Wolfe’s 2025 essay, whatever its polemical tone, is evidence that Losurdo’s work had acquired a significant English-language readership and an organised body of promoters and critics (Wolfe, New International).

Criticisms and controversies#

The scholarly criticism of Liberalism is substantive and varied. Andrew Nurse, reviewing the book in Labour / Le Travail 69 in 2012, accepted that the story of liberal exclusions was important but argued that it had been told many times before, that the text was repetitive, that its footnotes did not always separate a secondary author’s words from those of the thinker under discussion, and that gender was barely treated. He also criticised the use of the word “redskins” and concluded that Losurdo never supplied a workable definition of liberalism, so that Burke, Gobineau, Calhoun and supporters of Mussolini could all be called liberal (Nurse, Labour / Le Travail 69). Henry Flynt’s 2012 reply went further, accusing Losurdo of taking a Benjamin Franklin remark out of context, of citing a Tocqueville passage about Haiti that he could not find, and of ignoring the historical record of actually existing communism while treating republicanism as uniquely blameworthy (Flynt, Platypus Review 49). Chris Byron’s 2013 review was broadly sympathetic but noted that the condemnation could seem “helter-skelter” (Byron, Marx & Philosophy Review of Books). Joe Cleffie, writing from a Trotskyist standpoint in International Socialist Review, praised the historical material but objected to Losurdo’s political conclusions about Marxism (Cleffie, ISR). An Italian newspaper review recorded by Laterza had already called him an “apologeta di Stalin” in 2011; only the headline was read, not the review.

The Stalin book and the later political works drew sharper criticism from historians and from the left. Guido Liguori, who admired much of the work, called Losurdo’s argument about a pro-Stalin “Fifth Column” in the 1930s USSR “at least rather dubious” and rejected the comparison between the gulag and the Jewish Holocaust (Verso obituary by Liguori). David Broder’s review of Il marxismo occidentale in New Left Review argued that the East-West contrast was “Manichean” and that “Eastern Marxism becomes an undifferentiated bloc” in which contradictions were flattened out; he also questioned the claim that nation came before class for Lenin (Broder, New Left Review 107). Douglas Kellner, writing in Logos in 2026 and republished by the International Marxist-Humanist journal, called Western Marxism a “screed” and “apologetics for Stalinism”, and argued that Losurdo ignored the anti-imperialist record of Bloch, Marcuse, the Frankfurt School and Korsch (Kellner, Aquino and So, 2026). Ross Wolfe’s 2025 essay “Against Losurdo” made the same charge in stronger language, describing an “entry of Stalinism into philosophy” and a “school of falsification”, and documented what he takes as non sequiturs, selective quotation and misreading of Perry Anderson, whom Losurdo is said to have treated as a celebrant of Western Marxism (Wolfe, New International). The research dossier could not establish whether Wolfe’s series continues beyond Part I, or whether the essay read represents the whole of that series.

Two of the most useful sustained critiques focus on the structure of the argument rather than its tone. Joshua Nicholas Pineda, in Spectre in 2025, argues that Losurdo’s category of Western Marxism has no coherent object once it includes Arendt, Foucault and Agamben; that the only principle uniting them is a supposed rejection of anticolonial struggle; that state-led industrialisation is wrongly identified with socialist revolution; and that the widening of class struggle into a general struggle for recognition drains the concept of the specific relation of exploitation it had denoted (Pineda, Spectre). Stéphane Doucet, writing in People’s World in 2025, criticised the same binary from a Marxist-Leninist position, arguing that Losurdo elevated “Third World versus West” above capital versus labour and nationalism above imperialism (Doucet, People’s World). Carlos Garrido’s dissertation at Southern Illinois University is reported in a search result as arguing that Losurdo’s critique leaves class and social function underdeveloped; the dissertation was not opened and is not relied on here.

The book also drew favourable scholarly readings. Vernon Shaw’s 2025 review in the Marx & Philosophy Review of Books defended the East-West distinction as a real geopolitical and economic division, while conceding that it was “imprecise in some ways” (Shaw, Marx & Philosophy Review of Books). Zeyad el Nabolsy’s 2024 review essay, seen only as a search excerpt, treats the Della Volpe/Togliatti debate and the critique of Bloch as serious contributions; the endpoint was blocked and it is not used as evidence here. Ponce de León and Rockhill’s 2026 introduction is an explicit defence of Losurdo’s framework (Ponce de León and Rockhill). Many of these objections come from writers within the Marxist tradition itself, and several of the journals cited here have an explicit political stance. They are reported as arguments made against or for Losurdo, not as independent findings about the history he describes (editorial source policy).

Related thinkers#

The nearest published profile in this collection is Michael Parenti: both writers argued that Western left intellectuals misunderstood actually existing socialism. No Parenti source was opened for the Losurdo dossier, so this is a thematic overlap rather than a sourced comparison. Rockhill and others connect Losurdo to Parenti’s idea of “siege socialism”, but the phrase is theirs, not Losurdo’s (Ponce de León and Rockhill; Pineda, Spectre). Antonio Gramsci is the thinker Losurdo most often claimed as a guide, particularly on civil society, the state, hegemony and “integral humanism”. Perry Anderson’s Considerations on Western Marxism is the foil for the 2017 book, and critics on both sides note that Losurdo inverted Anderson’s evaluation while depending on his framework (Broder, New Left Review 107; Pineda; Wolfe).

Hannah Arendt, Ernst Nolte and François Furet are recurring targets: Losurdo treats their comparisons of Nazism and communism as historically revisionist (Verso book descriptions; Stalin, chapter 5). Locke, Burke, Constant, Bentham, Sieyès, Calhoun and Tocqueville are the liberal writers through whom he develops the exclusion thesis (Liberalism, table of contents and text). Frantz Fanon, Mao, Hồ Chí Minh, Lenin and Sun Yat-sen appear as the anticolonial counterweight in Western Marxism (Shaw’s review; Broder’s review). Vivek Chibber is not discussed by Losurdo in the material opened for this profile, but his work is part of the later debate about whether capitalism could have thrived without colonialism; no claim of influence is made here.
