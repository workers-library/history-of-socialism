Vladimir Lenin, Joseph Stalin and the Soviet Experience

Imperialism, the vanguard party, 1917, the NEP, socialism in one country, industrialisation and collectivisation.

In the first weeks of April 1930, an officer of the Ukrainian state security service compiled a confidential report on what workers were saying in the factories of Dnipropetrovsk, Kryvyi Rih and Kharkiv. Stalin had published “Dizzy with Success” a month earlier, blaming local officials for the excesses of collectivisation and telling peasants they could leave the collective farms. In the rolling mill of the Petrovsky plant, a worker named Bizhko welcomed the article because middle peasants had been swept up in the campaign against kulaks, the better-off peasants. In the open-hearth workshop, a labourer named Gasinsky said he had been starving with his family and hoped the markets would reopen. At the Kaganovich mine, a winch operator named Moroz was blunter: “All our newspapers lie about the gains of collectivization. In practice, everything is falling apart.” The report was written for the Central Committee of the Communist Party of Ukraine and was never intended for publication. That is precisely its value. It records what people said when they did not expect their words to be read outside a police file. Source: REESOURCES, “Response from workers of the USSR to Stalin’s article ‘Dizzy with Success,’ 1930”, Central State Archives of Public Organizations of Ukraine, 1/20/3192/66-74.

Those remarks were the reception of a set of arguments about capitalism, the party and the state that had been built over more than a generation. The men who made those arguments did not write for factory workers in 1930, but the consequences of their writings reached those workers directly. This account follows the texts in the order in which they were written, and sets beside them the evidence of people who lived through the results.

Vladimir Ilyich Ulyanov, known as Lenin, was born in 1870 into a family of the raznochintsy, educated commoners who stood outside the old hereditary estates, in Simbirsk. His early revolutionary formation took place in Marxist circles during the 1880s and 1890s. He was arrested in 1895 and exiled to Siberia from 1897 to 1900, where he wrote his first major economic work. From 1900 he lived abroad, editing Iskra with Plekhanov, Axelrod and others. He became the leader of the Bolshevik faction after the split with the Mensheviks at the Second Congress of the Russian Social-Democratic Labour Party in 1903, and led the party through the 1905 revolution, the years of emigration, the First World War and the revolution of 1917. From October 1917 he served as head of the first Soviet government, Chairman of the Council of People’s Commissars, until his death in January 1924. A year-by-year record of his activity appears in the MIA’s Life and Work of V. I. Lenin.

Joseph Vissarionovich Stalin, born Djugashvili, was born in 1879 in Gori, Georgia, and was formed in the Caucasian Social-Democratic movement. He worked in the Baku oilfields strike wave of 1903 to 1904, was repeatedly arrested and exiled, and was a Bolshevik from the split. He joined the first Bolshevik Central Committee and worked on Pravda in 1912 and 1913. The MIA’s 1947 Stalin biography records that “The Pravda of 1912… was the laying of the cornerstone of the victory of Bolshevism in 1917” and that Lenin “set the highest value” on Marxism and the National Question (Chapter IV). During 1917 he directed Pravda and the party’s work in Petrograd. He served as People’s Commissar for Nationalities from 1917 to 1922, joined the Politburo in 1919, and became General Secretary of the Central Committee in April 1922. That post became the base from which he built the party apparatus that underpinned his leadership. He led the USSR through industrialisation, collectivisation, the war against Germany, and the postwar consolidation until his death on 5 March 1953.

Lenin’s first major intervention was The Development of Capitalism in Russia, written between 1896 and 1899 and published in March 1899. Its full title, The Process of the Formation of a Home Market for Large-Scale Industry, indicates its purpose. Lenin set out to refute the Narodniks, or populists, who held that capitalism was an artificial import into Russia and that the mir, the peasant commune, offered a non-capitalist path. Using a large body of statistics, he argued that capitalism had already penetrated Russian agriculture. He held that the peasantry was splitting into a rural bourgeoisie of kulaks, a middle peasantry and a rural proletariat. This process is called class differentiation. Capitalism’s “mission,” he wrote, reduced itself to two propositions: “increase in the productive forces of social labour, and the socialisation of that labour.” He paired this recognition of capitalism’s historically progressive role with “the full recognition of the negative and dark sides of capitalism” (Chapter VIII, §VI). Large-scale machine industry brought about “the complete separation of industry from agriculture” (Chapter VII, §XI). These findings became the empirical basis for his later claim that Russia had entered the capitalist path, and for his view that the revolutionary subject was the industrial working class rather than the peasantry as such.

Lenin’s contribution to Marxist philosophy sits alongside that economic work. He wrote the Philosophical Notebooks chiefly between 1914 and 1916 while studying Hegel in Bern and Zurich. They remained unpublished in his lifetime and first appeared in Russian in 1929 and 1930. They contain the “Conspectus of Hegel’s Science of Logic” and the essay “On the Question of Dialectics.” In the latter, Lenin wrote that “In its proper meaning, dialectics is the study of the contradiction within the very essence of things” and that “Development is the ‘struggle’ of opposites.” Stalin later quoted these formulations with approval in Dialectical and Historical Materialism (Stalin 1938). Together with Materialism and Empirio-Criticism (1909), a defence of objective reality and the knowability of the world against Machism, a doctrine that denied the existence of matter outside experience, the notebooks make up Lenin’s philosophical work. His aim was a materialist appropriation of Hegelian dialectics, meaning an account of change driven by material conditions rather than by ideas alone.

What Is To Be Done?, written between autumn 1901 and February 1902 and published in March 1902, is the foundational text of Lenin’s theory of the party. It was directed against Economism, the tendency to reduce the workers’ movement to trade-union struggles over wages and conditions, and against “freedom of criticism” as a cover for revisionism, the revision of Marxist principles to make them acceptable to opponents. The MIA’s own account captures the argument: “while capitalism predisposes the workers to the acceptance of socialism it does not spontaneously make them conscious Socialists. The proletariat of its own can achieve only ‘trade-union consciousness.’ Accordingly, it was necessary to institute a ‘party of a new type’ capable of imbuing the working-class with revolutionary consciousness. This is the origin of Lenin’s famous theory of the Party as ‘vanguard of the proletariat’” (notes to the MIA edition). A vanguard is a leading group whose authority comes from its clarity of purpose and its service to the wider class, not from a title alone.

In Chapter II Lenin stated the thesis that “there could not have been Social-Democratic consciousness among the workers. It would have to be brought to them from without. The history of all countries shows that the working class, exclusively by its own effort, is able to develop only trade-union consciousness.” By this he meant that workers combine in unions, fight employers and press the government to pass labour laws. “The theory of socialism, however, grew out of the philosophic, historical, and economic theories elaborated by educated representatives of the propertied classes, by intellectuals” (Chapter II; quoted and discussed in O’Toole 2015). This “socialism from without” thesis has been much discussed inside the Marxist tradition. O’Toole’s defence, published in the Marxist Irish Marxist Review in 2015, stresses two points (2015). First, Lenin was describing the actual historical origin of socialist theory, since Marx and Engels themselves came from the bourgeois intelligentsia. Second, his concern was to fuse socialist consciousness with the mass movement, not to substitute a clique for it. The academic literature is more divided. Orlando Figes’s A People’s Tragedy presents the revolution as a tragedy in which ordinary people were participants rather than passive victims, while reviewers noted that his treatment of workers is thin and that his portrait of Lenin is more hostile than the evidence requires (H-Net review by Dave Pretty; Hobsbawm, London Review of Books, 1996). Lars Lih’s brief biography Lenin (2011) is recommended as a more balanced short account in a survey of Lenin scholarship (International Socialist Review).

Organisationally, Lenin argued for a party of professional revolutionaries. Under tsarist illegality, a broad, loose organisation would be useless. What was needed was a “solid connection” between mass, legal workers’ organisations and a relatively small, disciplined, conspiratorial organisation of people “who will devote themselves exclusively to Social-Democratic activities” (Chapter IV). Chapter V developed the idea of the all-Russian political newspaper as a “collective organiser.” The vanguard was a functional concept for Lenin. In Chapter III he warned that “it is not enough to call ourselves the ‘vanguard,’ the advanced contingent; we must act in such a way that all the other contingents recognise and are obliged to admit that we are marching in the vanguard” (Chapter III).

Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism was written in Zurich during the First World War, between January and June 1916, and published in mid-1917. Lenin intended it as a “popular outline” of the economic essence of the epoch. Above all, it explained the betrayal of the Second International, meaning the support given by socialist leaders to their own governments’ war credits in 1914, a stance Lenin called social-chauvinism. The preface to the French and German editions states the political stakes: “Imperialism is the eve of the social revolution of the proletariat” (Preface to the French and German Editions). The preface to the Russian edition calls the imperialist period “the eve of the socialist revolution” and social-chauvinism “the utter betrayal of socialism, complete desertion to the side of the bourgeoisie” (Preface).

The book’s central result defines imperialism as monopoly capitalism, meaning capitalism in which a few large firms dominate whole branches of production. Lenin listed five basic features:

“(1) the concentration of production and capital has developed to such a high stage that it has created monopolies which play a decisive role in economic life; (2) the merging of bank capital with industrial capital, and the creation, on the basis of this ‘finance capital,’ of a financial oligarchy; (3) the export of capital as distinguished from the export of commodities acquires exceptional importance; (4) the formation of international monopolist capitalist associations which share the world among themselves and (5) the territorial division of the whole world among the biggest capitalist powers is completed.”

Source: Chapter VII, “Imperialism as a Special Stage of Capitalism”

The shortest definition is “the monopoly stage of capitalism.” Monopoly, Lenin stressed, “does not eliminate” free competition “but exist[s] above it and alongside it.” The result is “acute, intense antagonisms, frictions and conflicts” (Ch. VII). This point underpins the law of uneven development, the idea that capitalism develops at different speeds in different countries and that crises therefore strike at different times, and hence the possibility of revolution in one country. The argument proceeds through the concentration of production and monopolies (Ch. I), the new role of the banks (Ch. II), finance capital and the financial oligarchy (Ch. III), the export of capital (Ch. IV), the division of the world among the great powers (Ch. VI), parasitism and decay (Ch. VIII), the critique of Kautsky’s “ultra-imperialism” (Ch. IX) and imperialism’s place in history (Ch. X).

The State and Revolution was written in August and September 1917 while Lenin was in hiding from the Provisional Government, and was first published in 1918. It is the classic Marxist text on the state. Its starting point is a complaint that Marx’s theory had been turned into a harmless icon. After a revolutionary dies, “attempts are made to convert them into harmless icons… while at the same time robbing the revolutionary theory of its substance, blunting its revolutionary edge and vulgarizing it” (Chapter I). The state, Lenin wrote, is “a product and a manifestation of the irreconcilability of class antagonisms.” It arises where and when classes arise, as “special bodies of armed men” with prisons and “institutions of coercion of all kinds” (Ch. I, §§1–2). It is an instrument for the exploitation of the oppressed class. The shift from a Commune-type state to a parliamentary state changes its form but not this essence.

The proletarian revolution must therefore smash the bourgeois state machine. “The working class cannot simply lay hold of the ready-made state machinery, and wield it for its own purposes.” The task is “to smash” it, a lesson Lenin traced through Marx’s Eighteenth Brumaire, the Civil War in France and Engels’s letters. Lenin added that Engels’s treatment of violent revolution was “inseparably bound up with the ‘withering away’ of the state into one harmonious whole” (Ch. I, §4); the “ready-made state machinery” formula is quoted in Ch. III and discussed in Ch. VI. Withering away means the gradual disappearance of coercion once the conditions that require it have gone.

What replaces the smashed machine follows the Paris Commune model, the government established in Paris in 1871 after a popular uprising. All officials were to be elected, subject to recall and paid ordinary workers’ wages. There was to be the “abolition of parliamentarism,” and the reduction of the state to simple functions of accounting and control (Ch. III). Lenin quoted Marx’s point that “National unity was not to be broken, but, on the contrary, organized by the communal constitution; it was to become a reality by the destruction of state power which posed as the embodiment of that unity yet wanted to be independent of, and superior to, the nation, on whose body it was but a parasitic excrescence.” The Commune is “the form ‘at last discovered’ by the proletarian revolution, under which the economic emancipation of labour can take place.” On the transition, Lenin followed Marx’s Critique of the Gotha Programme. In the first phase of communist society, which the tradition came to call socialism, “bourgeois law” remains because equality of labour and wages is only formal equality. The higher phase, “from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs,” requires the withering away of the state. Its economic basis is the habit of observing the “simple, fundamental rules of the community” (Ch. V). The book’s polemical target is the opportunist “vulgarisation of Marxism,” represented by Plekhanov, Kautsky and Pannekoek, who “repudiated the revolution in deeds, while accepting it in words” (Ch. VI). A planned seventh chapter on the experience of the Russian revolutions of 1905 and 1917 was never written; only a detailed plan survives (MIA note).

Lenin returned to Petrograd on 3 April 1917. On 4 April he delivered the report published as The Tasks of the Proletariat in the Present Revolution, known as the April Theses (full text). Their ten points defined the transition “from the first stage of the revolution, which, owing to the insufficient class-consciousness and organisation of the proletariat, placed power in the hands of the bourgeoisie, to its second stage, which must place power in the hands of the proletariat and the poorest sections of the peasants.” There was to be no support for the Provisional Government. Instead of the “impermissible, illusion-breeding ‘demand’” that a capitalist government cease to be imperialist, the slogan was “exposure.” Lenin called for “not a parliamentary republic… but a republic of Soviets of Workers’, Agricultural Labourers’ and Peasants’ Deputies throughout the country, from top to bottom.” Police, army and bureaucracy were to be abolished, the standing army replaced by the arming of the whole people, and officials made elective, recallable and paid no more than “the average wage of a competent worker.” The landed estates were to be confiscated and all land nationalised, disposed of by local Soviets, with separate Soviets of Poor Peasants and model farms on the large estates. The banks were to be unified into a single national bank under Soviet control. Crucially, “It is not our immediate task to ‘introduce’ socialism, but only to bring social production and the distribution of products at once under the control of the Soviets.” The theses also demanded a new International against the social-chauvinists and the “Centre,” and a note called for changing the party name from “Social-Democracy” to Communist Party, because the official leaders of Social-Democracy “have betrayed socialism and deserted to the bourgeoisie.”

The February Revolution had overthrown the tsardom in March 1917, new style, through a strike-and-soldier revolt. It produced the dual power of the Provisional Government and the Soviets, a situation Lenin analysed in “The Dual Power”. Dual power meant that two authorities claimed allegiance at once, neither strong enough to displace the other. Bolshevik work inside the Soviets, the failure of the June offensive, the July days, the Kornilov affair and the radicalisation of peasants and soldiers culminated in the October Revolution of 7 November, new style, which transferred power to the Soviets. A contemporary Marxist-Leninist account of these events, including Land, Bread and Peace, the Decree on Peace, the nationalisation of the banks, workers’ control, and the “breathing space” that let Lenin elaborate “The Immediate Tasks of the Soviet Government” in spring 1918, appears in R. Page Arnot, A Short History of the Russian Revolution, Part II. The workers’ state was constituted initially as the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic, then defended in the Civil War and against foreign military intervention. Figes’s history follows the same events through the lives of soldiers, peasants and workers, and the disagreement between his account and more sympathetic studies of mass participation is a matter of historical interpretation rather than of settled fact (H-Net review; Reviews in History).

“Left-Wing” Communism: An Infantile Disorder was written in April 1920, after the defeat of the German, Hungarian and Bavarian revolutions, for publication before the Comintern’s Second Congress, which met from 19 July to 7 August 1920. It is Lenin’s principal work on strategy and tactics, meaning the long-term aims and the concrete methods of the international communist movement. Its target was the “Left” Communists in Germany, Britain and elsewhere who rejected parliamentarianism, work in the “reactionary” trade unions and all compromise. Lenin’s central charge was “Left doctrinairism”: it “persists in the unconditional repudiation of certain old forms, failing to see that the new content is forcing its way through all and sundry forms, that it is our duty as Communists to master all forms” (“Several Conclusions”). Against the slogan “No Compromises!” Lenin quoted Engels against the Blanquists. The German Communists were those who “through all the intermediate stations and all compromises created, not by them but by the course of historical development, clearly perceive and constantly pursue the final aim, the abolition of classes” (Ch. VIII). On parliament, he distinguished the propagandistic sense in which parliamentarianism was “historically obsolete,” because the era of the proletarian dictatorship had begun, from the practical question, which required concrete analysis. “World history is counted in decades” (Ch. VII). He described the Russian system, in which “The dictatorship is exercised by the proletariat organised in the Soviets; the proletariat is guided by the Communist Party of Bolsheviks,” which then had 611,000 members and relied on the formally non-Party trade unions of over four million. This was a “flexible and relatively wide and very powerful proletarian apparatus” linking party, class and masses (Ch. VI). The work also contains his discussion of the Brest-Litovsk compromise as necessary, “a compromise with the imperialists, but… a compromise which, under the circumstances, had to be made” (Ch. IV).

War Communism lasted from 1918 to 1921. It comprised the nationalisation of large industry, the grain monopoly and compulsory surplus-grain requisitioning, known as prodrazvyorstka, and the extension of state control amid civil war. Its rationale and limits were set out in Lenin’s “The Immediate Tasks of the Soviet Government”, written in March and April 1918, which declared that the Russian Soviet Republic had gained a breathing space in which to concentrate on “the most important and most difficult aspect of the socialist revolution, namely, the task of organisation.” A contemporary account of this turn is Arnot, Part II, Ch. 2. By spring 1921, crop failure, the end of the civil war and peasant resistance in the Kronstadt crisis forced a retreat. The Tenth Congress of the R.C.P.(B.) in March 1921 adopted the tax in kind and the New Economic Policy, or NEP, a partial restoration of market exchange and private trading under state control. In The Tax in Kind, completed on 21 April 1921, Lenin defined the change: “the tax in kind is a transition from War Communism to a regular socialist exchange of products.” Ruin and the impossibility of rapidly restoring large-scale industry made it urgent. The first task was “to improve the condition of the peasants” through the tax, through exchange between agriculture and industry, and through small industry (Conclusion).

Lenin was explicit about the concession involved. NEP “means reverting to capitalism to a considerable extent, to what extent we do not know.” It included concessions to foreign capitalists and the leasing of enterprises to private capitalists (“The New Economic Policy and the Tasks of the Political Education Departments”). In a report of 29 October 1921 he warned that “every step in this New Economic Policy entails a series of dangers,” and added that “we thereby gave capitalism freedom to develop. Failure to understand this means losing sight of the fundamental economic relations” (Seventh Moscow Gubernia Conference). The debate over NEP turned on whether it was a tactical retreat or a strategic reorientation. Lenin insisted it was “to be carried out ‘in earnest and for a long time’” (MIA note to the Tenth All-Russia Conference). At the same time, the commanding heights of large industry, banks, transport and foreign trade stayed in the hands of the proletarian state. The Workers’ Opposition and the “Left” attacked the concessions as a betrayal. The “Right,” associated with Bukharin and the slogan “Enrich yourselves,” later pressed them further. Lenin’s own plan for transition through state capitalism and cooperation appears in his last writings.

Lenin set out his position on the national question in The Right of Nations to Self-Determination, written between February and May 1914. It was a polemic against Rosa Luxemburg and against opportunists who wanted to drop clause 9 of the 1903 party programme (full text). He insisted that self-determination “implies only the right to secede” and that the question must be posed “within definite historical limits,” distinguishing the epoch of bourgeois-democratic national movements from the epoch of imperialism (Ch. 2). He quoted Plekhanov with approval: if the Marxists forgot the right of nations to self-determination for fear of offending Great-Russian nationalism, “the call… ‘workers of all countries, unite!’ would be a shameful lie on our lips” (Ch. 9). The conclusion summarises the programme: “Complete equality of rights for all nations; the right of nations to self-determination; the unity of the workers of all nations” (Ch. 10). The Comintern, or Third International, was founded in March 1919 on Lenin’s initiative. Its Second Congress in 1920 adopted the Twenty-One Conditions and the theses on the national and colonial question. Lenin’s “Left-Wing” Communism was written for that congress. The Fourth Congress in 1922 debated the “United Front” tactic.

The USSR was founded on 30 December 1922 as a voluntary union of the Russian SFSR, Ukraine, Belarus and the Transcaucasian SFSR. Lenin only partially endorsed this federal solution. In his last notes on The Question of Nationalities or “Autonomisation” of 30 and 31 December 1922, he criticised the “autonomisation” of the republics, meaning their absorption into a centralised Russian structure under the label of autonomy, and the behaviour of Ordzhonikidze in Georgia. He warned that “an abstract presentation of the question of nationalism in general is of no use at all” and insisted that “the sovereignty of each republic” and full and effective equality of nations be secured (full text; see also the Prefatory note to LCW vol. 36).

In the last months of his active life, from December 1922 to March 1923, Lenin dictated a cycle of programmatic letters and articles collected as Lenin’s Last Works. “On Cooperation,” written on 4 and 6 January 1923, states that “given social ownership of the means of production, given the class victory of the proletariat over the bourgeoisie, the system of civilized cooperators is the system of socialism.” The practical task was to find the “bonus” that would induce the peasantry to join cooperatives, alongside a cultural revolution, meaning a decisive rise in literacy and education. Lenin wrote that “this cultural revolution would now suffice to make our country a completely socialist country; but it presents immense difficulties of a purely cultural (for we are illiterate) and material character” (full text). Earlier, in The Tax in Kind, he had written that state capitalism was “not terrible but desirable,” listing concessions, cooperatives, commission agents and leases, and observing that “the economic basis of socialism is not yet there… It is commodity exchange with the peasantry!!” (Plan of the pamphlet).

“Better Fewer, But Better” of 2 March 1923 was Lenin’s last document. It is a sustained warning against bureaucratic haste. “Our state apparatus is so deplorable, not to say wretched, that we must first think very carefully how to combat its defects.” In building it “we must at all costs set out, first, to learn, secondly, to learn, and thirdly, to learn.” The rule was: “Better get good human material in two or even three years than work in haste without hope of getting any at all” (full text). The strategic conclusion linked this to the international situation and to the peasantry. “We must reduce our state apparatus to the utmost degree of economy,” using every saving to develop “large-scale machine industry… electrification, the hydraulic extraction of peat, [and] the Volkhov Power Project,” so as to “change horses” from the “muzhik horse of poverty” to “the horse which the proletariat is seeking.”

The “Letter to the Congress,” the so-called Testament, was dictated in sections dated 23, 24, 25, 26 and 29 December 1922, with an addition on 4 January 1923 (MIA, “Letters to the Congress,” with the publication data; text). It proposed increasing the Central Committee to 50 or 100 members and characterised leading comrades. It suggested that Stalin, whom it describes as having become “rude” in his exercise of the Secretary-Generalship, be replaced. The letter was first published in full in 1956, in Kommunist No. 9. Moshe Lewin’s Lenin’s Last Struggle reads these final writings as an attempt by a sick leader to check the bureaucracy and the dangers he saw in Stalin’s methods (University of Michigan Press; review by Ian D. Thatcher). The authenticity of the document was contested after 1956. The August 1956 issue of Turning Point called it “the ‘infamous’ forgery, the Lenin ‘Testament.’ This forgery is to the arsenal of Trotskyism what the ‘The Protocols of Zion’ forgery is to anti-Semitism” (Turning Point, “Khrushchev’s Un-Secret Speech”). This was a polemical assertion published in a journal opposed to Khrushchev, not a documented finding. Lewin’s study and the publication history of the text point the other way (MIA publication data).

Lenin suffered a series of strokes in 1922 and 1923 and died on 21 January 1924. His legacy was contested at once. The 1924 Lenin Enrollment, a mass recruitment of workers into the party named after the dead leader, and Stalin’s lectures The Foundations of Leninism canonised him as the founder of “Leninism.” The Left Opposition and later currents critical of the Stalin period claimed the mantle of “Lenin’s last fight” against the bureaucracy. The dispute over the Testament continued into the cold war and beyond.

Stalin’s first major theoretical work, Marxism and the National Question, was written in Vienna in late 1912 and January 1913 and published in March to May 1913. It appeared under the pseudonym “K. Stalin” in Prosveshcheniye Nos. 3 to 5. Written at Lenin’s suggestion, it became the Bolshevik party’s authoritative statement on the national question (full text). Its most famous contribution is the definition of a nation:

“A nation is a historically constituted, stable community of people, formed on the basis of a common language, territory, economic life, and psychological make-up manifested in a common culture.”

Source: Chapter I, “The Nation”

Stalin stressed that “it is sufficient for a single one of these characteristics to be lacking and the nation ceases to be a nation.” He directed the argument against the “idealistic” theories of the Austrian Marxists Otto Bauer and Rudolf Springer, who treated “national character” as an autonomous, “mystical” essence detached from territory and economic life. His methodological conclusion, “the concrete historical conditions as the starting point, and the dialectical presentation of the question as the only correct way of presenting it,” applied the Leninist demand for a concrete analysis of a concrete situation (Chapter III).

On self-determination, Stalin’s 1913 position formally matched Lenin’s. “A nation has the right even to complete secession,” but “this does not mean that Social-Democracy will support every demand of a nation.” The form, whether autonomy, federation or separation, “will depend on the concrete historical conditions” (Ch. II–III). In 1920 he sharpened the point in the preface to a collection of his national-question articles. Communists who “fight for the secession of the colonies from the Entente… cannot but fight against the secession of the border regions from Russia.” The question of secession “must be decided in conformity with the specific international conditions and in conformity with the interests of the revolution” (Author’s Preface, October 1920). After 1917 Stalin elaborated the positive Soviet nationalities policy in “The Policy of the Soviet Government on the National Question in Russia” of 1920, “Concerning the Presentation of the National Question” of 1921, the theses “National Factors in Party and State Affairs” for the Twelfth Congress of 24 March 1923, and “The National Question and Leninism” of 1929. Khrushchev-era MIA commentary notes that the substance of the 1923 theses was the struggle against “Great-Russian chauvinism” and local nationalism, together with the training of “national” cadres (see Theses).

The Foundations of Leninism was delivered as nine lectures at Sverdlov University in April and May 1924 and published in Pravda in Nos. 96, 97, 103, 105, 107, 108 and 111 between 26 April and 18 May 1924. It is Stalin’s most systematic exposition of “Leninism” as a distinct stage of Marxist theory (full text, chapter index). Its nine chapters cover Historical Roots, Method, Theory, The Dictatorship of the Proletariat, The Peasant Question, The National Question, Strategy and Tactics, The Party, and Style in Work.

The organising idea is that Leninism arose in the epoch of imperialism, when “the contradictions of capitalism had reached an extreme point” and “the old period of preparation of the working class for revolution had arrived at and passed into a new period, that of direct assault on capitalism.” Stalin singled out three contradictions of imperialism. The first was between labour and capital. The second was among rival financial groups and imperialist powers struggling for raw materials and a redivision of the already divided world, a struggle that included imperialist war. The third was “the contradiction between the handful of ruling, ‘civilised’ nations and the hundreds of millions of the colonial and dependent peoples of the world.” Imperialism, he wrote, exploited these peoples and in doing so created railways, factories and a native intelligentsia, thereby turning colonies from reserves of imperialism into reserves of the proletarian revolution (Ch. I). Russia, he argued, “was the focus of all these contradictions of imperialism,” and so became “the birthplace of the theory and tactics of the proletarian revolution.”

The chapters on the dictatorship of the proletariat, meaning the rule of the working class in the transition from capitalism, and on the party became the classic Soviet statements of these doctrines. The dictatorship is “the instrument of the proletarian revolution.” It is needed to break the resistance of the exploiters, to organise the construction of socialism and the elimination of classes, and to arm the revolution against foreign enemies. Because “small production… engenders capitalism and the bourgeoisie continuously, daily, hourly, spontaneously,” the transition “must not be regarded as a fleeting period of ‘super-revolutionary’ acts and decrees, but as an entire historical era” (Ch. IV). On the party, Stalin developed the proposition that “the Party becomes strong by purging itself of opportunist elements.” Under conditions of “open revolutionary clashes,” the party gains a “great and decisive importance” unknown to the parties of the Second International (Ch. VIII). The ninth chapter defined “the style of Leninism” as a combination of “Russian revolutionary sweep” and “American efficiency” (Ch. IX).

The contest over “socialism in one country” was the central theoretical and political controversy of the 1920s. In the first edition of The Foundations of Leninism in April and May 1924, Stalin wrote the then-standard Marxist formulation: “For the final victory of Socialism, for the organisation of socialist production, the efforts of one country, particularly of such a peasant country as Russia, are insufficient. For this the efforts of the proletarians of several advanced countries are necessary” (quoted in Grant & Woods). In the second edition of August and September 1924, this was replaced with the thesis that “having consolidated its power, and taking the lead of the peasantry, the proletariat of the victorious country can and must build a socialist society.” Victory elsewhere was needed only to “fully guarantee the country against intervention” (quoted in Blunden; see Stalin’s later retrospective in “Concerning Questions of Leninism,” 25 January 1926).

Stalin’s theoretical rationale rested on uneven development. “The uneven and spasmodic character of the development of the various capitalist countries under the conditions of imperialism, the development within imperialism of catastrophic contradictions leading to inevitable wars, the growth of the revolutionary movement in all countries of the world, all this leads, not only to the possibility, but also to the necessity of the victory of the proletariat in individual countries” (1926). In “The October Revolution and the Tactics of the Russian Communists” of December 1924, he identified two “specific features” of the October Revolution. The dictatorship of the proletariat arose “on the basis of an alliance between the proletariat and the laboring masses of the peasantry,” and it became established “as a result of the victory of socialism in one country,” while capitalism remained elsewhere. He added that “not only does the October Revolution need support from the revolution in other countries, but the revolution in those countries needs the support of the October Revolution.”

Trotsky’s critique came from the theory of permanent revolution, the idea that in a backward country the bourgeois-democratic tasks could only be solved by the proletariat taking power and that the socialist revolution could not be completed within national borders. Trotsky held that “the completion of the socialist revolution within national limits is unthinkable” (Trotsky, The Permanent Revolution, Ch. 10, 1931). He described the new “official position” as “the construction of an isolated national socialist society and this in the ‘shortest possible time’,” which he called “national socialism” and compared to the German SPD’s betrayal of 1914 (introduction to the German edition). In The History of the Russian Revolution he noted that “in the period of the first revolution, as also in the years between the two revolutions, the dispute concerned the dynamics of the bourgeois revolution, and not the chances and possibilities of a socialist revolution,” and that the theory of socialism in one country “was first expressed in print only twenty years later, in the autumn of 1924” (Appendix III). The Left Opposition, including at times Zinoviev and Kamenev in the “United Opposition” of 1926 and 1927, also attacked the theory as a “nationalist” deviation. They connected it to the bureaucracy’s domination, the “underestimation of the peasantry,” and later to the pace of industrialisation and the treatment of the kulak (Shachtman, Struggle for the New Course, Ch. 7; Nine Years of the Struggle of the Left Opposition). A defence of Stalin’s position from inside the tradition emphasises that “Stalin’s argument was that the spread of revolution to the West was obviously the most desirable thing, but with the delay in this Russia had no alternative but to set itself the aim of building Socialism in the belief that she had all that was necessary to complete this,” and that “the course of revolutions in the world… has in no small measure confirmed Stalin’s broad perspective” (Monty Johnstone, quoted in Grant & Woods).

Stalin’s most widely disseminated philosophical text, Dialectical and Historical Materialism, was first published in September 1938 as the philosophy chapter of the History of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (Bolsheviks): Short Course, then issued as a separate pamphlet in millions of copies. Dialectical and historical materialism is the Marxist account of change in nature, society and ideas. It opens: “Dialectical materialism is the world outlook of the Marxist-Leninist party… Historical materialism is the extension of the principles of dialectical materialism to the study of social life” (full text). Its four “principal features” of the dialectical method are nature as a connected and integral whole, nature as continuous motion and change, quantitative changes leading to qualitative changes, and contradictions inherent in all phenomena. On the last point, “the process of development from the lower to the higher takes place not as a harmonious unfolding of phenomena, but as a disclosure of the contradictions inherent in things and phenomena, as a ‘struggle’ of opposite tendencies,” a formulation that quotes Lenin’s Philosophical Notebooks. Historical materialism derives social consciousness from social being and the political and ideological superstructure from the economic base. It concludes that “the spontaneous process of development yields place to the conscious actions of men, peaceful development to violent upheaval, evolution to revolution.” The work has remained a focus of debate inside Marxism. A sympathetic account says it “succeeds… very well in respect of historical materialism, and not as well in respect of philosophical materialism” and finds “serious shortcomings” in its treatment of dialectics (Nunes). Lance Hill’s Stalin’s Little Book on Philosophy argues that its “simplification” produces “fundamental distortions.” Maoist currents criticised Stalin’s account of contradictions while continuing to use the pamphlet in study circles (see the Swedish MIA introduction).

The First Five-Year Plan of 1928 to 1932 marked the “Great Turn” away from NEP toward rapid industrialisation and the collectivisation of agriculture. Stalin’s article “The Great Turn” of 3 November 1929 declared: “We are going full steam ahead along the path of industrialisation, towards socialism… We are becoming a country of metal, a country of motorisation, a country of tractorisation.” His official rationale listed rising labour productivity in industry, successes in industrial construction and a “radical change in the attitude of the peasantry in favour of collectivisation” (analysed critically in Costello). The collectivisation campaign, especially the “liquidation of the kulaks as a class” (Stalin, 21 January 1930), created the kolkhoz system, kolkhozes being collective farms in which land and implements were owned in common and members were paid according to labourdays, the unit in which each person’s work was recorded. Stalin’s article “Dizzy with Success” of 2 March 1930 was an official correction of excesses. “The successes are substantial,” he wrote, but he criticised the “violation of the voluntary principle” in forming collective farms and the “administrative” method of dekulakisation, the removal of kulaks from the countryside. The History of the C.P.S.U.(B.), Chapter Eleven, presents the official account of “the struggle for the collectivization of agriculture (1930 to 1934)” within the context of the world economic crisis, the Japanese seizure of Manchuria and the fascists’ advent to power in Germany (official history).

The official account and the experience of the countryside were far apart. The secret police report quoted at the opening recorded workers who had been starving, who had seen “peasants thrown into the streets, and their houses were ravaged to pieces,” and who concluded that “All our newspapers lie about the gains of collectivization.” A collective farmer from the Poltava region, Mykola Reva, wrote to Stalin on 1 May 1940 after returning from work in the Far East and finding his family and neighbours still short of food. He described the famine of 1933 as a time when “people ate tree bark, grass, and even their own children,” and insisted that “the people were dying of hunger not because there was a poor harvest but because the state took their grain.” He ended by predicting that the letter would never reach Stalin and that he would be “sent to the polar bears like a common bandit for such ‘counterrevolution.’” He received six years in prison. Source: Bohdan Klid and Alexander J. Motyl, eds., Holodomor Reader: A Sourcebook on the Famine of 1932–1933 in Ukraine, CIUS Press, 2012; letter translated by Bohdan Klid. Reva wrote seven years after the event and had every reason to address Stalin in the language of loyal supplication, so his letter is not a neutral report. Its value lies in what a peasant chose to put in writing to the man he held responsible.

Historians have debated both the scale and the causes of the famine. R. W. Davies and Stephen G. Wheatcroft, using Russian and Ukrainian archives, estimate deaths from the famine in the range of four to six million and treat it as the outcome of shortages, harsh grain procurements and policy failures rather than of a single act of intention (The Years of Hunger: Soviet Agriculture, 1931–1933, Palgrave Macmillan, 2004; Mark Tauger’s review for EH.NET). Hiroaki Kuromiya has argued that the evidence does not support the claim that Stalin deliberately caused the famine in order to kill millions (Europe-Asia Studies, 2008). This is a live scholarly disagreement, and it should not be confused with the political claim, made inside the communist tradition, that the famine was invented or that it was a deliberate act of genocide. Stephen Kotkin’s study of Magnitogorsk reconstructs the industrial side of the same years through archives and interviews, following blast-furnace workers and families who struggled with housing and services (Magnetic Mountain: Stalinism as a Civilization, University of California Press, 1997). Sheila Fitzpatrick’s Everyday Stalinism examines how city dwellers coped with queues, overcrowding, shortages and the purges, and how many nevertheless believed they were taking part in a historic transformation (Oxford University Press, 1999; H-Russia review). Trotsky’s The Revolution Betrayed acknowledged the industrial achievement: “Socialism has demonstrated its right to victory… not in the language of dialectics, but in the language of steel, cement and electricity” (Ch. 1). Using the categories of Thermidor, meaning a conservative reaction after a revolution, and degenerated workers’ state, the claim that a workers’ state had been captured by a bureaucracy, he argued that bureaucratic privileges were incompatible with the claim that socialism had been achieved (Ch. 3; Ch. 5). C. L. R. James, writing as a Marxist historian, offered a similar Left Opposition reading of the “liquidation of the kulak” and the famine (James, The World Revolution 1917–1936, Ch. 11).

The 1936 Soviet Constitution, known as the “Stalin Constitution,” was adopted on 5 December 1936 by the Extraordinary Eighth Congress of Soviets. Article 1 states: “The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics is a socialist state of workers and peasants.” Article 2 says that “the Soviets of Working People’s Deputies… constitute the political foundation.” Article 4 defines the economic basis as “the socialist economic system and socialist ownership of the instruments and means of production.” Article 5 distinguishes state, or all-people’s, property from cooperative-kolkhoz property. Article 13 defines the USSR as “a federal state, formed on the basis of the voluntary association of Soviet Socialist Republics having equal rights” (full text). Stalin’s report of 25 November 1936 explained the commission’s task as “further democratizing the electoral system by replacing not entirely equal suffrage by equal suffrage, indirect elections by direct elections and the open ballot by the secret ballot” and “bringing the Constitution into conformity with the present relation of class forces in the U.S.S.R.,” meaning that it registered in law the abolition of the exploiting classes (Stalin, “On the Draft Constitution”). The 1944 amendments added union-republican military formations and the right of union republics to direct diplomatic relations (1944 amendments). Trotsky’s contemporary critique, “The New Constitution of the USSR” of 16 April 1936, argued that the secret ballot and territorial representation replaced factory-based Soviets. He called this “the abolition of Soviets” by a bureaucracy that “raised itself on the NEP.” The two readings were opposing positions in a political debate, and neither can be treated as a neutral description of what the constitution did.

The companion doctrine was that class struggle sharpens under socialism as the exploiting classes are liquidated and the dictatorship of the proletariat is strengthened. The Soviet legal theorist Evgeny Pashukanis wrote in his 1936 self-criticism that “Despite the basic construction of a socialist classless society, it must be understood that the class struggle continues. Further work is essential both for the socialization and re-eduction of the working masses and for the suppression of recalcitrant and hostile elements.” He added: “The state apparatus – a coercive apparatus – is crucial in combating the enemies of socialism. Finally, it is also necessary to defend ourselves against capitalist encirclement.” Source: E. Pashukanis, “State and Law under Socialism,” 1936. Pashukanis wrote this in his own name, in an attempt to bring his earlier theories into line with Stalin’s policies. He was arrested and disappeared in 1937. This was the theoretical background to the Great Purge and the Yezhovshchina of 1936 to 1938, the mass arrests named after Nikolai Yezhov, the head of the NKVD, the People’s Commissariat for Internal Affairs. The official Comintern journal presented the “lessons of the trial of the Trotsky-Zinoviev terrorist center” in 1936 (Communist International, Vol. XIII, No. 11). NKVD Order 00447 of 30 July 1937 set targets for the repression of former kulaks and other “anti-Soviet elements,” dividing them into those to be shot and those to be imprisoned, and local authorities routinely exceeded the targets (Seventeen Moments in Soviet History, “Mass Repressions”).

The purges reached ordinary citizens as well as party leaders. On 10 December 1938, a man named V. Chernousov wrote to the Central Committee: “As an ordinary citizen of the USSR I cannot help but comment on the removal of Ezhov from the NKVD leadership,” adding that “hundreds of thousands of absolutely innocent, honest people, some of them even devoted to Soviet rule, have been arrested and exiled.” Source: V. Chernousov, letter to TsK VKP(b), 10 December 1938, in Lewis Siegelbaum and Andrei Sokolov, eds., Stalinism as a Way of Life, Yale University Press, 2000, Document 85. Chernousov was writing after Yezhov’s fall, when criticism of the security police had become possible, and his letter is a measured appeal rather than an underground protest. Khrushchev’s 1956 speech gave a figure from a party commission, stating that 98 of the 139 members and candidates of the Central Committee elected at the Seventeenth Congress, 70 per cent, were arrested and shot (Khrushchev, full text). That figure comes from a commission with a political purpose, namely Khrushchev’s campaign against the Stalinist leadership, and it must be read as such. It is also a fact that the commission had access to NKVD archives and that later archival research has confirmed the broad scale of the terror (J. Arch Getty, review of Fitzpatrick, London Review of Books, 2000; Melanie Ilic, “Women’s Experiences of 1937”, University of Gloucestershire). Bill Bland’s defence of Stalin rests on a single reported remark by Stalin to Lion Feuchtwanger in 1936, that the cult of personality was built up by political opponents “with the aim of discrediting him at a later date” (Bland, Stalin: The Myth and the Reality). This is a politically motivated assertion with no corroborating documentary support, and it cannot be placed alongside the documented record of the purges as an equal account of what happened.

On 22 June 1941 Nazi Germany invaded the USSR. The Soviet war effort, called the Great Patriotic War in Soviet usage, ended with the defeat of the German Army at Stalingrad in 1942 and 1943, the advance of Soviet forces into Eastern Europe, and the capture of Berlin in May 1945. Stalin’s wartime leadership is treated in the Soviet historical account in History of the C.P.S.U.(B.) and in the Stalin Internet Archive’s 1940s index. The postwar order that followed included the pro-Soviet states of Eastern Europe, which Soviet usage called people’s democracies, the Chinese People’s Republic in 1949, and the Korean and Vietnamese revolutions. The wartime alliance with the Western powers and the postwar division of Europe were assessed critically from a Trotskyist standpoint in Blunden, which also notes the 1939 Nazi-Soviet pact and the annexation of the Baltic states.

The “two camps” doctrine originated with Andrei Zhdanov’s report to the founding conference of the Cominform, the Communist Information Bureau, in Poland on 22 September 1947. Zhdanov argued that “the division of the political forces operating on the international arena into two major camps: the imperialist and anti-democratic camp, on the one hand, and the anti-imperialist and democratic camp, on the other,” had become the central fact of postwar politics (Zhdanov, “New Aspects of World Conflict: The International Situation”, Seventeen Moments in Soviet History). Stalin’s Economic Problems of Socialism in the USSR of 1952, written as a reply to a textbook discussion and published on the eve of the Nineteenth Party Congress, restated the country-bloc description in the phrase “the countries which have fallen away from capitalism,” naming “the U.S.S.R., China and the other people’s democracies” (Reply to Notkin, 21 April 1952). That was a later formulation, not the origin of the doctrine.

Economic Problems of Socialism in the USSR was Stalin’s last major work (full text and contents). Its central theses concern objective economic laws under socialism, meaning laws that operate independently of human wishes: “Marxism regards laws of science, whether they be laws of natural science or laws of political economy, as the reflection of objective processes which take place independently of the will of man. Man may discover these laws, but he cannot change or abolish them.” This was directed against those, notably Voznesensky, who held that the laws of political economy were “created” by the state under socialism (quoted in Bland). Commodity production and the law of value remain necessary, Stalin argued, because of the two forms of socialist property, state or all-people’s property and collective-farm property. The law of value operates but is not the regulator of production; planning uses it “in the interests of society.” Relations of production do not always act merely as a fetter on the productive forces. Under socialism they can “fully conform” for a period, a point directed against Yaroshenko (“Concerning the Errors of Comrade L. D. Yaroshenko”). The basic economic law of socialism is “the ensuring of the maximum satisfaction of the constantly rising material and cultural requirements of the whole of society through the continuous expansion and perfection of socialist production on the basis of higher techniques,” contrasted with the basic law of capitalism, the extraction of maximum profit (Ch. 7). The transition from socialism to communism requires raising collective-farm property to the level of public property and replacing commodity circulation with “direct exchange of products,” or produkto-obmen, meaning the direct exchange of goods without money, between state industry and the collective farms “by way of gradual transitions” (Reply to Sanina and Venzher). The disintegration of the single world market and the deepening crisis of world capitalism, along with the “inevitability of wars between capitalist countries,” follow from uneven development (Ch. 6). The Congress enshrined the work’s pro-heavy industry stance as official policy for the Fifth Five-Year Plan (Bland’s account of the Congress). Trotskyist currents read the same passages about the survival of commodity production as evidence that Soviet society had not yet reached socialism. That is an internal debate within the Marxist tradition, not a finding that all historians accept.

At the Twentieth Congress of the CPSU on 24 and 25 February 1956, Nikita Khrushchev delivered a closed report, “On the Cult of Personality and Its Consequences,” denouncing Stalin’s “crimes” and the cult of personality, the exaggerated public veneration of a single leader (full text; abstract). Khrushchev argued that the cult “became at a certain specific stage the source of a whole series of exceedingly serious and grave perversions of Party principles, of Party democracy, of revolutionary legality.” The editorial header on the MIA page carrying the speech states that “This speech would ultimately trigger a world-wide split” (MIA Khrushchev speech page). That sentence belongs to the archive’s introduction, not to the MIA Encyclopedia of Marxism. The Encyclopedia says that “Khrushchev’s denunciation of Stalin in his secret speech had profound consequences on the international movement” and that “The Twentieth Congress opened the process in which Khrushchev would enunciate the policy of ‘Peaceful Co-existence’ and would ultimately lead to the Sino-Soviet Split and the rise of ‘Euro-communism’,” the loosely coordinated tendency among west European communist parties to seek an independent path (MIA Encyclopedia, “Twentieth Congress of the CPSU”).

The anti-revisionist current, which held that Khrushchev had abandoned revolutionary Marxism, rejected the whole framework. The MIA Encyclopedia states, in the same entry, that “the ‘cult of personality’ and some ‘errors’ Stalin may have made provided the formula to accommodate the most unspeakable crimes against the working class” (Twentieth Congress entry). This is a political judgement from within the communist tradition, not an assessment of evidence. The Turning Point polemic of August 1956 went further, calling the Testament a forgery and comparing its role to a notorious antisemitic fabrication (Turning Point). What followed the Congress is a matter of record. The Sino-Soviet split developed through the 1960s, Eurocommunism emerged in the western parties, and communist movements divided over the meaning of 1956. Those divisions cannot be settled by treating Khrushchev’s report and its critics as equally supported accounts of the same events. The report rests on documentary work by a party commission; the counter-claims rest largely on political assertion.

Stalin died on 5 March 1953, and Lenin on 21 January 1924. Between them they had shaped the first socialist state and the language in which its defenders and critics argued. Lenin’s early research put the industrial working class at the centre of revolution. His party theory, his account of imperialism and his writing on the state defined how a revolutionary party should organise and what it should do with power. Stalin systematised these ideas into “Leninism,” defended socialism in one country and led the USSR through industrialisation, collectivisation and war. The consequences of those choices were recorded not only in party documents but in the letters of peasants, the conversations of workers and the appeals of citizens who wanted the terror to end. The arguments over NEP, the Testament, the purges and the 1956 report continued long after the men themselves were dead, because each generation of communists returned to the same texts to decide what had been achieved and what had gone wrong.
