John Pilger: Investigative Journalism Against Empire

A research profile of the journalist's reporting on Cambodia, East Timor and Western foreign policy, his documentary method and his critics.

John Pilger (1939–2023) was an Australian-born reporter and documentary maker who worked mainly in British newspapers and television. He made the victims of war, sanctions and authoritarian allies the subjects of mass-market reporting, and argued that news organisations helped those in power by repeating official accounts and dropping stories that did not fit them. This profile sets out what he claimed, the evidence he offered and the objections raised against him. It treats his work as a record of arguments and contested events, not as settled history. His method was investigative journalism: reporting that starts from documents and witnesses gathered by the reporter rather than from official briefings, and publishes facts that governments would rather keep hidden.

Biography#

John Richard Pilger was born in Sydney on 9 October 1939 and died in London on 30 December 2023, aged 84. The Guardian obituary by Anthony Hayward names his parents as Elsie (née Marheine), a teacher, and Claude Pilger, a carpenter, and says he attended Sydney Boys’ High School. His own biography says he was born and grew up in Bondi, launched a newspaper at Sydney High School and completed a four-year cadetship with Australian Consolidated Press. The two accounts agree on the outline but use slightly different school names, so the precise institution should not be stated more narrowly than the sources allow (Guardian obituary, 1 January 2024; official biography).

According to the Guardian, he joined Australian Consolidated Press in 1958, worked on the Sydney Sun and then the Daily and Sunday Telegraph, freelanced in Italy, moved to Britain in 1962, joined Reuters as a subeditor and moved to the Daily Mirror in 1963. His own career summary gives the same sequence with different boundaries: 1958–62 reporter and sub-editor in Sydney; 1962 freelance correspondent in Italy; 1962–63 on the Middle East desk at Reuters; 1963–86 reporter and chief foreign correspondent at the Daily Mirror; 1986–88 editor-in-chief and a founder of News on Sunday; 1969–71 reporter for World in Action at Granada; and from 1974 to 2023 an independent documentary maker working largely with ITV. These are first-party and obituary records, not audited personnel files.

The Guardian reports that the apartheid government banned him from South Africa in 1967 and that he was metres away when Robert Kennedy was assassinated in Los Angeles in 1968; his own biography puts him “in the same room”. He covered Vietnam, Cambodia, Bangladesh, Biafra and the Middle East, and reported from the United States during the 1960s and 1970s. The Guardian says he won the British Press Awards’ Journalist of the Year title twice, first for Vietnam and second for Cambodia, and received the United Nations media peace prize. His own list adds the 1991 Richard Dimbleby Award from BAFTA, an Emmy, the 2003 Sophie Prize, the 2009 Sydney Peace Prize and the 2017 Order of Timor-Leste. The Guardian gives pulmonary fibrosis as the cause of death; the BBC report of 31 December 2023 confirms the date and place but gives no cause. He was survived by his partner Jane Hill, a son Sam and a daughter Zoe (BBC News).

Two disputes shaped his public standing and belong here. After his first film, The Quiet Mutiny (1970), the US ambassador in London complained to the Independent Television Authority, which reprimanded Granada. The regulator’s successor, the IBA, required his films to be introduced as a “personal view”. Pilger called the regulator’s chief programme officer David Glencross “commercial television’s chief censor” and regarded demands for objectivity, balance and impartiality as “code for the establishment view of the world, against which most perspectives are measured”. Separately, his claim in Cambodia: The Betrayal (1990) that SAS members had trained Khmer Rouge guerrillas led Pilger and Central Television to lose a libel action. Oliver Kamm says the case was settled at substantial cost; the court record was not opened for this profile.

Intellectual tradition#

Pilger belonged to campaigning, dissident journalism rather than to a political party or a school of political theory. He wrote no systematic doctrine, and no source opened for this profile shows him claiming membership of a socialist party or accepting a Marxist label. Equally, no first-party statement was found in which he accepted or rejected “socialist”, “Marxist” or “anti-imperialist” as a description of himself. His tradition is inferred from his own writing and from the company he kept in print and on film, and is stated as such.

In an address published by the British Library he said documentary film had the “power” of “facts and witnesses”, and that its proper function was to subvert official lies: “This subversion of official lies is the power of documentary.” He placed himself among film-makers who “dared put cameras and microphones in front of ordinary Britons and allowed them to talk in their own language”, and said the form “should be the medium of people, not authority” (British Library, edited 2017 lecture). His quarrel with broadcasters was over the meaning of impartiality. He described how the ITA questioned the political loyalty of a chicken in his first film, and said the regulator’s serious purpose was to police the power of a documentary that let young American soldiers speak for themselves. The Guardian also reports his 2006 remark at a Columbia University panel that “journalists in the so-called mainstream bear much of the responsibility” for the devastation and lives lost in Iraq by failing to challenge “the lies of Bush and Blair”. That is a theory of media complicity, not a theory of the state or of class power in the conventional socialist sense.

Major contributions#

His central contribution was to put the victims of Western foreign policy and of authoritarian allies at the centre of mass-market newspaper reporting and prime-time television, and to make the media itself part of the story. Year Zero: The Silent Death of Cambodia (1979), made with the director David Munro, followed a Daily Mirror issue largely given over to his dispatches from Cambodia after the fall of Pol Pot. The amount said to have been raised is reported inconsistently: the Guardian obituary gives $45 million in relief, his official site says “almost $50 million”, and Pilger told the British Library in 2017 that “Year Zero raised more than $55 million” worldwide. These are claims from sympathetic sources, not an audited total. The Guardian says the film was watched in 50 countries by 150 million viewers and won more than 30 international awards; Pilger’s own talk says it was shown in more than 30 countries and that 26,000 first-class letters arrived at ATV’s offices in the first post after the British broadcast. Neither set of figures can be treated as checked statistics. The history of the period is covered in this book’s chapter on Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge.

His East Timor work included Death of a Nation: The Timor Conspiracy (1994), made after undercover travel, and Stealing a Nation (2004), about the expulsion of the Chagos Islanders to make way for a US base. His Iraq films, Paying the Price: Killing the Children of Iraq (2000) and Breaking the Silence: Truth and Lies in the War on Terror (2003), made sanctions and the “war on terror” subjects of mainstream ITV documentary. The British Library’s 2017 acquisition notice describes his career as revealing “stories of peoples who have been ignored by the mainstream media or left otherwise without voice”, and notes that The Quiet Mutiny broke the story of resistance and disintegration among US troops in Vietnam. That method is worth setting against embedded reporting, in which a reporter travels with a military unit and depends on it for access and protection: the point of Pilger’s early film, as the British Library tells it, was that young soldiers spoke for themselves (British Library festival notice). See also the chapters on the Vietnam Revolution and Indonesia in 1965.

Declassified UK reported in January 2024 that the British government took this body of work seriously enough to open a file on him in 1975 and to discuss counter-measures against his Cambodia films in 1980 (Declassified UK). The underlying file was not opened directly for this profile, so this is a finding of that investigation rather than a document examined here.

Analysis of capitalism#

Pilger’s account of capitalism was that of an investigative reporter, not an economist. In The New Rulers of the World he argued that globalisation — the opening of national economies to cross-border trade and investment — was a political project imposed by powerful states and corporations. The book’s official description says it gathers original work and expanded essays on “power, its secrets and illusions”, discloses how “up to a million Indonesians died as a price for being the World Bank’s ‘model pupil’”, describes Iraqi suffering under the Western embargo, examines Aboriginal dispossession in Australia, and after September 11 outlines what he called a new thrust of US power and its goal of “world order”. Verso’s edition summary describes the book as revealing “the secrets and illusions of modern imperialism”, beginning with General Suharto’s seizure of power in Indonesia in the 1960s as part of a western design to impose a “global economy” on Asia. These are descriptions of his argument, not verified historical findings; the casualty figure is Pilger’s claim (official book page; Verso).

He returned to corporate power in the 1984 film Burp! Pepsi v Coke in the Ice Cold War, which the British Library calls “an early example of investigative film-making that used originality and wit to examine the power of multinational corporations”. In Utopia (2013) he linked mining capital to Aboriginal poverty, recalling that “in 2010” the Australian mining industry spent A$22 million opposing a tax on super-profits and that the revenue forgone was estimated at A$60 billion, “enough to fund land rights and to end Aboriginal poverty”. Those are figures presented in the film; the underlying Treasury or industry sources were not opened. In The Dirty War on the NHS (2019) he attacked privatisation of public health care, arguing that the NHS was “crippled by bed shortages, the starvation of resources and accelerating privatisation” when COVID arrived (film page).

In a 2016 address he identified Edward Bernays’s “invisible government” and “engineering the consent” of the public as the mechanism by which corporate and state power sustains itself, and quoted the former Foreign Office official Carne Ross saying that during the Iraq sanctions “We would feed journalists factoids of sanitised intelligence, or we would freeze them out. That is how it worked.” (2016 address). Roy Hattersley, reviewing the book in 2002, accepted that Pilger’s account of what Indonesians paid for the IMF bail-out was “brilliant” but argued that “he writes about ‘the myth of globalisation’ as if it were a confidence trick rather than an economic inevitability”, and that “the analysis confuses the arguments against poor countries risking the penalties of reneging on their debts and those in favour of a rich country agreeing to forgo repayments”. That objection should accompany any use of Pilger’s economic claims (Hattersley, 2002).

Analysis of socialism#

Pilger wrote no blueprint for a socialist society and no systematic account of socialist institutions. This profile therefore describes his positive commitments rather than inventing a programme for him: public provision, land rights, national independence from great powers, and the right of colonised and indigenous peoples to control their own resources. In Utopia he ended by calling for “a genuine treaty that shares this rich country, its land, its resources and opportunities” with Aboriginal Australians, adding that “until we give back their nationhood, we can never claim our own”. In The Dirty War on the NHS he defended what the film’s own description calls “the world’s first public health service” against privatisation. In Freedom Next Time he framed the book as being “about empire, its facades and the enduring struggle of people for their freedom”, covering South Africa, India, Palestine, Afghanistan and the Chagos Islands (book page).

He reported from countries with socialist or post-revolutionary governments without offering a general theory of them. The Guardian obituary notes that his Cambodian reporting laid part of the blame on the United States’ secret bombing and on Western reluctance to give substantial aid to the government then in power in Phnom Penh. Oliver Kamm’s 2024 criticism alleges that his Cambodia films served as “propaganda on behalf of the Vietnamese puppet regime” and that he withheld abuses by that government; that is Kamm’s charge and is not established here. Nothing opened for this profile shows Pilger defending the Soviet model, the Chinese model or a party programme, and nothing shows him renouncing a socialist label in so many words. The accurate description is that his normative core was anti-imperialist and egalitarian, and that he judged existing states case by case.

Recommended reading#

These selections are starting points into Pilger’s reporting, not a list of all his books and films.

  • The New Rulers of the World (2002). It presents globalisation as a political project imposed by powerful states and corporations and exposes what Verso’s summary calls the secrets and illusions of modern imperialism, making it the clearest statement of his economic argument.
  • Freedom Next Time (2006). It frames the book as being about empire, its facades and the enduring struggle of people for freedom across South Africa, India, Palestine, Afghanistan and the Chagos Islands, making it the best guide to his positive commitments.
  • Tell Me No Lies: Investigative Journalism and Its Triumphs (2004). It collects investigative journalism of the kind that puts witnesses and overlooked victims on the record rather than repeating official accounts, making it the best starting point for his method.

Selected lectures and interviews#

The British Library held a two-day documentary festival on 9–10 December 2017 to mark its acquisition of Pilger’s digital archive. On the Saturday he gave a talk on “The Power of the Documentary”; the library published an edited version as “John Pilger: Why the documentary must not be allowed to die” on 21 December 2017, with his permission. That text is the best available first-person account of his method. It contains the extended story of the ITA’s reaction to the chicken in The Quiet Mutiny, his account of Peter Watkins’s banned The War Game, his praise for Patricio Guzmán’s The Battle of Chile and his account of the public response to Year Zero. On the Sunday he was in conversation at the same event. Access is to the edited published text, not to a checked transcript of the recording (British Library lecture).

The BFI’s “Hidden Truths: John Pilger and the Power of Documentary” season ran in October–November 2024. “The Pilger Effect” on 28 October 2024 was an illustrated career overview by Anthony Hayward followed by a discussion with Ken Loach, Christopher Hird, Richard Creasey and Victoria Brittain (BFI programme note). The BBC’s Desert Island Discs interview by Sue Lawley, first broadcast in February 1990, is available as a 40-minute archive recording; the programme page says the conversation covered his arrival from Australia, his years at the Daily Mirror, and the “fame and admiration, as well as criticism and controversy” his campaigning style brought (BBC programme record). A 2014 Columbia University panel on Breaking the Silence: War, Lies and Empire is attested only through the Guardian obituary’s report; no audiovisual record of it was opened.

His documentaries, as dated in the official filmography, include Vietnam: The Quiet Mutiny (1970), Year Zero: The Silent Death of Cambodia (1979), Cambodia: Year One (1980), The Truth Game (1983), Burp! Pepsi v Coke in the Ice Cold War (1984), The Secret Country: The First Australians Fight Back (1985), Cambodia: Year Ten (1989), Cambodia: The Betrayal (1990), War by Other Means (1992), Cambodia: Return to Year Zero (1993), Death of a Nation: The Timor Conspiracy (1994), Vietnam: The Last Battle (1995), Inside Burma: Land of Fear (1996), Breaking the Mirror: The Murdoch Effect (1997), South Africa: Apartheid Did Not Die (1998), Paying The Price: Killing the Children of Iraq (2000), The New Rulers Of The World (2001), Palestine Is Still The Issue (2002), Breaking the Silence: Truth and Lies in the War on Terror (2003), Stealing a Nation (2004), The War on Democracy (2007), The War You Don’t See (2010), Utopia (2013), The Coming War on China (2016) and The Dirty War on the NHS (2019). The films index gives different posting dates for some titles, so these dates should be read as the filmography’s, not as independently checked release dates (filmography; films index).

Intellectual influences#

The clearest early influence was his newspaper training under Brian Penton. In his official biography Pilger quotes himself: “Devised by a celebrated, literate editor, Brian Penton, the aim was economy of language and accuracy.” He credits that discipline with teaching him writing “that was spare, precise and free of cliches”, free of the passive voice and sparing with adjectives. In his 2016 Sheffield address he quoted Chekhov’s “When the truth is replaced by silence, the silence is a lie” via the Russian poet Yevtushenko, and opened with Edward Bernays’s account of “engineering the consent” and the “invisible government”. These are explicit debts in his own text.

In the British Library talk he named the film-makers who shaped his idea of documentary: Peter Watkins, John Grierson, Denis Mitchell, Norman Swallow, Richard Cawston, Patricio Guzmán, Edward R. Murrow and his collaborator David Munro. He wrote that Charles Denton, a BBC producer, “taught me that facts and evidence told straight to the camera and to the audience could indeed be subversive”. Wilfred Burchett is the most contested influence. Pilger included Burchett in his 1983 Channel 4 series The Outsiders and wrote the foreword to the 2007 anthology Rebel Journalism: The Writings of Wilfred Burchett, calling him “one of the greatest journalists and war correspondents Australia has ever produced”. Oliver Kamm identifies Burchett as “the model of his journalistic mentor” and alleges that Burchett knowingly repeated communist propaganda; the influence is documented, the moral judgement is Kamm’s. The American media critic Edward Herman is another documented reference: in his 1999 Kosovo piece “Acts of Murder”, reproduced on his site, Pilger quoted Herman’s account of “normalisation” and “a division of labour in doing and rationalising the unthinkable” (“Acts of Murder”). Verso’s page for the book carries a blurb from Noam Chomsky calling Pilger’s work “a beacon of light in often dark times”, but a publisher’s endorsement is weaker evidence of intellectual influence than Pilger’s own citations.

Influence and legacy#

The British Library’s acquisition in 2017 is the strongest institutional measure of his legacy. Its November 2017 announcement describes a digital archive of his journalism “covering print, film and radio broadcasts over six decades”, produced from his personal collection by Florian Zollmann, bringing together “nearly 1,500 news reports, films and radio broadcasts”, including articles from the Daily Mirror, Guardian and New Statesman, BBC Radio, and 60 films. A December 2017 post gives a slightly different breakdown: “some 60 films, 90 radio broadcasts and more than 1,400 articles”. The two counts should be quoted with their dates rather than merged. The BFI devoted its 2024 “Hidden Truths” season to his work, and its programme note says the BFI includes Year Zero among the ten most important documentaries of the twentieth century. His own site lists the Sophie Prize (2003), the Sydney Peace Prize (2009), the Order of Timor-Leste (2017) and the Gary Webb Freedom of the Press Award (2023).

Government records, as reported, show that his reporting was treated as a political problem. Declassified UK reported in January 2024 that the Foreign Office’s Information Research Department opened a file on him in 1975, that an official wrote “It must be hoped that John Pilger and his sort do not become influential in their current affairs coverage”, and that in September 1980 a British embassy official in Bangkok said his Cambodia work “deserves a rejoinder” before his film was shown. The Foreign Office sought a journalist willing to write a “hatchet job”; the plan was abandoned because of “the reluctance of one journalist”. The article says many documents have been removed from the file and some remain classified until 2041. The links it gives to National Archives catalogue descriptions were followed, but the catalogue returned a bot challenge, so the documents are known here only through the report’s quotations.

Criticisms and controversies#

Roy Hattersley’s Guardian review of The New Rulers of the World (19 July 2002) begins by saying “The brilliance of John Pilger’s reporting is, or ought to be, beyond dispute”, then argues that “his judgments are predictably black-and-white” and that “the notion that those he exposes and denounces might have any merit has never entered his head”. He calls Pilger “unscrupulous in his assaults on his enemies”, gives the example of noting that a BBC correspondent had later become an assistant to the NATO secretary general as a way of insinuating bias, and concludes that Pilger is “a great deal better at saying what is wrong than suggesting what might be put in its place”. Hattersley reports the common reaction “If Pilger’s for it, I am against it” while insisting that “more often than not, he is right”. This is a criticism of method and tone from a writer broadly sympathetic to the book’s aims, and it should be read with the economic objection quoted above.

Oliver Kamm’s Telegraph article of 5 January 2024 is far more severe. He argues there was “an essential continuity” in Pilger’s work rather than a late deterioration, and quotes a reporter who had worked closely with him: “Pilger was a polemicist who went out looking for what he wanted to find.” Kamm alleges that Pilger “fabricated his conclusions in order to accord with his premises” and “said things he knew to be untrue, and withheld things he knew to be true and material”. His specific charges concern a 1982 investigation of child slavery in Thailand that accepted a staged “purchase” of a girl, exposed by the Far Eastern Economic Review and by Auberon Waugh in The Spectator, with a libel action settled out of court; Lawrence Freedman’s description in The Price of Peace (1986) of “a tendentious television documentary … riddled with errors of its own”, which Kamm identifies as The Truth Game (1983), with errors itemised by Freedman and William Shawcross in New Society; the Cambodia films’ defence of the Vietnamese-backed government and their accusation that aid agencies lied about food aid; the SAS libel action; and, on Kosovo, a denial of Serb genocide, the quoting of William Cohen and David Scheffer out of context, a final count of 2,788 war dead, a claim of “up to 38 aircraft crashed or shot down” by NATO, and a 2016 assertion that the ICTY had “quietly cleared” Slobodan Milošević of war crimes including Srebrenica. Each of these is reported as Kamm’s charge. The New Society exchange, the Far Eastern Economic Review report, Waugh’s piece, the court papers, the transcripts he cites and the ICTY record were not opened, so none is independently established here (Kamm, 2024).

One part of that record could be checked. The page “Acts of Murder”, posted on Pilger’s site on 1 August 2011, states: “Nato is suffering significant losses. Reliable alternative sources in Washington have counted up to 38 aircraft crashed or shot down, and an undisclosed number of American and British special forces killed. This is suppressed, of course.” No source is named on the page, and the claimed losses were not checked against NATO records. The 2016 “ICTY cleared Milošević” sentence could not be found: the searchable 2016 address says Milošević “was prosecuted in The Hague” but contains no such sentence, and a site search for “Srebrenica” returned nothing (site search). Kamm also records a demeaning description of Barack Obama in 2008, the view that Hillary Clinton was more dangerous than Donald Trump in 2016, and a December 2021 sentence: “It was the US that overthrew the elected govt in Ukraine in 2014 allowing Nato to march right up to Russia’s western border.” The BBC independently records that in a 2014 Guardian article Pilger called Vladimir Putin “the only leader to condemn the rise of fascism in 21st-century Europe”, and that he told RT the 2018 Salisbury nerve-agent attack was “a carefully constructed drama in which the media plays a role”.

The BBC also records more measured dissent. John Simpson said that although he disagreed with Pilger over the years, “I admired the force of his writing, even when I often didn’t support what he wrote, and he was always warm when we met.” The Guardian obituary’s remark that “the ferocity of rightwing criticism of his views indicated the effectiveness of his journalism” is itself an advocacy claim and is not used here as an explanation of the criticisms set out above. This profile follows the site’s editorial source policy: charges are attributed to their authors, and material that was not opened is not presented as verified.

Related thinkers#

The nearest allies are the media critics Noam Chomsky and Edward Herman, whose argument that commercial media manufacture consent parallels Pilger’s own account of propaganda and omission. Pilger quoted Herman directly in “Acts of Murder”; Chomsky supplied the endorsement on the Verso edition of The New Rulers of the World. Kamm notes that Chomsky and Herman wrote a 1977 article complaining that US newspapers emphasised Khmer Rouge atrocities and downplayed the American role in Cambodia, and treats that as part of his indictment of Pilger’s Cambodia coverage; the article itself was not opened here. Guardians of Power connects to Pilger through his foreword and through the shared claim that corporate ownership and government appointments distort news, though the official page does not name the authors, so their identity should be checked against the book before use. Wilfred Burchett is both an influence and a point of dispute, as set out above. Anthony Hayward’s In the Name of Justice is the principal book-length study of Pilger’s television reporting, and the BFI used Hayward as the presenter of its 2024 career overview. The British Library’s acquisition was assembled by Florian Zollmann, whose work on propaganda and the media is acknowledged in the archive notice.

For a contrasting approach to the same foreign-policy debates, see the companion profile of Michael Parenti, whose arguments rest on a class analysis of existing states that Pilger did not attempt in the sources opened here. No direct exchange between the two is recorded in those sources, and none is claimed. The broader question of how movements use media is treated in this book’s chapter on movement media.
