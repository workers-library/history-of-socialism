Mark Fisher: Capitalist Realism and Cultural Power

A research profile of the cultural theorist's claim that capitalism has become unthinkable to replace, its sources and the criticisms it drew.

Mark Fisher was a cultural theorist whose central claim was that capitalism had become the unquestioned frame of reality. He called this capitalist realism and defined it in the opening chapter of his 2009 book as “the widespread sense that not only is capitalism the only viable political and economic system, but also that it is now impossible even to imagine a coherent alternative to it” (Fisher, Capitalist Realism, 2009). The claim belongs to the study of ideology: here, a set of ideas that makes the existing social order appear natural and inevitable, rather than something people made and could change. Fisher asked how that order is lived in work, education, culture and mental health, and where its openings might be. This profile reports his arguments as arguments, records the sources on which they rest, and sets out the objections other writers made.

Biography#

Mark Fisher was born in Leicester on 11 July 1968 and died on 13 January 2017, aged 48. Repeater Books, his publisher, gives the years 1968–2017, and The Wire’s obituary supplies the full dates. The research brief for this profile gave 1967 as the birth year. Every source opened for the dossier that states a birth date gives 1968, including The Wire, Simon Reynolds’s memorial in The Guardian and Repeater’s author biography, and the 1967 date could not be verified. It is flagged here as a discrepancy in the brief and is not used. Reynolds said Fisher took his own life after an intermittently severe struggle with depression. Carl Freedman reported that Fisher’s widow, Zoë, had stated the date of death, and that Fisher left a wife and a young son; the LARB memorial names the son as George. These are contemporaneous recollections by colleagues rather than a civil record; no birth or death certificate was examined (The Wire, 16 January 2017; Freedman and others, LARB, 11 March 2017).

Fisher’s education and early career ran through the philosophy department at the University of Warwick. The Wire reports that he made music in the early 1990s with the group D-Generation, which released a twelve-inch record called Entropy In The UK, and that he entered the orbit of the Cybernetic Culture Research Unit (CCRU) in the middle of that decade. The CCRU was conceived by Sadie Plant and Nick Land and, according to The Wire, was never formally founded or dissolved; after Plant left, Fisher became one of its driving forces. He completed a PhD in 1999 titled Flatline Constructs: Gothic Materialism And Cybernetic Theory-Fiction. Simon & Schuster’s current publisher record describes the thesis as completed in 1999 during the CCRU period and lists a published edition dated 1 April 2025. He then worked as a philosophy lecturer in Kent (The Wire, 2017; Simon & Schuster, Flatline Constructs).

A decisive break came in 2003. The Wire records that, despondent at the pressures of further education and impatient with academic structures, he began the blog k-punk. Fisher explained the shift in a 2010 interview quoted by The Wire: blogging was a more informal space and “a way of tricking myself back into doing serious writing”, because a blog post did not carry the weight of an academic paper. The blog’s live archive runs from September 2003 to July 2015, with posts on philosophy, music, film and politics. In 2004 he co-founded the discussion forum Dissensus with the blogger Woebot (Matthew Ingram), again according to The Wire. He joined The Wire as a contributor in 2007 and became Acting Deputy Editor in 2008 (The Wire, 2017; k-punk archive).

Fisher’s later institutional base was Goldsmiths, University of London, in the Visual Cultures department; Repeater Books and Reynolds confirm this, with Repeater describing him as a Visiting Fellow. He also worked as a commissioning editor for Zero Books, the imprint with which he had forged a close alliance after leaving The Wire. Reynolds identifies Tariq Goddard as the founder of Zero Books and says Fisher worked alongside him; Repeater’s own author page says Fisher was a co-founder of Zero Books and, later, of Repeater Books. Reynolds notes that much of Fisher’s income in his last years came from sessional teaching and freelance writing, and Jeremy Gilbert recalls him living in Felixstowe and commuting to London while supporting a family. Gilbert’s account is a friend’s recollection, not an institutional record (Repeater author page; Reynolds, The Guardian, 18 January 2017; LARB, 2017).

Intellectual tradition#

Fisher belongs to the Western Marxist tradition of cultural theory, but his route into it was unusual. The CCRU phase was saturated with Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, cybernetics, dance music and Nick Land’s speculation that capital’s dissolving flows might be accelerated rather than resisted. Reynolds records that in the 1990s Fisher shared the CCRU’s enthusiasm for those border-dissolving flows and “scorned socialism as a decrepit early 20th-century relic”. By the time he launched k-punk he had moved left and, in Reynolds’s words, “for a while identified as a communist”. In his final years Reynolds heard “pragmatic notes”, including talk of working inside the Labour Party, which he connected to the later Corbyn phenomenon. Gilbert gives a similar arc: Nietzschean neoliberalism in the CCRU years, then a “Lacanian Marxist phase” around 2009 shaped “essentially just by Žižek”, with some Badiou, and finally a return to something closer to classical cultural studies (Reynolds, 2017; LARB, 2017).

The Frankfurt School connection needs to be stated precisely. No source opened for the dossier has Fisher describing himself as continuing an Adorno and Horkheimer line. What can be verified is that in the opening chapter of Capitalist Realism he discusses Adorno through Jameson: Jameson’s work on postmodernism “began with an interrogation of the idea, cherished by the likes of Adorno, that modernism possessed revolutionary potentials by virtue of its formal innovations alone”. Fisher’s own concern is what happens when modernism is absorbed into popular culture and survives only as a “frozen aesthetic style, never as an ideal for living”. Carl Freedman, in the LARB memorial, went further and placed Fisher’s concept alongside Lukács’s reification, “Horkheimer and Adorno with the dialectic of enlightenment”, and Althusser’s Ideology. That is Freedman’s judgment about the stature and lineage of the concept, not a quotation from Fisher, and it is presented as such here. No opened source records Fisher rejecting a label applied to him, so no such rejection can be reported (Fisher, 2009; LARB, 2017).

Other threads run through the same period. Fisher drew on Slavoj Žižek’s account of ideology and belief through action, on Alain Badiou’s treatment of the possible and the “Communist Hypothesis”, on Fredric Jameson’s periodisation of postmodernism, and on Marx and Engels’s Communist Manifesto, which he quotes at length in Capitalist Realism. He borrowed “hauntology” from Jacques Derrida, as The Wire records, and applied it to music and culture. His later “acid communism” turned toward the counterculture, psychedelia and anti-psychiatry. The Weird and the Eerie draws on H. P. Lovecraft, H. G. Wells, M. R. James, Christopher Priest, Joan Lindsay, Nigel Kneale, Daphne du Maurier, Alan Garner and Margaret Atwood, with films by Kubrick, Glazer and Nolan, according to Repeater’s book description (The Wire, 2017; Repeater, The Weird and the Eerie).

Major contributions#

The central contribution is the concept of capitalist realism. Fisher’s own definition is quoted at the start of this profile. The slogan he was glossing is, in his phrasing, “the phrase attributed to Fredric Jameson and Slavoj Žižek, that it is easier to imagine the end of the world than it is to imagine the end of capitalism”. The attribution matters: Fisher popularised the formula and built a theory around it, but he did not claim to have coined the line. Dan Hassler-Forest made the same point about the term itself in the LARB memorial: “While the phrase had been used before in different contexts, Mark’s definition helped crystallize decades of work in Marxist critical theory into one potent concept” (Fisher, 2009; LARB, 2017).

His specification of the concept is broader than a claim about disbelief in alternatives. He argues that capitalist realism operates as a “lived ideological framework”, a phrase used in the Zero Books and City Lights descriptions, colouring work, education, culture and mental health. In the book he calls it “not a particular type of realism; it is more like realism in itself”. He identifies business ontology as one of its mechanisms: “capitalist realism has successfully installed a ‘business ontology’ in which it is simply obvious that everything in society, including healthcare and education, should be run as a business.” He names reflexive impotence as its subjective form: people “know things are bad, but more than that, they know they can’t do anything about it”, a knowledge that functions as “a self-fulfilling prophecy”. He adds depressive hedonia, a condition “constituted not by an inability to get pleasure so much as it by an inability to do anything else except pursue pleasure”. He calls the audit culture that accompanies neoliberal management market Stalinism, and treats Thatcher’s “there is no alternative” as “as succinct a slogan of capitalist realism as you could hope for” (Fisher, 2009; Zero Books, archived 28 December 2015; City Lights, revised edition).

His second major contribution is hauntology, developed across Ghosts of My Life and his music writing. The Zero Books distributor’s description says the book “argues that we are haunted by futures that failed to happen”, tracing those lost futures through David Peace, John le Carré, Christopher Nolan, Joy Division and Burial. The Wire credits Fisher with being instrumental in coining the term in its music-critical sense, borrowed from Derrida, to describe a culture that “looks backwards”. The third book, The Weird and the Eerie, develops two categories of fiction concerned with “the outside and the unknown”, which Repeater’s description says are “not intrinsically horrifying, even if they are always unsettling” (Simon & Schuster, Ghosts of My Life; The Wire, 2017; Repeater, The Weird and the Eerie).

The unfinished acid communism project, known through the introduction printed in the k-punk collection and through Postcapitalist Desire, links political consciousness-raising to psychedelic experience and the failed promises of the 1960s (Repeater, k-punk collection; Repeater, Postcapitalist Desire).

Analysis of capitalism#

Fisher’s account of capitalism is less an analysis of exploitation at the point of production than an account of how capitalism becomes the unquestioned frame of reality. He writes that “capitalist realism can only be threatened if it is shown to be in some way inconsistent or untenable; if, that is to say, capitalism’s ostensible ‘realism’ turns out to be nothing of the sort.” His method is to find those inconsistencies in ordinary experience. One is bureaucracy: far from sweeping away rigid hierarchy, post-Fordist capitalism multiplies audits, targets and self-assessment, so that workers become their own auditors and public services are run to satisfy measurement rather than their purpose. A second is mental health. In his account, depression, addiction and attention difficulties are not simply private pathologies caused by “chemical imbalances in the individual’s neurology and/or by their family background”; treating them that way “rules out” any question of “social systemic causation”. A third is cultural sterility, the suspicion that “the future harbors only reiteration and re-permutation”, which he explores through Children of Men (Fisher, 2009).

He treats the 2008 crash as confirmation rather than refutation of capitalist realism. In the book’s conclusion, the bank bailouts were “a massive re-assertion of the capitalist realist insistence that there is no alternative”: letting the banking system fail “was held to be unthinkable”, and public money flowed into private hands. Yet he also argues that the crash destroyed the ideological cover for accumulation built since the 1970s, leaving neoliberalism as “inertial, undead defaults” rather than a confident project. That opens a strategic possibility: “The long, dark night of the end of history has to be grasped as an enormous opportunity”, because “even glimmers of alternative political and economic possibilities can have a disproportionately great effect”. The book closes: “From a situation in which nothing can happen, suddenly anything is possible again” (Fisher, 2009).

It is therefore misleading to describe Fisher as simply a fatalist. His diagnosis is deliberately claustrophobic, but his stated politics are that mental distress should be converted into antagonism: “We must convert widespread mental health problems from medicalized conditions into effective antagonisms.” He calls for struggle over who controls work, for worker autonomy against management, for the withdrawal of the “machineries of self-surveillance” that auditors depend on but that do not improve education, and for an argument that a left could deliver “a massive reduction of bureaucracy”. He also makes a case for a “new austerity” and collective rationing of resources on ecological grounds, while insisting that the form such collective management should take is “an open question, one that can only be resolved practically and experimentally” (Fisher, 2009).

Analysis of socialism#

Fisher wrote no blueprint for a socialist society, and this profile says so plainly. What he offers are strategic constraints and a negative definition. An effective anti-capitalism must be “a rival to Capital, not a reaction to it”; there “can be no return to pre-capitalist territorialities”. He rejects what he sees as the left’s “romantic attachment to the politics of failure”, its “endless rehearsal of historical debates” over Kronstadt and the New Economic Policy, and the “comfortable position of a defeated marginality”. He also rejects reactionary anti-modernism: the left cannot simply oppose flexibility and decentralisation, because “calls for inflexibility and centralization are, to say the least, not likely to be very galvanizing”. A new left, he writes, could “begin by building on the desires which neoliberalism has generated but which it has been unable to satisfy”. He warns that without a credible alternative, “capitalism could revert to a model of social democracy or to a Children of Men-like authoritarianism” — social democracy here is treated as a capitalist fallback, not as the goal (Fisher, 2009).

The nearest he came to a positive programme in the years after the book was the pamphlet Reclaim Modernity: Beyond Markets Beyond Machines, written with Jeremy Gilbert for Compass and published, by Gilbert’s account, in late 2014. The pamphlet itself was not opened for the dossier. Gilbert describes the argument in the LARB memorial: that working-class people were not nostalgic for an unspecified golden age but for “the very sense of modernity as such”, for a future in which they had a place; that a twenty-first-century socialism “would have to recover some of the radical democratic legacy of the New Left”, the tradition covered in this book’s chapter on the New Left; and that the questions of community and authority raised by Blue Labour could only be addressed “from a radically democratic perspective”. Gilbert also says he and Fisher were “always trying to encourage [each other] to engage more directly with the mainstream left”, and Reynolds reports Fisher speaking pragmatically about working within Labour (LARB, 2017; Reynolds, 2017).

The unfinished Acid Communism project points in another direction. Gilbert describes Fisher exploring whether “raising consciousness” in the political sense — class consciousness and the forms promoted by women’s liberation, gay liberation and black power — could be connected to the consciousness-expansion sought by psychedelic and anti-psychiatry movements. Repeater’s description of Postcapitalist Desire likewise presents Fisher asking “what new forms of desire we might still excavate from the past, present, and future”, from the failure of the 1970s counterculture to left accelerationism. None of this amounts to an institutional design. It is a theory of the desires and of the structures that block collective agency (LARB, 2017; Repeater, Postcapitalist Desire).

Recommended reading#

These selections are starting points into Fisher’s writing, not a list of everything he published.

  • Capitalist Realism: Is There No Alternative? (2009). It defines capitalist realism as the sense that capitalism is the only viable system and no coherent alternative can be imagined, then traces it through work, education, culture and mental health, making it the necessary first stop.
  • Ghosts of My Life: Writings on Depression, Hauntology and Lost Futures (2014). It develops hauntology, the sense of being haunted by futures that failed to happen, through music and culture, making it the best guide to that strand of his work.
  • The Weird and the Eerie (2017). It develops the two categories of the weird and the eerie as ways of thinking about the outside and the unknown, making it the clearest short example of his cultural analysis.
  • Reclaim Modernity: Beyond Markets Beyond Machines (2014). Written with Jeremy Gilbert, it argues that a twenty-first-century socialism has to recover the radical democratic legacy of the New Left, making it the best guide to the positive programme he sketched.

Intellectual influences#

Fisher’s own book supplies the clearest evidence. He writes that “what is currently called realistic was itself once ‘impossible’”, citing Badiou, and quotes Badiou’s claim that modernisation makes “impossible what used to be practicable (for the largest number), and making profitable (for the dominant oligarchy) what did not used to be so”. He endorses Žižek’s claim that liberal communism, far from being a corrective to capitalist ideology, “constitutes the dominant ideology of capitalism now”. He adopts David Harvey’s and Badiou’s description of neoliberalism as “Restoration” — a return of class power rather than a novelty — and quotes Harvey’s formulation of neoliberalisation as “a political project to re-establish the conditions for capital accumulation and to restore the power of economic elites”. He names Brecht, Foucault and Badiou together as theorists of the need to destroy the appearance of a natural order. Marx and Engels supply the epigraph-length quotation from the Communist Manifesto about drowning “the most heavenly ecstasies of religious fervor” in “the icy water of egotistical calculation” (Fisher, 2009).

Beyond the book, the influences are documented by colleagues. Reynolds identifies Deleuze and Guattari, Spinoza and H. P. Lovecraft among the k-punk “canon”, and traces the accelerationist school to the CCRU’s legacy. Gilbert says Fisher never lost his earlier philosophical sources but gained a “much crisper, clearer framework” from Žižek and Badiou. The Wire records the Derrida borrowing for hauntology and Fisher’s 2005 audio essay londonunderlondon, made with Justin Barton and inspired by J. G. Ballard. Hassler-Forest in the LARB memorial names Jameson, Žižek and Deleuze and Guattari as the “notoriously baroque theorists” Fisher made accessible, and compares him to Walter Benjamin. Freedman groups him with Lukács, Adorno and Horkheimer, and Althusser. These are colleagues’ and scholars’ accounts of influence, not a self-inventory by Fisher (Reynolds, 2017; The Wire, 2017; LARB, 2017).

Influence and legacy#

Capitalist Realism was a commercial and intellectual success for a very small publisher. The Wire calls it a “word of mouth success in 2009”; Reynolds calls it “Zero’s smash hit”. Freedman reports that he teaches it in a postgraduate seminar on Marxist theory and judges that Fisher’s contribution “could well turn out to be as durable as theirs”. Hassler-Forest writes that “no other contemporary author has managed to capture so vividly the stultifying lack of social and political alternatives in the age of global capital”. The book received a review-article in Historical Materialism and a full review in the Marx & Philosophy Review of Books, both by writers within the Marxist tradition, and a revised edition appeared in 2022 with new framing material by Fisher’s widow, his friend Alex Niven and his original publisher Tariq Goddard (The Wire, 2017; Reynolds, 2017; LARB, 2017).

Fisher’s imprint work mattered as much as his authorship in the accounts opened for the dossier. Reynolds describes Zero Books as a way of nurturing talent from the blog scene and names Owen Hatherley, Nina Power and Laurie Penny as writers it introduced; he notes that the work continued at Repeater. That history belongs to the wider story of movement media. Gilbert argues that Fisher’s “animating objective” was the building of a new radical public, from CCRU through Dissensus to his later collaborations with the group Plan C, and calls his success in doing so his “greatest and most important achievement”. Reynolds credits k-punk with reviving the polymath spirit of the post-punk music press and says Fisher’s books, journalism and recordings remain a legacy. Posthumous publication has kept the work in circulation: the k-punk collection, Postcapitalist Desire, the 2022 revised Capitalist Realism, and the 2025 edition of the thesis (Reynolds, 2017; LARB, 2017).

The limits of that legacy are part of the record. Rooksby’s review-article argues that Fisher overstated the novelty and distinctness of “capitalist realism” and caricatured contemporary left-wing thinking. Gliniecki argues that events since 2008 — Occupy, the Arab uprisings, Sanders, Corbyn — have told against Fisher’s claim that the end of capitalism has become unimaginable. Both objections are set out in the next section (Rooksby, 2012; Gliniecki, 2018).

Criticisms and controversies#

The most substantial academic critique opened for the dossier is Ed Rooksby’s review-article in Historical Materialism 20(1), published online 1 January 2012, pages 222–231, DOI 10.1163/156920612X632827. Only the abstract and metadata were accessible; the full text is paywalled. Rooksby concedes that the book supplies “valuable conceptual and strategic resources for the Left”, but judges it “hamstrung by several weaknesses — not the least of these a tendency to make unconvincing, sweeping claims about the novelty and distinctness of what Fisher terms ‘capitalist realism’ and a tendency to present a caricature of current left-wing thinking”. The abstract also notes that Fisher attributes the “easier to imagine the end of the world” line to Žižek’s “well-known observation”, which reinforces the point that Fisher built on an existing formulation (Rooksby, 2012).

Steven Sherman’s review in the Marx & Philosophy Review of Books, dated 16 July 2010, was opened in full. Sherman is broadly sympathetic — “on balance, I was impressed by his critique” — but makes two objections that matter. First, Fisher rejects both a “moral” critique of capitalism and the critique of the anti-globalisation protesters, and Sherman finds this unnecessary: “Theorists like Fisher tend to emphasize the bad taste of the ‘food’ of capitalism, but the small size of the portions for most of humanity is by no means therefore irrelevant.” Second, “cultural critiques like Fisher’s would be stronger if they attended a bit more to the global architecture of that world”, including accumulation, militarism and the rise of capitalism elsewhere, “most notably China”. Sherman’s overall judgment is that “the diagnosis is clearer than the prescription”, a criticism several other writers echo (Sherman, 16 July 2010).

The clearest rejection of Fisher’s cultural pessimism from within the Marxist camp is Ben Gliniecki’s article “‘Capitalist Realism’ and the errors of academic Marxism”, published on marxist.com on 10 January 2018 by the Revolutionary Communist International. It is an organisational polemic rather than a peer-reviewed study, and it is identified as such here, but its argument is substantive and directly addresses the claim. Gliniecki contends that Fisher “has seen his 2009 statement that ‘it’s easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism’, decisively disproved by millions of people”, citing the 2011 Occupy and Arab uprisings and later political polarisation. He argues that consciousness develops through experience and struggle rather than snapping into being at a crisis, that Fisher mistook a crisis of overproduction for “the credit crisis of 2008”, and that Fisher displaces class antagonism into individual psychology. For the last claim he quotes the book directly: “Antagonism is not now located externally, in the face-off between class blocs, but internally, in the psychology of the worker, who, as a worker, is interested in old-style class conflict, but, as someone with a pension fund, is also interested in maximizing the yield from his or her investments.” The dossier records that this passage was checked against the primary text and is accurate. Gliniecki concludes that Fisher’s proposed politics amount to “think differently, and then everything will be alright” and offer “nothing but confusion and frustration” to revolutionaries (Gliniecki, 2018; Fisher, 2009).

A criticism from inside Fisher’s own intellectual circle came from Jeremy Gilbert, in the LARB memorial. Gilbert recalls challenging Fisher at a seminar around 2011 for what he called Fisher’s “hippyphobia”, which he argued was “itself a symptom of ‘capitalist realism’ in its rejection of utopianism and in its belief that the ‘failure’ of the counterculture was inevitable”. Gilbert’s point was that the capture of countercultural elements by neoliberalism was a symptom of political defeat, not proof of intrinsic degeneracy. He reports that Fisher “took the points on board fully” and developed the idea of a lost possible future in the introduction to Ghosts of My Life. This is a useful example of a Marxist critic rejecting Fisher’s pessimism while remaining within the same broad tradition and crediting him with responding to the argument (LARB, 2017).

“Exiting the Vampire Castle” was Fisher’s most contested published essay. The openDemocracy text opened for the dossier argues that left-wing social media had produced “an atmosphere of snarky resentment” in which “solidarity is impossible, but guilt and fear are omnipresent”, and ends with a call to “learn, or re-learn, how to build comradeship and solidarity instead of doing capital’s work for it by condemning and abusing each other”. Reynolds notes that Fisher was “an early critic of call-out culture”. No primary source documenting the objections to the essay in detail was opened, so the controversy is recorded here without a sourced account of the opposing arguments (openDemocracy, 24 November 2013; Reynolds, 2017).

Related thinkers#

The clearest theoretical relatives are Fredric Jameson, whose account of postmodernism Fisher adopts and adapts; Slavoj Žižek, who supplied much of the language of ideology, desire and liberal communism in Capitalist Realism; and Alain Badiou, whose distinction between reaction and rivalry Fisher uses for strategy. Behind them stand Marx and Engels, Lukács, Adorno and Horkheimer, Althusser, Brecht and Foucault, all named in the primary text or in Freedman’s assessment. Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari, Nick Land and Sadie Plant belong to the CCRU phase; Jacques Derrida and H. P. Lovecraft to hauntology and the weird. Jeremy Gilbert is both collaborator and critic, and Ed Rooksby, Steven Sherman and Ben Gliniecki are the critics whose objections are recorded above. Carl Freedman and Dan Hassler-Forest are scholars who situate Fisher’s importance. No cross-links are made to Jameson, Žižek, Harvey, Badiou or Gilbert here; the research dossier supplies the points of agreement and dispute for profiles of those thinkers when they are written (Fisher, 2009; LARB, 2017).
