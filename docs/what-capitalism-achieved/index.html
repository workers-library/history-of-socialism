What Capitalism Achieved, and What It Cost

Productivity, machinery, consumption and reform, set beside the labour and environmental record, with an explicit account of what the sources cannot measure.

MarxismPart II: Capitalism, Imperialism and the Social Question

Lenin’s first major work, The Development of Capitalism in Russia of 1899, made a judgment often missing from accounts written by socialists. Capitalism’s “mission,” he wrote, reduced itself to two propositions: “increase in the productive forces of social labour, and the socialisation of that labour.” He paired it with “the full recognition of the negative and dark sides of capitalism” (Lenin, chapter VIII, section VI). That pairing is the right starting point. A history that records capitalist achievements without their costs becomes an advertisement. Productive forces here means the labour, tools, techniques and knowledge available for production; socialisation means that production comes to depend on many people working together rather than isolated households.

The case for what was built#

The classical texts concede the scale of the change. Marx’s 1859 preface laid down that “no social order is ever destroyed before all the productive forces for which it is sufficient have been developed” (1859 Preface). In Capital he described accumulation as a process in which “the more the capitalist has accumulated, the more is he able to accumulate” (Capital, volume I, chapter 24). The Communist Manifesto argued that productive forces eventually become “too powerful for these conditions” of bourgeois property, so that in crises “a great part not only of the existing products, but also of the previously created productive forces, are periodically destroyed” (Manifesto, chapter I). Engels gave the periodicity: “since 1825, when the first general crisis broke out, the whole industrial and commercial world are thrown out of joint about once every 10 years” (Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part III). These writers did not deny the productive achievement. They argued that its form was unstable.

The accomplishment they identified was socialised production. Engels wrote that “the means of production, and production itself, had become in essence socialized” but had been subjected “to a form of appropriation which presupposes the private production of individuals,” a contradiction he called “the germ of the whole of the social antagonisms of today” (Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, Part III). In Anti-Dühring he allowed that state ownership “is not the solution of the conflict, but concealed within it are the technical conditions that form the elements of that solution” (Anti-Dühring). Marx described work as an exchange of matter between people and nature common to every society (Capital, volume I, chapter 7), while Engels warned in an essay of 1876 that “for each such victory nature takes its revenge on us” (Engels, “The Part Played by Labour”).

Some of this can be seen at a smaller scale. Robert Owen showed that a mill could be run without the worst abuses and a school built inside a factory village. In 1816 he opened a school at New Lanark, fixing ten as the age for entering the mill; his son counted about 600 pupils (Owen address, 1816; Robert Dale Owen, 1824). G. D. H. Cole judged that Owen showed “low wages and long hours were not necessary to industrial success,” while arguing that he was a bad leader of men (Cole introduction, 1927). In 1844 weavers and artisans opened a shop in Toad Lane, Rochdale, with a first stock of flour, butter, sugar and oatmeal, gathering twopence at a time (Rochdale Pioneers Museum, archived). A consumer cooperative is a shop owned by those who use it, with surplus returned in proportion to spending. John Kershaw, a founder who had begun in a mill at seven, remembered the trigger: “Distress was far and widespread. People were badly fed and badly clothed” (Kershaw, 1893). Rising consumption, where it came, was often organised by those denied it.

Large infrastructure tells a similar story about who builds. Nkrumah described the Volta River scheme as a dam for hydroelectric power, the smelting of Ghana’s bauxite and the electrification of other industries. The main dam was funded by Ghana, Britain, the United States and the International Bank, with an agreement for a second dam at Bui with the Soviet Union (Nkrumah, Africa Must Unite, 1963). It required states, international finance and political negotiation.

Reforms won rather than granted#

Improvements in pay and hours usually appear in this record as outcomes of conflict. The Lawrence settlement of 1912 gave roughly 30,000 workers increases of between 5 and 20 per cent after a strike against a cut in hours without matching pay (Neill report, 1912). The Clyde Forty Hours strike of January 1919 ended with a 47-hour week (Gallacher, 1916). Five of the six Chartist points eventually became law, including equal electoral districts in 1885 (Chartist chapter). The World Federation of Trade Unions was founded in 1945 by 204 delegates representing 60 million workers, with a constitution aimed at combating “war and the causes of war” (WFTU official history). Each required organisation and was contested. Capitalism adapted by conceding some demands without ceasing to be a system of wage labour and private appropriation.

The costs in the same record#

The environmental costs are documented by witnesses. Engels described the Irk as “a narrow, coal-black, foul-smelling stream, full of debris and refuse” (Condition of the Working Class in England, chapter 4). At Love Canal, Lois Gibbs testified to Congress in 1979 that “thick, black, oily leachate was found running off the north section of the canal onto the street and into the storm sewers” on 9 March (Gibbs, testimony, 1979). Leachate is liquid that has passed through waste and picked up chemicals. At Minamata, industrial mercury poisoning was denied for years; Masami Ogata was refused compensation ten times before certification in 2007 (Ogata, 2013). The Aral Sea’s fishing economy came apart after irrigation diverted its feeder rivers; a fisherman at Muynak remembered the catch of 1957 and said: “I don’t think I will ever fish again” (Kenesbay, quoted by Hinrichsen, 1995). In 1972 The Limits to Growth modelled five interacting trends and warned that “the limits to growth on this planet will be reached sometime within the next one hundred years” if they continued unchanged (Meadows and others, 1972). Its assumptions were criticised; the report put the problem in growth itself, socialist ecology in the social relations of production.

Owen reported children of seven or eight sent to work at six in the morning (Owen, 1817). Engels’s Manchester pages describe housing and immigrant labour as he observed them. Earlier chapters of this part record slavery, racial hierarchy and colonial forced labour as parts of the same expansion. None of these costs can be deducted from the gains with a single figure, and the sources do not try.

What the sources cannot apportion#

Long-run output data now exist against which such questions could in principle be tested, though this chapter has not analysed them. The Maddison Project Database 2023 covers 169 countries up to 2022; the Penn World Table 11.0 covers 185 countries from 1950 to 2023 (Maddison Project Database; Penn World Table). PWT separates expenditure-side real GDP, used to compare living standards, from output-side real GDP, used to compare productive capacity, a distinction that matters when asking what any economic system achieved (PWT variable guide). Version 11.0 also changed its treatment of Chinese statistics, so figures from different vintages cannot be compared without checking the documentation. These are datasets, not findings; no series from them has been computed here.

How much of the improvement came from markets, how much from public infrastructure and how much from organised political pressure? The sources make all three visible but do not weigh them. Marx and Lenin establish the claim that competitive accumulation developed productive forces. The Volta scheme shows state and international finance building what individual firms did not. Strikes, petitions and charter campaigns show workers winning specific concessions. No source here decomposes a rise in output or consumption into those contributions, and none supplies a time series of real wages, productivity or life expectancy against which the question could be tested. The components are documented separately; their relative weight is not.

Limits of the record#

This book has no source for the postwar welfare state, and none for Keynes, Polanyi or Piketty. Those arguments cannot be reported here, still less used to explain what changed in capitalist countries after 1945. The proposed study of the 2008 crisis has no sources in the current book. There are no national income series analysed in this chapter: the Maddison and Penn World Table datasets exist and are cited above, but no series from them has been computed here. There are no consumer price series, industrial injury statistics or measurements of how living standards changed over a working life. The environmental sources are uneven: Love Canal’s accessible voice became a movement spokeswoman, Minamata’s is a museum storyteller, and the Aral testimony is retrospective and translated. Health claims and death tolls remain contested, and this is a URL and text check rather than archival or laboratory work.

Research priorities are long-run wage and price series, public investment records, accident data, and studies separating the effects of markets, state provision and collective bargaining. What can be established here is narrower: capitalism developed productive power on a scale its critics acknowledged; that power was built through wage labour, slavery and colonial extraction; some of its worst effects were reduced by reforms won through organisation; and its environmental record remains unresolved.
