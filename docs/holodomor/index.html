Collectivisation and the Ukrainian famine of 1932–33

On 5 April 1932, Oleksandra Radchenko, a teacher in the Kharkiv region, wrote in her diary: “Famine, artificially created famine is taking on a nightmarish character.” (Oleksandra Radchenko, diary, 5 April 1932; translated by Bohdan Klid. Source). She had recorded village life since February 1931: dekulakisation, cattle seizures, meat levies and peasants tried for having too little grain. She was not a peasant and says her own family was not starving, so she observed the worst rather than endured it, and she wrote privately before the outcome was known. Collectivisation, begun in 1929, replaced individual holdings with kolkhozes, collective farms whose land and livestock were held in common and whose grain deliveries the state fixed.

The arithmetic of a quota#

In September 1932 a collective farmer named Shvets wrote to Hryhorii Petrovsky, head of the All-Ukrainian Central Executive Committee, from the village of Horozhene. He set out his arithmetic: 600 hectares sown, 1,052 people to feed, a quota of 2,400 poods, a Russian weight of roughly sixteen kilograms, and nothing left for food. He asked “has full agreement been reached with our district authorities to leave our village and cooperative hungry for the whole year” (Y. Shvets to Hryhorii Petrovsky, 13 September 1932. Source). A petition is a rhetorical form, and Shvets wrote in loyal language, even comparing the authorities to former landowners. The letter shows what he thought might move Petrovsky, not what became of him. In the German settlement of Komarivka, Adam and Rosina Ketterling wrote to relatives in North Dakota in April 1933 that their brother had starved, that they had eaten no fat for three months, and that they lived on potato peelings, roots and grass. “Many people are dying of hunger. They swell up and then die.” (Adam and Rosina Ketterling, 23 April 1933. Source). The letter reaches us through a newspaper and an edited collection, and its appeal for money shapes what it emphasises.

Kiev writes to Moscow#

On 10 June 1932 Petrovsky told Moscow that after touring Pryluky, Lokhvytsia and other districts he had seen much of the countryside engulfed in famine, with swollen villagers asking why grain had been taken after a good harvest. Vlas Chubar, head of the Ukrainian government, reported hundreds of starving people dying in every village in March and April and children abandoned by their parents. Petrovsky asked for 1.5 to 2 million poods of relief grain and put the villagers’ question: “Why did they create an artificial famine? After all, we had a harvest. Why did they take away the seed grain?” (Hryhorii Petrovsky to Molotov and Stalin, 10 June 1932. Source). Both reported to the men who set the quotas and had reason to shift blame; neither letter appeared in the press. Their use of the word famine matters because the leadership denied it publicly. Stalin rejected the appeals on 11 August: “If we do not correct the situation in Ukraine immediately, we could lose Ukraine.” (Joseph Stalin to Lazar Kaganovich, 11 August 1932. Source). A month earlier Molotov and Kaganovich, sent to the Ukrainian party conference, had cabled that even Politburo members wanted the plan reduced and that they had refused. They advised on the wording instead. “In order not to give fodder to the foreign press, we deem it necessary to expound on this criticism in our press in moderate tones, without printing facts about the situation in the bad regions.” (Molotov and Kaganovich to Stalin, 6 July 1932. Source). This is a decision about publicity, not a full account of what the leadership knew.

The black boards#

The enforcing authority’s own orders document the mechanism. A decree of 7 August 1932 made gleaning a crime punishable by imprisonment or execution. On 18 November the Ukrainian party committee subordinated all collective-farm reserves, including seed and food, to the procurement plan and blacklisted farms accused of sabotage. “Immediate suspension of delivery of goods and of cooperative and state commerce in these villages and the removal of all available goods from cooperative stores” (Resolution of the CC CP(B)U on grain procurement, 18 November 1932. Source). It called for 1,100 brigades by 1 December to extract grain from private farmers. Mendel Khataevich, recalled by Victor Kravchenko years later, told such brigades: “Your job is to get the grain at any price. Pump it out of them, wherever it’s hidden, in ovens, under beds, in cellars or buried away in back yards.” (Mendel Khataevich, speaking in 1932, recalled in Kravchenko’s I Chose Freedom, 1946. Source). Remembered speech is weaker evidence than a signed order. On 22 January 1933 the Soviet leadership acknowledged mass flight and ordered the OGPU, the secret police, to stop it. “to prevent the mass flight of peasants from Ukraine to other regions and entry into Ukraine from the North Caucasus” (Order of the CPC USSR and CC AUCP(B), 22 January 1933. Source). That enemies organised the flight was a regime claim. On 12 March the Ukrainian GPU reported “problems with food supplies were registered in 738 population centers of 139 raions, where 11,067 families are starving”, and recorded people eating straw and the flesh of cats and dogs. (GPU Ukrainian SSR report, 12 March 1933. Source). Police figures count what police registered; the same report called the phenomenon massive, so they are a floor, not a total. Dmytro Zavoloka, an official whose private diary ran from March to June 1932, recorded farmers eating pig feed and a district that met two-thirds of its plan in his entry of 14 March 1932. “And we, the Party, have consciously brought this about with our line, our behavior, our leadership.” (Dmytro Zavoloka, diary, 22 April 1932; translated by Bohdan Klid. Source). By 15 March 1933 Stanislav Kosior was writing to the central committee that “starvation has not yet knocked sense into the heads of a great many collective farmers” (Stanislav Kosior, memorandum, 15 March 1933; translated by Maksym Motorenko and Bohdan Klid. Source).

The witness outside#

In March 1933 Gareth Jones, a Welsh journalist travelling without an escort, walked towards Kharkiv and noted empty bread shops, swollen children and peasants who said they had been without bread for two months. His notebooks were private. On his return he wrote: “I walked alone through villages and twelve collective farms. Everywhere was the cry, ‘There is no bread; we are dying.’” (Gareth Jones, Manchester Guardian, 30 March 1933. Source). Walter Duranty of the New York Times answered the next day. He admitted a severe shortage and that collective farming had made a mess of food production, but wrote: “There is no actual starvation or deaths from starvation, but there is widespread mortality from diseases due to malnutrition.” (Walter Duranty, “Russians Hungry, But Not Starving,” 31 March 1933. Source). Duranty had not gone to Ukraine and wrote under censorship with exceptional access to the Kremlin. Malcolm Muggeridge, who did travel through the countryside, described “On the one side, millions of peasants, starving, often their bodies swollen with lack of food; on the other, soldiers, members of the G.P.U. carrying out the instructions of the dictatorship of the proletariat.” (Malcolm Muggeridge, “The Soviet’s War on the Peasants,” 1933. Source). Six months later Duranty told the British diplomat William Strang, after a ten-day trip, that peasants reaching the towns had died off like flies. “The Ukraine had been bled white.” (Walter Duranty, private conversation recorded by William Strang, 26 September 1933. Source). The gap between the published denial and the private remark is a fact about Duranty, not proof of either figure.

Andrew Cairns, a British agricultural expert, reported in August 1932: “Every station had its crowd—from scores to several hundred, depending on the size of the town—of ragclad hungry peasants, some begging for bread” (Andrew Cairns, report of 3 August 1932. Source). The Italian vice-consul at Novorossiisk described the blackboard system in the North Caucasus; the consul at Kharkiv called the famine a contrived scarcity. Gradenigo’s report also contains an antisemitic claim which is rejected here: a witness can be right about administrative facts and wrong about their cause. Martha Stebalo told Le Matin: “This is an organized famine. The harvest has never been so rich, but we are forbidden to touch it.” (Martha Stebalo, interviewed by Suzanne Bertillon, Le Matin, 29 August 1933; translated by Iryna Fedoriw. Source). Harry Lang wrote in the Jewish Daily Forward: “This famine did not stem from a poor harvest in the fields, but from a harvest of party manoeuvres and a harvest of peasant mistrust of the state.” (Harry Lang, Jewish Daily Forward, 27 December 1933; translated by Moishe Dolman. Source). Lev Kopelev, who had taken part in grain requisitions, asked in a 1980 memoir: “How could all this have happened? Who was guilty of the famine which destroyed millions of lives? How could I have participated in it?” (Lev Kopelev, The Education of a True Believer, 1980; translated by Gary Kern. Source).

No witness can settle the numbers. The Italian consul reported seven million dead, a Ukrainian source quoted eight, Duranty told Strang of four to five million in Ukraine, and émigré accounts often said six. These are reports of what people said, not counts. Demographers using the 1926, 1937 and 1939 censuses and reconstructed vital records put excess deaths, the number above normal mortality, at about 3.9 million in the Ukrainian SSR for 1932 to 1934, with roughly 0.6 million births foregone; published estimates range from 2.6 to 5 million depending on treatment of migration and the 1939 census (Rudnytskyi and others, 2015). On intention the sources conflict: the August letter, the black boards, the flight order and the reversal of Ukrainisation, the 1920s policy of promoting Ukrainian language, point to a political as well as an economic aim, while Davies and Wheatcroft and Kuromiya argue that deliberate mass killing is not demonstrated. Concealment and the scale of death are documented; a premeditated plan to kill millions remains contested. Establishing intent to destroy a group does not require proof that a numerical target for deaths was fixed in advance, and the absence of such a target would not by itself settle the question. What these sources establish is the policy, its effects and the concealment; they do not establish a predetermined plan to kill a specified number.

Limits of the record#

The primary texts are in Russian, Ukrainian, German, Italian and Yiddish, all read here in English translation. No original archive file has been consulted, and the Jones notebooks are known through typed transcriptions. Duranty’s wording was checked against two reprints after the newspaper’s site returned an access error. The surviving testimony favours those who could write, survived or reached a consulate; officials and journalists are overrepresented, and the peasant voice arrives through a translator, an editor or a police report. Much émigré testimony was gathered in the Cold War by organisations with a political purpose, which requires caution rather than dismissal. Gradenigo’s antisemitic framing is rejected outright. No single source yields a defensible death toll, and how many deaths followed each order cannot be recovered from this record.

How historians have read it#

  • Bohdan Klid and Alexander J. Motyl, eds., Holodomor Reader: A Sourcebook on the Famine of 1932–1933 in Ukraine (CIUS Press, 2012). The sourcebook on which this chapter rests; its introductory essays argue for treating the famine as an assault on the Ukrainian peasantry. Source: holodomor.ca
  • R. W. Davies and Stephen G. Wheatcroft, The Years of Hunger: Soviet Agriculture, 1931–1933 (Palgrave Macmillan, 2004). The first English monograph to use Russian and Ukrainian archives extensively; it treats the famine as a policy disaster shaped by false harvest figures and harsh procurements and rejects the claim that Stalin planned mass death. Source: link.springer.com
  • Omelian Rudnytskyi, Nataliia Levchuk, Oleh Wolowyna, Pavlo Shevchuk and Alla Kovbasiuk, “Demography of a man-made human catastrophe” (2015). Reconstructs rural and urban losses from census and vital data, estimating 3.9 million excess deaths and 0.6 million lost births. Source: journals.library.ualberta.ca
  • Hiroaki Kuromiya, “The Soviet Famine of 1932–1933 Reconsidered,” Europe-Asia Studies 60(4), 2008. Reviews the debate between Ellman, Tauger and Davies and Wheatcroft, concluding that the evidence establishes neither an intention to kill millions nor a specifically Ukrainian genocide. Source: ideas.repec.org
  • Robert Conquest, Harvest of Sorrow (1986). The first major English monograph, treating the famine as deliberate terror and setting the terms of the argument later scholars dispute. Source: holodomor.ca
  • Andrea Graziosi, Lettere da Kharkov (Einaudi, 1991), and “The Great Soviet Peasant War” (2009). Edits the Italian consular dispatches and argues that the famine culminated a long Bolshevik war against the peasantry. Source: holodomor.ca

Broad agreement: the famine was man-made, centred on Ukraine and the Kuban, followed collectivisation and requisitioning, and was concealed or minimised in public. Live disagreement: whether the leadership intended mass death, whether the famine should be classified as genocide, and how much weight to give nationality against class and grain supply. These are not equally supported: the demographic figures and the concealment are documented; a premeditated plan to kill millions remains contested.
