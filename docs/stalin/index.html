Stalin: The Complete History and Writings

The full biography of Stalin and a systematic enumeration of every work in the MIA Stalin archive.

On 2 March 1930 Stalin published an article telling party activists that collective farms “must not be established by force”. Seven years later the Soviet secret police issued Operational Order 00447, which sorted suspected enemies into two categories and gave each region a quota: the first category to be shot, the second to be sent to a labour camp for eight to ten years (Dizzy with Success; NKVD Order 00447, 30 July 1937). The distance between those two documents is the problem at the centre of this life. Stalin wrote the theoretical texts that defined Leninism for communist parties across the world, and he presided over a state that used coercion on a mass scale. Neither fact cancels the other. His career ran from a Georgian seminary to the Kremlin, and the record he left includes both arguments about how socialism should be built and the consequences of building it under a one-party dictatorship.

A Georgian seminary and the Bolshevik underground#

The official biographical chronicle attached to the first volume of Stalin’s collected works records that Joseph Vissarionovich Djugashvili was born in Gori, Georgia, in 1879, entered the elementary clerical school there in September 1888, and began at the Tiflis Theological Seminary on 2 September 1894 (Biographical Chronicle). The seminary was one of the few routes by which a boy from a poor Georgian family could get an education under tsarism. It also produced revolutionaries: between 1896 and 1898 the chronicle has him running Marxist study circles, reading Marx’s Capital and The Communist Manifesto, and becoming acquainted with Lenin’s early writings. In January 1898 he organised a workers’ circle in the Tiflis railway workshops. In August he joined Messameh Dassy, a Georgian Social-Democratic group, where he formed with V. Z. Ketskhoveli and A. G. Tsulukidze what the chronicle calls “the core of the revolutionary Marxist minority”. He was expelled from the seminary on 29 May 1899 for propagating Marxism and went to work at the Tiflis Physical Observatory (Ibid.).

The years that followed turned propaganda into organisation. In 1901 he went underground, launched the illegal Georgian Marxist paper Brdzola (The Struggle) and joined the Tiflis committee of the R.S.D.L.P., the Russian Social-Democratic Labour Party. That party split at its Second Congress in 1903 into Mensheviks, who wanted a broad workers’ party, and Bolsheviks, who wanted a small disciplined party of professional revolutionaries; the Tiflis committee followed the Leninist or Bolshevik current. In Batum he set up an underground press and led strikes at the Mantashev and Rothschild works. On 9 March 1902 he led a demonstration of more than six thousand workers. Troops fired, killing fifteen and wounding fifty-four, and that night he wrote a manifesto about the shootings. Arrest followed in April 1902, then imprisonment in Kutais and deportation to Novaya Uda in Siberia, where he arrived in November 1903 (Biographical Chronicle).

He escaped in January 1904, reached Tiflis, wrote the programmatic Credo on party disagreements, and moved to Baku, where he dissolved the Menshevik committee and formed a Bolshevik one. In December 1904 he led a general strike of Baku workers. At the Bolshevik conference at Tammerfors in December 1905 he met Lenin for the first time. His first substantial theoretical work, the series Anarchism or Socialism? of 1906 and 1907, set out a defence of Marxist dialectics and materialism against anarchism and introduced the philosophical vocabulary he used again thirty years later (Volume 1 contents).

The years of tsarist reaction after 1905 were spent in Baku, in repeated arrests and in exile at Solvychegodsk. By 1912 he was in St Petersburg editing Pravda and writing for the workers’ curia in the Duma elections. The intellectual result of this period was Marxism and the National Question (1913), which opened by describing the disillusionment that followed 1905 and insisted that the national question had to be faced rather than evaded (full text). Stephen Kotkin’s biography of Stalin, based on archives and a wide scholarly literature, treats these years as a political apprenticeship rather than the rise of a legend, and argues that Stalin’s suspicion of rivals was rooted in the actual insecurity of a revolutionary party operating under tsarist repression and later in a hostile international environment (Kotkin, Stalin: Paradoxes of Power, 1878-1928).

The national question and the making of a Soviet state#

Marxism and the National Question gave the Soviet movement its standard definition of a nation. Stalin wrote that it was “a historically constituted, stable community of people, formed on the basis of a common language, territory, economic life, and psychological make-up manifested in a common culture”. He added that no single characteristic was enough, and that a nation ceased to be a nation if one of them was missing (Marxism and the National Question, I). The argument was directed against Otto Bauer’s idea of a nation as a community of character: Stalin claimed that this detached the nation from its territory and turned it into “something mystical, intangible and supernatural” (Ibid., I). The same work defended the right of nations to self-determination, including autonomy, federation and complete secession, and insisted that concrete historical conditions, not abstract formulas, had to govern the answer (Ibid., II and III).

In 1917 Stalin wrote a stream of articles for the Bolshevik press in Petrograd, on the soviets, the war, the land question and federalism. He spoke at the Seventh (April) Conference of the R.S.D.L.P.(Bolsheviks) on the national question and supported Lenin’s resolution on the current situation (Seventh (April) Conference). At the Sixth Congress in July and August he delivered the Central Committee report and the report on the political situation, and on 16 October he spoke at the Central Committee meeting that voted for insurrection (16 October meeting; Volume 3 contents). During the Civil War he served on military councils. His telegrams from Tsaritsyn in September 1918 show him intervening directly in the southern front (telegram of 18 September 1918). He also co-signed the investigation into the fall of Perm in December 1918, a piece of collective leadership work often left out of accounts centred on the dictator (report on Perm). Soviet accounts of the Polish-Soviet war in 1920 described it as a defensive struggle against intervention by the Entente, the alliance led by France and Britain (Volume 4 contents).

As Commissar for Nationalities he wrote repeatedly on federal structures and on autonomy for the peoples of the former empire, including Daghestan and the Terek region in 1920 and the theses for the Tenth and Twelfth Party Congresses in 1921 and 1923 (Volume 4 contents). The culmination was the founding of the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics, announced in reports of 26 and 30 December 1922 (Volume 5 contents). In 1936 Stalin described the Union as having been formed “on the principles of equality and the voluntary affiliation of the peoples of the U.S.S.R.” (On the Draft Constitution). Lenin’s last notes on “The Question of Nationalities or ‘Autonomisation’”, dictated on 30 and 31 December 1922, objected to the scheme then taking shape and insisted on the sovereignty of each republic (Lenin, “Autonomisation”). Here “autonomisation” means the practice of folding nominally self-governing republics into a centralised Russian state. The two documents remain in tension: the founding principle and the objection were both written by the men who created the Union.

General Secretary, the “Testament”, and the canonisation of Leninism#

Stalin became General Secretary of the party in 1922, a post concerned with appointments and organisation rather than with doctrine, but one that gave him control over the machinery of the party. Lenin’s “Letter to the Congress” was dictated in sections between 23 and 29 December 1922, with an addition on 4 January 1923 (full text). Its second section warned that Stalin, “having become Secretary-General, has unlimited authority concentrated in his hands”, and that Lenin was “not sure whether he will always be capable of using that authority with sufficient caution”. It called Trotsky “perhaps the most capable man in the present C.C.” but prone to “excessive self-assurance”. The addition of 4 January was blunt:

“Stalin is too rude and this defect, although quite tolerable in our midst and in dealing among us Communists, becomes intolerable in a Secretary-General. That is why I suggest that the comrades think about a way of removing Stalin from that post and appointing another man in his stead who in all other respects differs from Comrade Stalin in having only one advantage, namely, that of being more tolerant, more loyal, more polite and more considerate to the comrades, less capricious, etc.… it is a detail which can assume decisive importance.”

Source: Lenin, Letter to the Congress, addition of 4 January 1923

Khrushchev told the Twentieth Congress in 1956 that the letter was distributed to delegates and that the Thirteenth Congress had discussed the question and “declared themselves in favor of retaining Stalin in this post, hoping that he would heed Vladimir Ilyich’s critical remarks” (Khrushchev, 1956 report). The Chinese party later argued that the criticism breached democratic centralism, the Leninist rule that decisions are made collectively and binding on all members (Chinese 1963 document).

Lenin died in January 1924. Stalin’s memorial speeches at the Second All-Union Congress of Soviets and at the Kremlin Military School cast Lenin as a figure of heroic simplicity and resolution (speech of 26 January; speech of 28 January). The Sverdlov University lectures published in April 1924 as The Foundations of Leninism did the more durable work. They defined Leninism as “Marxism of the era of imperialism and the proletarian revolution”, and more exactly as “the theory and tactics of the proletarian revolution in general, the theory and tactics of the dictatorship of the proletariat in particular” (Concerning Questions of Leninism, 25 January 1926, §I, quoting The Foundations of Leninism). Imperialism here means the competition of powerful states for colonies, markets and raw materials. Stalin identified three contradictions in that system: between labour and capital; among rival financial groups and powers struggling over raw materials and the redivision of the world, which drove imperialist war; and between “the handful of ruling, ‘civilised’ nations” and “the hundreds of millions of the colonial and dependent peoples of the world” (The Foundations of Leninism, ch. I). The centrality of the dictatorship of the proletariat, rather than the peasant question, was restated in 1926: “This is absolutely wrong. The fundamental question of Leninism, its point of departure, is not the peasant question, but the question of the dictatorship of the proletariat” (Concerning Questions of Leninism, §II).

The struggle for the succession#

Between Lenin’s death and the turn to the five-year plans, the party’s leading bodies fought over the meaning of his legacy. The published volumes of Stalin’s works read as a record of that fight from the winner’s side: reports to the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Congresses, the 1926 texts against Zinoviev and the “new opposition”, the 1927 campaign against the Trotskyist opposition, and the 1929 turn against Bukharin, Rykov and the Right (Volumes 6 to 11).

The argument with Trotsky turned on “permanent revolution”, Trotsky’s thesis that the Russian revolution could not survive unless it spread to more advanced capitalist countries. Stalin’s answer was that Lenin had fought the permanent-revolution theory not because he denied that revolutions could be uninterrupted, but because its supporters “under-estimated the role of the peasantry, which is an enormous reserve of the proletariat” (Concerning Questions of Leninism, §III). He added a second charge:

“Hitherto only one aspect of the theory of ‘permanent revolution’ has usually been noted—lack of faith in the revolutionary potentialities of the peasant movement. Now, in fairness, this must be supplemented by another aspect—lack of faith in the strength and capacity of the proletariat in Russia.”

Source: Concerning Questions of Leninism, §III

The counter-thesis of “socialism in one country” held that a workers’ and peasants’ alliance under party leadership could build socialism even while the rest of the world remained capitalist. Stalin quoted Lenin’s 1915 statement that “only an agreement with the peasantry can save the socialist revolution in Russia as long as the revolution in other countries has not taken place” (Ibid., §IV).

The party theory of the time also defined how leadership was supposed to work. Stalin wrote in 1926 that no important question was decided by Soviet or mass organisations “without guiding directives from the Party”, and that the dictatorship of the proletariat was in essence the “dictatorship” of its vanguard, the party (Ibid., §V). The “transmission belts” image, in which unions, soviets, cooperatives and the youth league carry party directives into society, was meant to describe influence; in practice it also described a chain of command.

Opposition did not disappear. Trotsky’s The Revolution Betrayed of 1936 argued that the Soviet Union had undergone a “Soviet Thermidor”, using the term for the stage of a revolution when its radical phase gives way to conservative rule, and that the workers’ state had degenerated, meaning that a party bureaucracy had taken power from the class it claimed to represent (index). Khrushchev’s 1956 report later conceded that the ideological struggle against Trotskyists and others had been real and that “Here Stalin played a positive role”, while distinguishing that struggle from the repressions that followed (Khrushchev, 1956 report). That distinction is the one on which most later accounts, whether sympathetic or hostile, turn.

Industrialisation and the ends of the five-year plans#

Stalin’s writings on economic construction run from the 1926 report on industrialisation and socialist accumulation through the speeches to business executives in 1931 and the report on the results of the First Five-Year Plan in January 1933 (1926 report; 1931 speech; 1933 report). The aim was heavy industry: steel, coal, oil, electric power and machine tools, built at a pace that consumption and housing were made to serve. The 1936 constitution report gave the Soviet case in figures. Socialist industry, it said, exceeded prewar output more than sevenfold, agriculture produced one and a half times as much as before the war, national income had grown fourfold, and the collective farms had 316,000 tractors (On the Draft Constitution). These were numbers presented by Stalin to a Congress of Soviets in a report defending a political record; they are not independent measurements, and the bases for comparison are not set out in the speech.

What the plan meant on the ground is visible in Stephen Kotkin’s study of Magnitogorsk, the steel city built on the Urals steppe from 1929. Working from archives and interviews, Kotkin follows blast-furnace workers and their families through the factory’s labour discipline, the shortages of housing and services, and the gap between the promises of a new civilisation and the conditions of the settlement (Kotkin, Magnetic Mountain: Stalinism as a Civilization). One arrival recorded in the contemporary press was Ibragim Akhmetzyanov, who reached Magnitogorsk in July 1931 in a wooden boxcar with his wife and eight children, to find tents and barracks beside the construction site (Alec Luhn, “The secret history of Magnitogorsk”). From 1935 the Stakhanov movement, named after a coal miner who set a production record, used exemplary workers to raise output norms for everyone (speech to Stakhanovites, 17 November 1935). Kotkin’s argument is that ordinary people were not merely victims of the plan: many took part in it, learned its language and built lives within it, while the coercion and the privileges of the few remained structural features of the system. That interpretation has been disputed by historians who see less genuine consent in the record, but the documentary base, from party archives to workers’ petitions, is real on both sides.

Collectivisation, the kolkhoz, and the famine#

Collectivisation was the forced reorganisation of peasant farming into large collective farms. A kolkhoz was a collective farm in which land and the main implements were owned jointly and members worked under an elected chairman, usually keeping a small private plot. A kulak was a relatively well-off peasant, often with hired labour or more land than neighbours; the Soviet campaign used the word for prosperous villagers and also for anyone who resisted the policy. “De-kulakisation” meant the seizure of property and the arrest or deportation of people so labelled.

The documentary sequence begins with Stalin’s articles on the grain procurements crisis of 1928, continues through the December 1929 theses “Concerning Questions of Agrarian Policy in the U.S.S.R.”, and reaches the January 1930 article “Concerning the Policy of Eliminating the Kulaks as a Class” (1928 article; December 1929; January 1930). Dizzy with Success of 2 March 1930 reported that by 20 February half of Soviet peasant farms had been collectivised, “overfulfilled the five-year plan of collectivisation by more than 100 per cent”, and warned that success had made local activists “dizzy with success” (Dizzy with Success). It then restated the rules that had been broken:

“Collective farms must not be established by force. That would be foolish and reactionary. The collective-farm movement must rest on the active support of the main mass of the peasantry.”

“The art of leadership is a serious matter. One must not lag behind the movement, because to do so is to loose contact with the masses. But neither must one run too far ahead, because to run too far ahead is to loose the masses and to isolate oneself. He who wants to lead a movement and at the same time keep in touch with the vast masses must wage a fight on two fronts—against those who lag behind and against those who run too far ahead.”

Source: Dizzy with Success, 2 March 1930

The article named the agricultural artel, a form of collective farm in which production was shared but households kept small plots, as the main form to be used. Its practical effect was to slow the drive and to shift blame for excesses onto local officials.

Historians using archives opened after 1991 have reconstructed what the official articles omit. Lynne Viola’s study of peasant rebellion, based on secret-police reports, describes 1930 as a wave of unrest: her conservative tally runs to more than thirteen thousand village riots and over two million participants in that year, with more than 1,100 people killed across the wider campaign (Viola, Peasant Rebels Under Stalin). These are counts from police documents, which record what the state noticed and feared, so they are best read as a lower bound rather than a complete total. Sheila Fitzpatrick’s Stalin’s Peasants used letters of complaint and petition sent to the newspaper Krest’ianskaia gazeta, along with police reports, to show the village after collectivisation. Peasants called the kolkhoz a “second serfdom”, resented the loss of their horses as draft animals and transport, blamed Stalin personally for the famine, and resisted by doing as little work on the collective farm as they could (Fitzpatrick, Stalin’s Peasants). Fitzpatrick and Viola disagree about how much open resistance there was during the drive itself; both reject the official picture of willing, happy collective farmers. Moshe Lewin’s earlier study asked why the leadership chose mass collectivisation at all, answering in terms of the grain-supply crisis and the regime’s search for a social base in the village; the social historians shifted the question toward what happened once the policy reached the countryside (Lewin, Russian Peasants and Soviet Power; Ronald Suny on Lewin).

The famine that followed is the central fact of the countryside in 1932 and 1933, and Stalin’s articles and speeches of those years do not name it. Demographers working from the 1926, 1937 and 1939 censuses and reconstructed annual birth and death records estimate that the Ukrainian SSR lost about 3.9 million people to excess deaths in 1932 to 1934, with roughly 0.6 million births foregone, a combined demographic loss of about 4.5 million (Rudnytskyi, Levchuk, Wolowyna and others, 2015). Their method compares recorded deaths and births with hypothetical non-crisis rates interpolated from 1931 and 1935. About 90 per cent of the excess deaths fell in 1933, and about 93 per cent were in rural areas, where the death rate exceeded sixteen per hundred people over the three years. The same team notes that published demographic estimates of excess deaths in Ukraine run from about 2.6 million to 5 million, depending on assumptions about migration and the 1939 census, and that figures of 7 to 10 million are not supported by the statistical series. An older and much cited account by Robert Conquest put the Ukrainian loss near 5 million over a wider 1930 to 1937 window; the difference is mostly one of period and method, not simply of politics (Ibid.). These figures cover the Ukrainian SSR inside its 1937 borders, including the Moldavian ASSR but excluding western Ukraine and Crimea, so they cannot be read as a total for the whole Soviet Union. They also do not establish that deaths were counted by nationality; the researchers caution that the rural population was largely Ukrainian but included other peoples, and that the question of relative losses by nationality needs separate measurement (Wolowyna, “Measuring Mortality in the Holodomor”).

Philosophy and constitution: the claim of achieved socialism#

In September 1938 Stalin published Dialectical and Historical Materialism, which became the standard Soviet statement of the Marxist-Leninist world outlook. It defined dialectical materialism as “the world outlook of the Marxist-Leninist party”, dialectical because it treated nature as connected and changing, materialist because it held that matter exists independently of consciousness (full text). Its best known proposition, quoted from Marx, was that “It is not the consciousness of men that determines their being, but, on the contrary, their social being that determines their consciousness” (Ibid.). It listed five kinds of relations of production known to history: primitive communal, slaveholding, feudal, capitalist and socialist. The book was a simplification of a long philosophical tradition, and its neat sequence was later criticised by historians as a rigid scheme imposed on a more varied Marxian literature. Its political function was clearer than its philosophical originality: it made the party’s doctrine into a single teachable system.

The report on the draft constitution of 25 November 1936 and the constitution adopted on 5 December 1936 were the political counterpart (report; constitution). Stalin drew a distinction between a party programme and a constitution: a programme “speaks of that which does not yet exist”, while a constitution “must speak of that which already exists”, so the draft was “a summary of the gains already achieved” (Ibid.). He listed its features in separate claims. It reflected socialism as he defined it, rested on socialist rather than capitalist principles, proceeded from the absence of antagonistic classes and the power of the working class, was “profoundly internationalistic” on the national question, and offered “consistent and thoroughgoing democratism” without property, sex or national qualifications. It also promised more than formal rights: it “does not confine itself to stating the formal rights of citizens, but stresses the guarantee of these rights”. The report claimed that “the exploitation of man by man has been abolished, eliminated” and that the working class was “an entirely new working class, a working class emancipated from exploitation” (Ibid.). On party competition it was explicit: “In the U.S.S.R. there is ground only for one party, the Communist Party” (Ibid.). The constitutional claim of a classless society and the one-party state were parts of the same document, written in the year before the mass repressions reached their height.

The Great Terror#

Sergei Kirov, the Leningrad party leader, was shot in December 1934. A decree of 1 December accelerated trials and executions of those accused of terrorism, and Khrushchev’s 1956 report argued that the circumstances of the killing remained unexplained (Khrushchev, 1956 report). The years from 1936 to 1938 are known as the Yezhovshchina, named after Nikolai Yezhov, who headed the NKVD, the People’s Commissariat for Internal Affairs, from 1936 to 1938. The word denotes mass arrests, torture to extract confessions, executions and deportation on a scale unknown in earlier Soviet politics. The regime’s public face was the series of show trials of former opposition leaders in Moscow. Its far larger machinery was the secret “mass operations” against people labelled former kulaks, criminals, former members of non-Bolshevik parties and national minorities.

Order 00447 of 30 July 1937 set the machinery in motion. It divided those to be repressed into a first category, to be shot after a three-member tribunal reviewed the case, and a second category, to be imprisoned for eight to ten years, and it assigned quotas to each region (text of Order 00447). Regional officials repeatedly asked for higher quotas, and Stalin approved increases in Politburo resolutions (Werth; the archival record shows collective as well as personal approval). The first wave of the public trials is inseparable from this larger campaign: Khrushchev quoted a telegram of 25 September 1936 in which Stalin and Zhdanov urged that Yezhov be appointed because “The OGPU is four years behind in this matter”, and commented that this pushed the security services toward “mass arrests and executions” (Khrushchev, 1956 report). The OGPU was the earlier Soviet secret police, whose functions passed to the NKVD.

Khrushchev’s report is a primary source with an institutional purpose: it was delivered by a leader of the party that had authorised the repressions, at a congress committed to distinguishing his rule from Stalin’s. Its figures need to be read with that in mind. It said that a party commission, working from NKVD archives, had found that of 139 members and candidates elected to the Central Committee at the Seventeenth Congress in 1934, 98, or 70 per cent, were arrested and shot, mostly in 1937 and 1938; and that 1,108 of 1,966 delegates to that congress were arrested (Ibid.). It also preserved the words of Robert Eikhe, a veteran Bolshevik executed in 1940, who wrote to Stalin: “There is no more bitter misery than to sit in the jail of a government for which I have always fought” (Ibid.). The report’s own case material shows how confessions were obtained: it states that torture was used and that accusations were fabricated.

Historical estimates for the terror as a whole differ from both the larger figures in Cold War writing and the narrower focus of Khrushchev’s speech on party elites. Archival work by Getty, Rittersporn, Zemskov and others put executions in 1937 and 1938 at roughly 680,000, against about 2.5 million arrests, while Nicolas Werth’s synthesis gives about 1.5 million arrests and around 800,000 executions between August 1937 and November 1938, of whom only 40,000 to 50,000 came through the public show trials (Getty, “Victims”; Werth, “Mass Crimes under Stalin”). The differences arise from definitions of period, categories of sentence and the reliability of different NKVD counts. Roy Medvedev’s Let History Judge, written from within Soviet dissent, treated the terror as a deformation of socialism that could be explained and overcome; the archival studies just cited move the emphasis toward the quota system and the local officials who administered it (Medvedev, Let History Judge). The broad shape is not in doubt: most victims were not party leaders but ordinary people selected by quota.

Evgeniia Ginzburg was one of them. A teacher and party activist in Kazan, and the wife of a senior local party official, she was arrested in February 1937, expelled from the party, held in prison for about two years and then sent to Kolyma, the labour-camp system in the far east. Released in 1947, she was arrested again in 1949 and could return to Moscow only in 1955 (Swarthmore reading guide; Harvard collection record). Her memoir, Journey into the Whirlwind, was first published abroad in translation in 1967 and in the Soviet Union only in 1989. It describes the arrest, the interrogations, the transport east and the strategy of surviving through work and reciting poetry to other prisoners. It is a retrospective account, written after release and shaped by the hope of publication, so it establishes the texture of detention rather than a statistical measure. Read beside the party reports and the police records, it shows what a quota meant when it reached a household.

War#

Stalin’s first wartime address was a radio broadcast on 3 July 1941, after Germany had invaded the Soviet Union in June. It opened by addressing listeners as “COMRADES, citizens, brothers and sisters, men of our Army and Navy”, and explained the 1939 non-aggression pact as a way of gaining “peace for a year and a half and the opportunity of preparing our forces” (radio broadcast of 3 July 1941). It called the conflict “not only a war between two armies, it is also a great war of the entire Soviet people against the German-fascist armies”, ordered scorched-earth measures so that the enemy would be left “not a single engine, a single railway car, not a single pound of grain or gallon of fuel”, and called for guerrilla units and for a State Committee of Defence, the GKO, in which “the entire state authority has now been vested” (Ibid.).

The reports to the Moscow Soviet in November 1941, 1942 and 1943 tracked the war as it turned: the failure of the German blitzkrieg, the reasons for the early reverses, the demand for a second front in western Europe, and the “radical turn” of 1943 (6 November 1941; 6 November 1942; 6 November 1943). The archive also holds a stream of Orders of the Day announcing individual offensives and salutes to the formations concerned, along with correspondence with Churchill and Attlee and with Roosevelt and Truman (war speeches index; correspondence, volume 1; volume 2). On 28 May 1943 he explained the dissolution of the Communist International as a wartime measure, a step meant to reassure the Western allies (statement on the Comintern).

The victory speech of 9 May 1945 proclaimed Germany’s unconditional surrender. It credited “the Red Army and the troops of our Allies” and closed with a salute to the dead (victory speech). Its most quoted passage turns on the failure of Hitler’s predictions:

“However, Hitler’s crazy ideas were not fated to come true—the progress of the war scattered them to the winds. In actual fact the direct opposite of the Hitlerites’ ravings has taken place. Germany is utterly defeated. The German troops are surrendering. The Soviet Union is celebrating Victory, although it does not intend either to dismember or to destroy Germany.”

Source: Victory Speech, 9 May 1945

The speech and the accompanying orders of the day are triumphalist texts, written at the peak of Soviet prestige. They document the official language of victory, not the experience of the soldiers and civilians who bore its cost.

The last years and the postwar settlement#

The election speech of 9 February 1946, published as “Origin and Character of the Second World War”, set out Stalin’s account of the war’s causes. He denied that it was “a casual occurrence or the result of mistakes of any particular statesmen” and argued that it was “the inevitable result of the development of world economic and political forces on the basis of modern monopoly capitalism” (speech of 9 February 1946). The same speech described the war as an anti-fascist war of liberation and credited the coalition of the Soviet Union, the United States, Britain and others with defeating the Axis. The wording is the Soviet government’s, and it omits the division of Europe into spheres that the wartime conferences had begun.

In 1947 the Cominform, the Communist Information Bureau, was founded to coordinate the European communist parties; Andrei Zhdanov’s report at its founding meeting at Szklarska Poręba gave the “two camps” doctrine its classic statement, dividing world politics between a Soviet-led camp and an American-led one (Zhdanov’s report at the founding meeting; full text not opened here). Stalin’s archive for these years holds interviews with foreign correspondents, answers to Pravda and telegrams to the parties of Poland, Czechoslovakia, Romania, Hungary, Bulgaria, the GDR, Mongolia, China, Korea and Albania (Volume 16 contents). The polemics with Yugoslavia in 1948 and 1949 are part of this record. The Albanian leader Enver Hoxha later defended Stalin’s reading of the Yugoslav split: “Stalin made a very correct assessment of the danger of the Yugoslav revisionists”, he told a meeting of eighty-one communist and workers’ parties in Moscow in November 1960 (Hoxha, 16 November 1960). That is a party verdict given in an internal dispute, not an independent account of the episode.

Khrushchev’s report named three late cases as fabrications: the Leningrad affair, in which Voznesensky, Kuznetsov, Rodionov, Popkov and others were put to death; the Mingrelian affair, based on an alleged nationalist organisation in Georgia; and the “doctors’ plot”, which he said rested on a letter from the doctor Lydia Timashuk and led to the arrest of eminent medical specialists (Khrushchev, 1956 report). Stalin died on 5 March 1953, before the doctors’ case went to trial. His last major theoretical work, Economic Problems of Socialism in the U.S.S.R. of 1951 and 1952, argued that the basic economic law of socialism was “the securing of the maximum satisfaction of the constantly rising material and cultural requirements of the whole of society through the continuous expansion and perfection of socialist production on the basis of higher techniques” (chapter 7). His final public speech was to the Nineteenth Party Congress in October 1952 (speech of 14 October 1952).

The arguments after his death#

Khrushchev’s report of 24 and 25 February 1956 attacked the “cult of the individual” while defending Stalin’s earlier record. Its opening formulation was that the cult “became at a certain specific stage the source of a whole series of exceedingly serious and grave perversions of Party principles, of Party democracy, of revolutionary legality”. The speech then said that “an entirely sufficient number of books, pamphlets and studies had already been written in his lifetime” about Stalin’s merits, and that “Here Stalin played a positive role” in the fight against political opponents (Khrushchev, 1956 report). The report is the foundational document of de-Stalinisation and also a self-defence: it quotes Khrushchev speaking in Moscow in June 1937 about annihilating enemies “to the last man”, which is what makes the Chinese response’s charge that he shifted all blame onto Stalin harder to dismiss as mere polemic.

The Chinese Communist Party’s “On the Question of Stalin” of 13 September 1963 rejected de-Stalinisation and defended the record. It argued that “A comparison of the two shows that his merits outweighed his faults. He was primarily correct, and his faults were secondary”, and added, “Yes, we do defend Stalin.” It also accepted that “in 1937 and 1938 there occurred the error of enlarging the scope of the suppression of counter-revolutionaries”, while insisting that many of those punished deserved it (Chinese 1963 document). This is a party’s political balance sheet, not a historical finding. Testing it would require defining what counts as a merit and a fault, weighing the documented industrial and military results against the documented deaths, and separating Stalin’s decisions from those of his subordinates. Scholarly opinion divides on how those weights should be assigned; the document offers a verdict, not a method. Hoxha’s 1960 speech reached the same conclusion from a different quarter, and Trotsky’s The Revolution Betrayed had argued from 1936 that the revolution had been usurped by a bureaucracy (Hoxha, 1960; Trotsky, 1936).

A weaker claim in the same tradition deserves separate treatment. Bill Bland argued in 1999 that the cult was built up by Stalin’s political opponents so that they could later blame him for it, relying on a remark Stalin is said to have made to Lion Feuchtwanger in 1936: “with the aim of discrediting him at a later date” (Bland, Stalin: The Myth and the Reality). The claim explains away the entire documented record of official praise, party resolutions and controlled publicity, and rests on a single reported conversation relayed by a committed partisan. It is not corroborated by the material that historians use to study the cult: the press, the archives of the party’s propaganda departments and the testimonies of those who organised it. It should be treated as a political defence, not as an evidentiary alternative to Khrushchev’s commission.

What remained in Marxism-Leninism#

The theoretical legacy is easier to state than the moral one. Stalin’s definition of Leninism tied it to the era of imperialism and placed the dictatorship of the proletariat, not the peasantry, at its centre; his one-country thesis made the worker-peasant alliance under party leadership the route to socialism in a single state; and Dialectical and Historical Materialism supplied a compact account of a material world governed by knowable laws (Concerning Questions of Leninism; Dialectical and Historical Materialism). These were Stalin’s formulations of a tradition that predated him, and later communist parties used them as a common vocabulary rather than as a settled interpretation. The Chinese and Albanian documents of the 1960s treated his writings as “immortal Marxist-Leninist works” and made their defence a test of orthodoxy (Chinese 1963 document). Khrushchev’s report kept the same vocabulary while removing the person from its centre. That split, between a body of doctrine and the record of the ruler who codified it, continued to shape communist parties long after 1953.
