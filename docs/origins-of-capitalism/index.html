Before the Factory: Commerce, Land and the Origins of Capitalism

What the sources already in this book show about merchant wealth, the separation of producers from the land, and the making of a class that sells its labour, and what they leave out.

This book documents socialist movements and governments far more fully than the economic world they answered. Capitalism enters most chapters through its critics or through the institutions built against it. This chapter asks how capital and a class compelled to sell its working capacity came into being, using only evidence already gathered here. Commercial capitalism means trade for profit by merchants who buy and sell rather than produce. Wage labour means work sold by people who do not own the tools, land or factories with which they could work for themselves. Primitive accumulation is Marx’s name for the historical process that created capital’s preconditions by separating producers from the means of production. The sources contain Marx’s account in detail and little independent evidence with which to test it.

Marx was not writing into an empty field. Adam Smith’s An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, in Edwin Cannan’s 1904 edition, is a non-socialist account of how commerce and the division of labour increased productive power, and it criticised merchants who pursued state monopolies (Smith, Cannan edition). Its presence matters here: the socialist critique of capitalism was written against a political economy that could itself attack mercantile privilege. Neither text is a neutral description of industrial life, and neither supplies the tenant records or wage series that would test their claims.

Marx placed the question inside a theory of modes of production, a mode being the historically specific way a society organizes the making and distributing of goods. In 1859 he listed “the Asiatic, ancient, feudal and modern bourgeois modes of production” as “epochs marking progress in the economic development of society” (1859 Preface). The same preface stated the premise later called historical materialism: “It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but their social existence that determines their consciousness” (1859 Preface). That is a claim about how to explain institutions. He warned that “no social order is ever destroyed before all the productive forces for which it is sufficient have been developed,” a qualification often dropped (1859 Preface).

Merchant wealth in the surviving record#

The closest this book comes to a documented case of commercial capitalism is not European. Rodney’s research on the Upper Guinea Coast used Portuguese merchant records in England and Portugal and appeared in 1970 as A History of the Upper Guinea Coast, 1545 to 1800 (MIA Encyclopedia, “Rodney, Walter”). His better-known book of 1972 moved from Africa’s contribution to European capitalist development to the European roots of African underdevelopment up to 1885 and the colonial and neo-colonial systems that perpetuated it (Rodney, preface and contents). Drayton traced the book’s roots to the Caribbean, to Cheddi Jagan’s account of how British Guiana’s plantation and mining economy created wealth abroad and poverty at home, and to Eric Williams’s Capitalism and Slavery (Drayton, 2022). These are described research routes, not the ledgers themselves. Wallerstein observed in 1986 that the book had no footnotes, was not based on archival research and was openly didactic (Wallerstein review, 1986). Araujo later tested one of its supporting claims, that enslaved people were bought with goods of little value (Araujo, 2022). The exchange is a genuine dispute and a reminder that a sweeping account of merchant wealth can be argued over at the level of a single purchase.

Marx’s account and its mechanism#

Marx framed the origin of capital as an apparent circle: accumulation requires surplus value, the excess over wages that a worker produces for the owner of capital, surplus value requires capitalist production, and capitalist production requires capital already to exist. What is needed, he wrote, is “a primitive accumulation, an accumulation not the result of the capitalistic mode of production, but its starting point” (Capital, volume I, chapter 26). He ridiculed the “idyllic” story in which a diligent elite saved while lazy people did not, insisting that “in actual history it is notorious that conquest, enslavement, robbery, murder, briefly force, play the great part” (Capital, volume I, chapter 26). The structural result was “the complete separation of the labourers from all property in the means by which they can realize their labour” (Capital, volume I, chapter 26).

In the final chapter of volume I he summarized the process as “the expropriation of the immediate producers, i.e., the dissolution of private property based on the labour of its owner,” and the transformation of “the pigmy property of the many into the huge property of the few” (Capital, volume I, chapter 32). That sentence carries a distinction worth keeping. Marx wrote that the account applies “in so far as it is not immediate transformation of slaves and serfs into wage labourers, and therefore a mere change of form” (Capital, volume I, chapter 32). He knew that capitalism inherited older forms of unfreedom rather than simply replacing them. The Grundrisse notebooks treat the theme under “Original Accumulation of Capital” and “Forms which precede capitalist production” (MIA analytical contents, Grundrisse). For Engels the political side was the state, “a product of society at a particular stage of development” and “the admission that this society has involved itself in insoluble self-contradiction” (Engels, chapter IX).

The making of a wage-earning class#

A class exists in this framework only when a particular relation of production exists. Engels’s footnote to the Communist Manifesto defines the bourgeoisie as “the class of modern capitalists, owners of the means of social production and employers of wage labour” and the proletariat as “the class of modern wage labourers who, having no means of production of their own, are reduced to selling their labour power in order to live” (Manifesto, chapter I, notes). Capital distinguishes the capacity to work, called labour-power, from the activity of working. The value of that capacity is “the labour-time necessary for the production, and consequently also the reproduction, of this special article,” while its use yields “a source not only of value, but of more value than it has itself” (Capital, volume I, chapter 7). Engels identified the purchase and sale of labour-power as the step that turned money into capital (Engels, Preface to Capital, volume II, 1885).

The one observer here who watched a working class forming at close range was Engels. The son of a Manchester manufacturer, he spent two years in England from 1842 and wrote The Condition of the Working-Class in England in 1844 and 1845 (MIA editorial headnote; SEP, “Friedrich Engels”). He described the towns, housing, Irish immigrant labourers, mines and agricultural workers, drawing on observation and official reports. He was not a worker and wrote as a young middle-class radical with a political purpose, so his pages are evidence of what one observer recorded, not a transcript of workers’ own accounts. Marx denied discovering classes, noting that bourgeois historians and economists had described them before him (Marx to Weydemeyer, 1852). His claim was that classes belong to definite historical phases of production.

How far the account is settled#

The derivation is contested. Derek Sayer points out that in the 1859 preface the key terms are left undefined or defined circularly in terms of one another (Sayer, The Violence of Abstraction). G. A. Cohen reconstructed the theory around the idea that productive forces tend to develop and that production relations are explained by their function in that development; philosophers have disputed the reconstruction and its claim to defend Marx (SEP, “Karl Marx”; SEP, “Analytical Marxism”). Engels himself rejected a crude economic reading. To turn his formula into the claim that “the economic element is the only determining one” was, he wrote in 1890, to make it “a meaningless, abstract, senseless phrase” (Engels to Bloch, 1890). None of this establishes that primitive accumulation did not happen. It concerns what sort of explanation Marx’s account is and how much it can bear.

Limits of the record#

The gravest gap is structural: this book has no dedicated chapter on enclosure, and none of the chapters cited here opens an enclosure deed, poor-law record, estate map, wage series or merchant ledger. Rodney’s Portuguese merchant records are described in a biographical entry, not reproduced. No document here follows a named family off the land and into a mill, and no contemporary opponent of enclosure speaks. There is no way to weigh Marx’s claim about force against a local series of evictions. The general statements come from theory; the concrete observations from 1840s Manchester and twentieth-century anti-colonial writers.

These are research priorities. A fuller account would need enclosure and estate records, regional wage and price series, port and merchant archives, and testimony from people who lost access to land. The proposed studies of the Triangle fire, Blair Mountain, Bhopal, Rana Plaza and the 2008 crisis also have no sources in the current book and remain priorities rather than chapters. What can be said here is narrower: the sources establish what Marx and Engels argued about the origins of capital, how later writers have disputed those arguments, and how little independent evidence this book has assembled about the transition itself.
