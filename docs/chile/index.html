The Chilean Road: Popular Unity, the Coup and the Voices It Left

On the morning after the vote of 4 September 1970, Salvador Allende stepped onto the balcony of the University of Chile Students' Federation building in Santiago.

On the morning after the vote of 4 September 1970, Salvador Allende stepped onto the balcony of the University of Chile Students’ Federation building in Santiago. Popular Unity, the coalition that had won, had campaigned under a song whose chorus ran:

“Venceremos, venceremos, con Allende en septiembre a vencer.” — Popular Unity anthem “Venceremos”, quoted by Memoria Chilena, National Library of Chile, “El gobierno de la Unidad Popular (1970–1973)” (Source: memoriachilena.gob.cl).

Allende promised to respect the rights of all Chileans and not to trade away the programme. The song was the sung form of a written platform agreed the previous December:

“La única alternativa verdaderamente popular […] es terminar con el dominio de los imperialistas, de los monopolios, de la oligarquía terrateniente.” — Popular Unity Basic Programme, 17 December 1969 (ellipsis marks my omission). Marxists Internet Archive: diciembre17

The Basic Programme of 17 December 1969 named the industries to be taken into state hands, copper, iron, nitrates, banking, foreign trade and strategic industry; promised agrarian reform and a “social area” of state ownership with worker representation on public-sector boards; and pledged to preserve democratic freedoms and not use the armed forces against the people. That last claim was tested within three years.

A road built in law#

Allende’s transition preserved legality while transferring ownership. In a message to Congress on 21 May 1971 he set out a second, democratic model of revolution:

“Vamos al socialismo por el rechazo voluntario, a través del voto popular, del sistema capitalista y dependiente.” — Salvador Allende, speech to Congress, 21 May 1971. Marxists Internet Archive: 21 5 71

There would be a plurality of parties and institutions preserved while ownership changed, with a socialist constitution later. He argued that a social revolution takes years, not weeks. His confidence that the armed forces would defend the constitutional order was an assumption, not a finding.

The Statute of Democratic Guarantees, Law 17.398, promulgated on 30 December 1970 with Justice Minister Lisandro Cruz, protected organisation, expression, assembly, teaching and the press, banned prior censorship and extended social rights. It defined the armed forces and the Carabineros, the police force, as professional, hierarchical, disciplined, obedient and non-deliberative bodies. Its existence does not prove enforcement, but it fixes the settlement the coup destroyed.

Copper nationalisation was the central economic act; Allende maintained it was lawful and approved unanimously by Congress. The most concrete content given to “workers’ control” came in a draft law of 15 July 1972 on a National System of Self-Management, whose first article stated its purpose was:

“cuyo objetivo será acentuar el proceso de control de la economía por los trabajadores” — Draft law on the National System of Self-Management, Article 1, 15 July 1972. Marxists Internet Archive: julio15

Capital in a self-managed firm belonged to the state development corporation, management to the workforce. An Assembly of Workers, one vote per permanent worker and no proxies, would elect a five-member Management Council to appoint the manager. The state fixed plans, pay and a compulsory levy on surplus, and workers could not divide the capital. More precise than the slogan, it was a draft, not an enacted law, and no minutes of an actual assembly survive. The local industrial bodies known as CORD were the intended channel, but the record holds no account from a factory council.

Blockade and rehearsal#

The government’s case was that it was being strangled from outside. In an open letter to President Nixon on 20 August 1971, Allende listed the withdrawal of a scientific mission from Easter Island, cancellation of the warship Enterprise’s visit, opposition to Santiago hosting the third UN Conference on Trade and Development, and suspension of Export-Import Bank credits after copper nationalisation:

“La dura realidad de nuestra tierra, el hambre, la ignorancia, la miseria, la casi total desesperanza han convencido a nuestra gente de que necesitamos cambios profundos para superarlas.” — Salvador Allende, open letter to Richard Nixon, 20 August 1971. Marxists Internet Archive: agosto20

He insisted Chile had the sovereign right to set compensation terms and had submitted the question to its courts. The letter is advocacy, establishing what he alleged rather than proving intent. At the United Nations on 4 December 1972 he denied that foreign mining companies had been confiscated:

“No hemos confiscado las empresas extranjeras de la minería del cobre.” — Salvador Allende, UN General Assembly, 4 December 1972. Marxists Internet Archive: diciembre04

He cited profit rates up to 205 per cent in a Kennecott subsidiary and accused the International Telephone and Telegraph Company of plotting to prevent him taking office, saying he held an eighteen-point plan dated October 1971; the documents are not reproduced and the figures are his government’s. Historians still divide between external warfare and the government’s own deficits and wage settlements; these sources support both without settling their weight.

On 29 June 1973 a military rising failed. Allende’s speech from La Moneda the next day is a detailed participant account: the 8:55 call reporting tanks outside the palace, more than 500 bullet marks on its walls, General Prats confronting rebel tank crews, and his decision to consider a plebiscite rather than close Congress:

“No voy a cerrar el Congreso, porque sería absurdo.” — Salvador Allende, La Moneda, 29 June 1973. Marxists Internet Archive: junio29

Members of the extreme-right Fatherland and Freedom front later admitted taking part; a newspaper reported about 22 dead, including the cameraman Leonardo Henrichsen, who filmed the fighting before he was shot.

Eleven September#

On 11 September 1973 Allende broadcast from La Moneda at 7:55, 8:15, 8:45, 9:03 and about 9:10. He reported a naval uprising in Valparaíso, told workers to hold their workplaces but avoid provocations, and refused exile and resignation. In his last broadcast he thanked women, young people and workers, attacked foreign capital and the “reaction” for breaking the armed forces’ tradition, and closed with the image of the great avenues:

“No voy a renunciar!” and “La historia es nuestra y la hacen los pueblos.” — Salvador Allende, final radio broadcasts, 11 September 1973. Marxists Internet Archive: 11 09 73

The transcript has inaudible markers; the recording was catalogued but not listened to, so the spoken wording is not verified by ear.

The junta’s first communiqué alleged a grave social and moral crisis, chaos the government could not control and paramilitary groups backed by Popular Unity. Signed by Pinochet, Merino, Leigh and Mendoza, it demanded the president’s immediate resignation:

“El Presidente de la República debe proceder a la inmediata entrega de su cargo a las Fuerzas Armadas y Carabineros de Chile.” — First Junta communiqué, 11 September 1973. Source: memoriachilena.gob.cl

It promised workers’ gains would undergo no fundamental change, threatened air and ground punishment against pro-government media and ordered residents indoors. The two texts contradict each other on nearly every point of legitimacy, and the contradiction itself is the evidence.

Boris Navia, a detainee who said he shared a cell with Víctor Jara, described seeing an officer strike the singer at the Chile Stadium, receiving two sheets of paper on 15 September and hiding the manuscript in his shoe. He was tortured after a copied page was found but did not give up the original. The poem he carried out begins:

“Canto, qué mal me sales / cuando tengo que cantar espanto. / Espanto como el que vivo, / como el que muero, espanto.” — “Estadio Chile,” text reproduced with Boris Navia’s testimony; attribution and variants disputed in the source. Source: magalico.com

Navia’s account was collected decades later, his date for Jara’s death conflicts with the judicial finding of 16 September reported on the same page, and the first published version differs from Joan Jara’s 1983 edition over a final verse and a line about the soldiers’ faces.

From inside the intelligence apparatus that outlived the coup came the confession of Michael Townley, an American-born operative of Pinochet’s DINA, written in 1978 in fear for his life:

“It is obvious that they intend to use the fact that I am a foreigner, to attempt to divert the responsibility of the Chilean government.” — Michael Townley, 13 March 1978; English rendering by the National Security Archive of the Spanish original. Source: nsarchive.gwu.edu

Townley said he acted on orders from DINA director Manuel Contreras and described the 1976 car bombing of Orlando Letelier in Washington. The English is the Archive’s translation of a garbled machine OCR text, and the confession concerns the aftermath, not the coup. The truth commissions that followed had to reconstruct what happened from such fragments. Rettig documented thousands of cases of disappearance, killing, torture and kidnapping, with torture outside death cases outside its mandate; the later Valech report extended the record, but its individual statements are reported to be access-restricted.

How historians have read it#

  • José del Pozo, Rebeldes, reformistas y revolucionarios: una historia oral de la izquierda chilena en la época de la Unidad Popular (1992). Oral history of the Chilean left, based on retrospective interviews with participants; valuable for how actors remembered their choices and to be checked against contemporary documents. Source: memoriachilena.gob.cl
  • Mark Ensalaco, “Truth Commissions for Chile and El Salvador: A Report and Assessment,” Human Rights Quarterly 16(4) (1994). Compares the two commissions’ mandates and methods; useful for judging what the Rettig process could and could not establish.
  • David Weissbrodt and Paul Fraser, “Report of the Chilean National Commission on Truth and Reconciliation,” Human Rights Quarterly 14(4) (1992). A contemporaneous legal assessment of the Rettig report, strong on evidentiary standards and reparations.
  • Brian Loveman and Elizabeth Lira, Las suaves cenizas del olvido: vía chilena de reconciliación política (1999). Studies Chile’s longer history of reconciliation and its limits; agrees that truth-telling without institutional change had narrow effects. Source: bibliotecanacionaldigital.gob.cl
  • Stephen Tapscott, ed., Twentieth-Century Latin American Poetry: A Bilingual Anthology (University of Texas Press, 1996). Contains the poem attributed to Jara with an English translation; use it to check the translation, not as authority for the poem’s disputed wording. Internet Archive: twentiethcentury00step
  • United States Institute of Peace, “Truth Commission: Chile 90” (institutional reference). Summarises the Rettig Commission’s charter and findings; its archived page states that the commission documented 3,428 cases and that torture outside death cases fell outside its mandate. Internet Archive snapshot: truth commission chile 90

Broad agreement exists on the major facts: Allende won a free election, the coup was carried out by the armed forces, and systematic repression followed. Interpretation divides on the causes, between external economic warfare and US involvement on one side and the government’s own economic choices and collapsing parliamentary coalition on the other. The two are not mutually exclusive, and the sources here support elements of both. The Rettig and Valech reports are authoritative for documented cases, not for every claim made in their name, and disputed details such as the last verse of “Estadio Chile” should be presented as disputes.

Limits of the record#

The record is top-heavy. Allende’s speeches and drafts dominate because they were collected and digitised; workers, peasants, soldiers and detainees reach us through a handful of later publications. No first-hand account of an actual CORD factory council or of a rank-and-file participant in the September events appears here, a gap in the record rather than proof that no such material exists. The archive is largely in Spanish, the Rettig and Valech reports could not be rendered into readable text, and the National Library’s books are access-restricted. The Valech testimony is reported to be sealed for decades, but the governing decree was not checked; the ITT and Kennecott documents Allende said he held were not opened, and the Townley OCR is partly corrupt.

Allende’s speeches are advocacy, the junta’s communiqué self-justification, Navia’s account collected decades later and at odds with the judicial date of Jara’s death, Townley’s confession self-protective. None is neutral description. The record leaves open the sincerity of the junta’s promise to workers and whether the plebiscite strategy could have worked; his speeches show only that he proposed it. A writer with physical access should consult the National Library and Archivo Nacional in Santiago, the Víctor Jara Foundation’s archive and the declassified US records before claiming numbers of victims or describing what workers in the factories actually did.
