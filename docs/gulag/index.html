The Archipelago: Forced Labour and the Camps

The promise and the petition#

On 14 December 1926 three prisoners returning ill from the camp on the Solovetsky Islands, in the far north, wrote to the Soviet Central Executive Committee. G. Zheleznov, Vinogradov and F. Belinskii described sixteen-hour days, inadequate food and clothing, and punishment handed out to anyone who complained. “People die like flies, i.e., they die a slow and painful death,” they wrote (letter from Solovki, 14 December 1926). The letter was a plea for intervention, and its writers had a reason to stress their suffering; the Library of Congress translation notes that the Russian grammar was corrected for clarity. It is a contemporary document nevertheless, sent a decade before the great purges. The Gulag was the system of forced-labour camps and exile run by the Soviet secret police, its name an acronym from the Russian for the main camp administration. A decade after the Solovki letter the state published a constitution whose Article 127 read: “Citizens of the U.S.S.R. are guaranteed inviolability of the person. No person may be placed under arrest except by decision of a court or with the sanction of a procurator.” (Constitution of the USSR, 5 December 1936). A procurator was the official who sanctioned arrests and supervised the law. The two documents sit easily side by side. One describes arbitrary power at a camp; the other promises that no one can be arrested without legal authority. A constitution records what a state promises, not what its agencies do.

The island, the canal and the work#

Solovki, the monastery island in the White Sea, had become a camp in the 1920s. The White Sea–Baltic canal, dug in the early 1930s, was presented in Soviet propaganda as proof that work could “reforge” prisoners, remaking them into useful members of society. A photograph from 1932 in the collection of Memorial, the Moscow human-rights archive, shows women prisoners at the canal works with shovels and wheelbarrows (Belomorkanal construction photograph, 1932). The catalogue names no photographer and cannot establish how many worked there or whether the scene was arranged. A 1933 camp newspaper drove the lesson home to prisoners in other camps: “Camp inmates of Karlag! Learn from the heroe workers of the White Sea Canal construction project how to fight and win.” (Putevka, no. 25 (53), 1933). The spelling “heroe” is the catalogue’s. The canal was shallow and narrow, of little later use, but the story of reforging travelled.

Inside the camps, work and food were joined. A norm was the quota a prisoner had to meet, and failure cut the ration. Evgeniia Ginzburg, a party official arrested in 1937 and sent to Kolyma in the far east, recalled: “We no longer had the strength to fulfill our output norms. Our rations were steadily reduced.” (Ginzburg, “Days and Lives” exhibition). Lev Razgon, who spent years in the camps and later set norms himself, a privileged job known as being a trusty, was blunt: “In fact it was impossible to survive if you were felling timber.” (Razgon, “Days and Lives” exhibition). A brigade was the gang of prisoners who worked together, and survival often depended on the job a prisoner held. Varlam Shalamov, who spent sixteen years in Kolyma, wrote after his release about cutting a road across virgin snow; the people who would use it were not the people who made it: “The people on the tractors and horses, however, will be not writers but readers.” (Shalamov, “Through the Snow,” 1956). In “Rain” he describes standing in prospecting shafts in cold rain: “We couldn’t climb out of the shafts, or we would have been shot.” (Shalamov, “Rain,” Rayfield translation, 2018). These are stories, fiction shaped from what he witnessed, not depositions; the publisher says everything in them was experienced or seen by Shalamov.

Hunger and the body#

Bread was measured in grams. Evfrosiniia Kersnovskaia, deported as a landowner in 1941, remembered the ration falling sharply: “Instead of 350 grams we got barely 120-150.” (Kersnovskaia, “Days and Lives” exhibition). She recalled washing bloodstained army clothing without soap and being beaten by common criminals in transit. Camp authorities made the link between output and survival explicit. One photograph in the Memorial collection shows a mock graveyard whose tombstones carry surnames and percentages, marking prisoners said to have died for failing their norms (camp propaganda display, undated). It is a teaching device, not proof that the named people died. The official record could be worse than the propaganda. Bamlag, the camp building the Baikal–Amur railway, was inspected by its procurator Dimakov, whose 1938 report is quoted by the historian Mikhail Nakonechnyi: “In the infirmary, there are prisoners lying naked on long bunks, literally packed like sardines in a barrel.” (Dimakov report, 1938, quoted 2021). The same report lists lice, infected wounds, mixed cases of tuberculosis and syphilis, and prisoners brought off trains already frozen. A procurator wrote for superiors and may have been pressing for resources or assigning blame; the figures come through the historian. The material record also survives. Two 1933 Bamlag photographs show women shovelling dirt into railway flatcars and prisoners eating lunch (Bamlag photographs, 1933; second image). They show what stood before the lens, not how the work felt.

Words and silence#

The camp produced its own literature, and the question is how to read it. Shalamov set down aphorisms after his release: “The extraordinary fragility of human nature, of civilization. A human being would turn into a beast after three weeks of hard work, cold, starvation and beatings.” (Shalamov, “What I Saw and Learned in the Kolyma Camps,” 1961). He refused, he said, the foreman’s job that would have sent others to their deaths, and he argued that solidarity did not survive where life itself was at stake. His “Duck” ends: “a duck dies on a patch of water, a man in a barrack.” (Shalamov, “Duck,” 1963). Kersnovskaia and Ginzburg describe women keeping each other going, and Kopelev remembers a brigade of thieves meeting its quota through one man’s effort; these are different sites and years, and not necessarily contradictions. Lev Kopelev, a young Communist expelled for speaking against the army’s treatment of German civilians, explained why: “I had been expelled from the Party for ‘gross political errors, for showing pity for the Germans, for bourgeois humanism, and for harmful statements on questions of current policy.’” (Kopelev, “Days and Lives” exhibition). Sergei Sedov, an engineer banished to Krasnoyarsk in 1935, wrote home from deportation jail: “My situation is still the same and I know nothing about my future.” (Sedov letter, 17 August 1935). Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, who compiled The Gulag Archipelago from letters and testimony that the official record excluded, called his work a literary investigation. Its best-known line runs: “the line separating good and evil passes not through states, nor between classes, nor between political parties either—but right through every human heart” (Solzhenitsyn, The Gulag Archipelago, Part 4, Chapter 1). Each of these is a shaped account: a story, a memoir written for readers, a petition, a letter, a compilation. None is a transparent record, and none lets us speak for the people who left nothing at all.

Limits of the record#

The record is uneven. Almost every first-person account available in English comes from a survivor who was literate, often a party member or from the intelligentsia, and who wrote for publication. Kopelev, Ginzburg, Razgon and Shalamov all look back on events from many years later; memory, later politics and the expectations of publishers shape what they say, and Shalamov’s stories are explicitly literary. The 1926 letter was translated and edited, and the exhibition extracts carry no page numbers and name no translator. The procurator’s report and Sedov’s letters reach us through secondary authors. The documentary material is no less partial. The Memorial photographs have no photographers’ names and only brief catalogue entries; the Karlag newspaper survives as a scan with a catalogue description whose spelling may be wrong; the constitution on the Marxists Internet Archive is a later reprint with amendments added after 1936. The full text of The Gulag Archipelago and the original 1934 Belomor volume lie behind the secondary accounts used here, and nothing beyond those accounts is claimed for them. Checking the Dimakov figures, the Bamlag death count and the camp newspapers would require Russian-language archives in Moscow and a physical visit. The guards appear only through an abstract and an official newspaper; the wartime camps, the German occupation, the “special settlements” of deported families and the deportation of whole nationalities lie almost outside this record, and no first-person account from a Bamlag prisoner in English was found. The evidence can establish texture, institutions and documented contradictions. It cannot supply a camp-by-camp death rate, reconstruct an unrecorded conversation, or recover a private thought.

How historians have read it#

  • Anne Applebaum, Gulag: A History (2003). A narrative synthesis built from archives, interviews and published memoirs, covering the camps from Lenin to Gorbachev and describing daily life alongside the system’s political role. It broadly agrees with the testimony about hunger, work and survival strategies, and treats memoirs as evidence to be checked rather than rejected. Source: anneapplebaum.com
  • Nicolas Werth, “Mass Crimes under Stalin (1930–1953)” (2008). An archival chronology of deportations, mass operations and camp mortality that distinguishes secret repression operations from the public show trials and rejects the view that denunciation from below drove the terror. More insistent than Applebaum on central planning. Source: sciencespo.fr
  • Cynthia Ann Ruder, Making History for Stalin: The Story of the Belomor Canal (1998). A literary-historical study of the 1934 volume celebrating the White Sea–Baltic canal; the natural companion when asking how the canal was turned into a story. Internet Archive: makinghistoryfor0000rude
  • Jehanne M. Gheith and Katherine Jolluck, Gulag Voices: Oral Histories of Soviet Incarceration and Exile (2010). Interviews with men and women across the former USSR, arguing for the range of Gulag experience and the specific problems of oral history and memory in post-Soviet Russia. Source: history.stanford.edu
  • Maya Vinokour, “2+2=5: On the White Sea-Baltic Canal and Totalitarian Pipe Dreams” (2018). A comparative essay that reads the 1934 canal volume as propaganda and extends the argument to post-Soviet memory and contemporary penal politics. Source: lareviewofbooks.org
  • Alun Thomas, “On Guard at BAMlag: Representations of Guards in the 1930s Gulag Press” (2014). Based on newspapers printed for Bamlag guards, arguing that the guard press depicted disciplined soldiers while the prisoner press used a more rehabilitative language; it adds the guard side that memoir evidence largely omits. Source: eprints.staffs.ac.uk

Historians broadly agree that the camp system was large, coercive and lethal, and that its official self-description was propaganda. They disagree about how much to attribute to central planning as against local initiative, how to read economic gains built on forced labour, and how far the testimony of survivors can be generalised to the whole camp population.
