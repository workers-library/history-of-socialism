Amílcar Cabral and Walter Rodney

The Weapon of Theory, national liberation and culture, and How Europe Underdeveloped Africa.

Amílcar Cabral and Walter Rodney both wrote about revolution while taking part in one. Cabral led the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde, known as the PAIGC, through a war against Portuguese rule in Guinea-Bissau and died months before victory. Rodney was a Guyanese historian whose How Europe Underdeveloped Africa (1972) became a standard reference for a generation of scholars, and who then returned home to organise workers against an authoritarian government (MIA Encyclopedia, “Rodney, Walter”; How Europe Underdeveloped Africa). Neither treated ideas as a separate activity from politics. Cabral argued that a revolution could not succeed without a theory of its own society. Rodney argued that the history of Africa had to be written as a weapon in a present struggle. Their lives ended violently within eight years of each other (MIA Encyclopedia, “Cabral, Amilcar”; “Rodney, Walter”), and the movements they served preserved their words in the institutions they had helped to build.

The two men are usually placed in the same broad current of anti-colonial Marxism, but the connection between them is narrower and easier to document than that label suggests. Rodney read Cabral, adopted some of his categories and cited him in public. There is no evidence in the surviving documents that Cabral read Rodney. What they shared was a problem: how to make a revolution in societies where the class that European Marxism treated as the agent of change was barely formed, and where the state inherited at independence could be turned against the people who had fought for it. Their answers converged on culture, on the educated middle group that inherited colonial power, and on the need for intellectuals to become answerable to ordinary working people.

Cabral’s formation and the founding of the PAIGC#

Amílcar Cabral was born on 12 September 1924 at Bafatá in Portuguese Guinea, the son of an employee of the colonial administration. He was educated at the lyceum on São Vicente and entered the Lisbon Higher Institute of Agronomy in 1945, qualifying as an engineer-agronomist in 1952. As a student he took part in Portugal’s democratic opposition. He returned to Guinea in 1953 to work at the Pessuba experimental station and was dismissed in 1955 for anti-colonial activity and expelled from the territory. That year and the next he worked as an agronomist on sugar plantations in Angola and joined Agostinho Neto and others in building the Movement for the Liberation of Angola, better known by its Portuguese initials, MPLA. His training as an agronomist mattered later. Cabral surveyed crops and soils across Guinea, and the habit of direct observation shaped the way he analysed its society (MIA-hosted movement biography, Fighters for National Liberation, Progress Publishers, 1984).

On 19 September 1956 he founded the African Party for the Independence and Union of the Peoples of Guinea and Cape Verde, renamed in 1960 the African Party for the Independence of Guinea and Cape Verde, or PAIGC. In Lisbon in 1957 he helped establish the Anti-Colonial Movement, described in the movement biography as the first illegal organisation of Africans in Portugal to make the struggle against colonialism its objective. From the middle of 1960 he directed the party’s bureau in Conakry, and in April 1961 he became deputy general secretary of the Conference of National Liberation Organisations of the Portuguese Colonies. The party began armed operations in 1963 and within two years held liberated zones in which it effectively governed. Cabral argued in 1971 for a National People’s Assembly, which was created in 1972 on the basis of popular vote in the liberated territories (MIA Encyclopedia of Marxism, “Cabral, Amilcar (1924 to 1973)”).

Cabral was careful to say that his movement had not copied a foreign model. Asked in New York in October 1972 how the struggle had been prepared, he replied that when the party began, its members had not known the writings of Mao Zedong. He first encountered a book by Mao in 1960, four years after the party was created. Some foreign experiences were set aside because the differences were too great to make study useful. “Because what you have to do in your country you have to create yourself,” he told his audience (Cabral, “Connecting the Struggles,” informal talk, October 1972, in Return to the Source). In his address to the United Nations Fourth Committee in October of that year, he said that the patriotic forces had launched a general struggle in January 1963 in response to what he called the colonial genocidal war waged against the people by the government of Portugal. This was a claim made by a party leader in wartime to an international forum, and it should be read as such (Return to the Source, “United Nations Address”).

His earlier appearance before the United Nations Special Committee on Territories under Portuguese Administration, in 1962, had argued from law and from daily experience at once. He told the committee that the constitutional and legal status of Guinea was not that of a province of Portugal but of a non-self-governing country conquered and occupied by force. Its people had no political rights, did not elect representatives, could not remove the officials who governed them and did not enjoy the most basic freedoms (Cabral, At the United Nations (1962)). Ten years later the tone had changed. He told the Fourth Committee that the PAIGC had come not to seek propaganda victories or condemnations of Portugal but to work for a constructive solution. His party and people had outgrown indecision and promises and had chosen definitive action (Return to the Source).

The weapon of theory#

The address Cabral gave to the first Tricontinental Conference in Havana in January 1966 is the central text of his political thought. Its title states its argument. Theory was not an ornament added to armed struggle but one of the weapons of liberation. He opened by saluting the Cuban Revolution and turned at once to what he called the struggle against “our own weaknesses,” by which he meant the conflicts within the economic, social and cultural reality of each country. A national or social revolution that ignored that reality, he warned, ran a grave risk of failure (Cabral, “The Weapon of Theory” (1966)).

Much of the speech is an argument against imported formulas. He rejected the view that a revolution could be exported like a commodity, insisting that national liberation was formed by the historical reality of each people. This was a rejection of the idea later called foco theory, from the Spanish and Portuguese word for focus, which held that a small armed band could generate a revolution wherever it established itself. For Cabral the armed group had to grow out of a particular social structure. He named what he considered the greatest weakness of the liberation movements: the absence of an adequate ideology, which he traced to ignorance of the historical reality those movements claimed to transform. He did not mean that fighters should read more books in the abstract. His justification for theory linked practice and knowledge in a single sentence. “Every practice produces a theory,” he said, “and if it is true that a revolution can fail even though it be based on perfectly conceived theories, nobody has yet made a successful revolution without a revolutionary theory” (Ibid.).

He made the same point with a proverb from his own country. When a house is burning, there is no use beating the tom-toms. Insults against imperialism would not remove it; taking up arms would. He distinguished carefully between the violence of the oppressor and liberating violence. The struggle did not end when a flag was raised and an anthem played, and national liberation could not be won without force answering the criminal force of the colonial power (Ibid.).

Patrick Chabal, who wrote a reassessment of Cabral’s political thought in the Journal of Modern African Studies in 1981, argued that Cabral was primarily a man of action whose writings were analyses of the situations in which he was involved rather than abstract theories. He did not call himself a political theorist, and most of his texts were party documents written for a particular audience and purpose (Patrick Chabal, “The Social and Political Thought of Amílcar Cabral: a Reassessment,” Journal of Modern African Studies 19, no. 1, March 1981, pp. 31 to 56). That judgement is a useful warning against turning Cabral’s occasional speeches into a sealed system. Carlos Cardoso’s later survey of Cabral scholarship reaches a similar conclusion by a different route. He notes that scholars disagree over whether Cabral was a Marxist in any orthodox sense. Carlos Lopes found that Cabral used Marxist language without producing Marxist orthodoxy, while Chabal stressed the occasional and practical character of the writing. Reiland Rabaka reads him instead as a theorist within an African radical tradition. Cardoso argues that the way forward is to examine the concepts Cabral actually used rather than to multiply quotations (Carlos Cardoso, “A meta-analysis of Amílcar Cabral’s thought,” Cadernos de Estudos Africanos 48, 2024, pp. 83 to 103). The disagreement among these historians is a reason to read the 1966 speech as an intervention in a war, not as a treatise.

Class, productive forces and the peasantry#

Cabral accepted the Marxist claim that class struggle drives history, but he insisted that this had to be understood in its historical setting. He wrote that the socio-economic phenomenon of class “is created and develops as a function of at least two essential and interdependent variables,” the level of productive forces and the pattern of ownership of the means of production. By productive forces he meant the tools, skills and labour a society uses to produce what it needs. He rejected the idea that societies which had not yet formed classes were outside history. It was wrong to suppose, he wrote, that peoples such as the Balantes of Guinea, the Coaniamas of Angola or the Macondes of Mozambique had no history because they lacked the social forms Europe recognised (Cabral, “The Weapon of Theory” (1966)).

His positive claim was that class struggle was the motive force of history only in a particular period. Before and after it, he argued, other factors drove historical change, and in each human group the decisive factor was its mode of production, the level of productive forces and the pattern of ownership. He sketched three stages: a classless communal society; a class society with a state; and a socialist or communist society in which the state withered away and social life returned to equality at a higher level of productive forces. He warned that this sequence was neither mechanical nor universal. A society could pass rapidly through the stages appropriate to its own conditions, depending on the nature of the political power ruling it (Ibid.).

Cabral defined imperialism in the language of monopoly capital and then gave it a sharper image. It was piracy moved from the sea to dry land, reorganised to exploit the natural and human resources of colonised peoples. He distinguished direct colonial rule from indirect rule through local agents, which he called neo-colonialism, and held that the essence was the same in both: the violent seizure of a people’s right to develop its own productive forces. From this he derived his definition of national liberation. It was the regaining of a people’s historical personality, its return to history through the destruction of imperialist domination. He subordinated the legal formula to the material one. Whatever the language of international law, the right that mattered was the right of a people to have its own history, and the objective of liberation was to free the development of its productive forces. In the contemporary world, he concluded, the main front of the struggle was against neo-colonialism, and national liberation necessarily meant revolution (Ibid.).

The empirical basis for this analysis is in a seminar paper of 1964, Brief Analysis of the Social Structure in Guinea. Cabral distinguished the Fula society of the interior, where chiefs, nobles and religious leaders formed one group, from the Balante society, where land belonged to the village and each family received what it needed to subsist. In the towns he refused to misname the social groups. He was careful, he wrote, not to call them the proletariat or the working class. His method was to define each group’s dependence on the colonial regime, then its attitude to the liberation struggle, then its capacity for nationalism and finally its capacity for revolution after independence (Cabral, Brief Analysis of the Social Structure in Guinea (1964)).

Two findings in that paper deserve attention because they resist easy assumptions. The first concerns the peasantry. Guinea was a country of peasants, and the whole armed struggle rested on them, yet Cabral said plainly that the peasantry was not a revolutionary force. He drew a distinction between a physical force and a revolutionary force. Peasants were almost the whole population, controlled the country’s wealth and produced its food, but the party had faced real difficulty in persuading them to fight. This is a leader’s account of a political problem, and it tells us as much about the distance between the party and the countryside as it does about peasant consciousness. The second finding concerns the shortage of educated cadres. Cabral said the Portuguese had not educated the population, and he questioned what an intellectual could be in such a country, suggesting someone with concrete knowledge of the country and the enemy rather than deep theoretical learning. The party trained about a thousand cadres at its school in Conakry over roughly two years. When they returned to rural areas they inculcated a new mentality among peasants, and it was from these cadres, mostly of urban origin, that the leadership was drawn (Ibid.). This is one of the more candid passages in anti-colonial literature, and it also gives an indirect picture of ordinary people: peasants who had to be won over, and young cadres who went from the town to the village to do the winning.

Cardoso, drawing on Lopes, notes that this distinction between physical and revolutionary force became a live question in later scholarship. Lopes argued that for Cabral the peasant mass was not revolutionary, although the revolution could not be made without its support, and that Cabral never fully defined what a revolutionary force would be. Chabal interpreted the same passages as evidence that Cabral avoided systematic theory. The two readings are not simply contradictory. They show how writers have used the same text for different purposes (Cardoso, “A meta-analysis of Amílcar Cabral’s thought”).

The petty bourgeoisie and the choice Cabral set before it#

In “The Weapon of Theory,” Cabral identified the group he thought would inherit colonial power. In colonial conditions, he wrote, the sector capable of understanding imperialist domination and of directing the state apparatus inherited from it was the native petty bourgeoisie, the educated and salaried middle stratum. Because the working class was barely formed and the peasantry had not yet gained political consciousness, this stratum became the leadership, and that was one of the movement’s weaknesses. He began from a material premise. However revolutionary the consciousness of this group, it was a service class, not directly involved in production, and it did not possess the economic base needed to take and hold power.

From that premise came the dilemma for which the speech is best known. To retain the power that liberation placed in its hands, the petty bourgeoisie could allow itself to become more bourgeois, encouraging a commercial and bureaucratic class and turning itself into a national pseudo-bourgeoisie. That path meant betraying the revolution. The alternative was to strengthen its revolutionary consciousness, refuse the temptations of its own class, identify with the working people and not obstruct the revolution. To fulfil its role, Cabral wrote, the revolutionary petty bourgeoisie had to be capable of committing suicide as a class in order to be reborn as revolutionary workers. This alternative, betrayal or class suicide, was the dilemma of the petty bourgeoisie in the national liberation struggle (Cabral, “The Weapon of Theory” (1966)).

He returned to the theme in the 1964 seminar. Under colonialism, he said, the colonial state commanded history. After liberation the only group able to handle the instruments of that state was the African petty bourgeoisie, the inheritor of state power, though he added that he wished he were wrong. His positive definition was moral as well as political. The revolutionary petty bourgeoisie was honest, he said, meaning that it remained identified with the fundamental interests of the popular masses even under hostile conditions. It might have to commit suicide, but it would not lose, for in sacrificing itself it could be reborn in the condition of workers or peasants (Cabral, Brief Analysis of the Social Structure in Guinea (1964)).

The language of class suicide has attracted scholarly attention. Care is needed here. Cabral was describing a political choice, not reporting that any class had actually made it. He added that national liberation was essentially a political problem whose conditions gave it a moral character, which is why he wrote so much about the personal honesty of leaders (Cabral, “The Weapon of Theory” (1966)).

Culture, identity and the masses#

The companion piece to the 1966 speech is National Liberation and Culture, delivered in 1970 at a commemoration for Eduardo Mondlane, the Mozambican leader assassinated the previous year. Where the Havana address set out the political economy of liberation, this speech set out its cultural logic. Cabral began with a story about the Nazi propagandist Joseph Goebbels reaching for his revolver when culture was mentioned, and drew the lesson that even enemies of liberation understood culture as a factor of resistance. To take up arms against a people, he said, was to take up arms against its cultural life, because as long as any part of that people retained its own culture, foreign domination could never be certain of lasting (Cabral, National Liberation and Culture (1970)).

He defined culture in material terms. It was the vigorous expression, on the plane of ideas, of the physical and historical reality of a dominated society. It was both the fruit of a people’s history and a force that shaped history, because it influenced the relations between people and their environment and between groups within a society. He refused to romanticise it. Every culture contained strengths and weaknesses, virtues and failings, factors of progress and factors of stagnation. There was no single African culture; there were many. He attacked indiscriminate praise, the systematic exaltation of virtues without condemning faults and the blind acceptance of a culture’s values. What he wanted was a selective analysis of those values within the struggle for liberation (Ibid.).

The sentence that gave the speech its lasting force followed from his definition of national liberation as the freeing of productive forces. A people that freed itself from foreign domination would be culturally free only if it returned, without complexes and without rejecting useful influences from other cultures, to the upward paths of its own culture. “National liberation is necessarily an act of culture,” he wrote, and the liberation movement was the organised political expression of the culture of the people struggling (Ibid.).

This raised a question about who needed to change. In “Identity and Dignity in the Context of the National Liberation Struggle,” given in 1972, Cabral argued that the masses had not lost their culture at all. Repressed and humiliated, it had taken refuge in the villages and the forests and in the memory of the victims of domination. It survived and blossomed again through struggle. A return to the source or a cultural renaissance, he said, could not arise as a task for the masses, because they were the repository of the culture. The task belonged to those social groups that had been partly or fully assimilated into the colonial culture. He put a number on the failure of Portugal’s assimilation project. In the Portuguese colonies, he said, the maximum number of people assimilated was 0.3 percent of the population in Guinea after five centuries of so-called civilising influence and half a century of colonial peace, and more than 99 percent of the indigenous population remained untouched or almost untouched by the culture of the colonial power. These figures were Cabral’s own and were part of a political argument, not an independent demographic study (Cabral, “Identity and Dignity in the Context of the National Liberation Struggle” (1972), in Return to the Source).

The implication was that the war itself changed the educated minority. Colonial domination produced cultural alienation in part of the population, notably the urban and peasant petty bourgeoisie, who absorbed the coloniser’s outlook and looked down on their own people. The remedy was not a change of dress but a reconversion of minds, which Cabral called re-Africanisation in the Guinean case. It could begin before the struggle but was completed only during it, through daily contact with the popular masses and the shared sacrifices of war. He warned that political leaders, even famous ones, could be culturally alienated (Cabral, National Liberation and Culture (1970)).

Branwen Gruffydd Jones has traced this strand of Cabral’s thought, drawing on the archives of Cabral and Mário Pinto de Andrade to place his ideas about culture, race and liberation in a continental and global setting. Bongani Nyoka, reviewing Reiland Rabaka’s Concepts of Cabralism, notes that Rabaka traces Cabral’s intellectual ancestry through the Negritude writers and Fanon and argues that Cabral studied the concrete conditions of his own locality rather than repeating orthodox Marxism (Bongani Nyoka, “Concepts of Cabralism: On Cabral’s Intellectual Contributions,” Africa Development 42, no. 4, 2017, pp. 63 to 85). Whether Cabral is best read as a Marxist, a critical theorist or a man of action remains disputed. What is not disputed is that he treated culture as a field in which the political struggle was fought, not a decorative reflection of economic life.

Building a state in the liberated zones#

The PAIGC Programme turned these propositions into a platform. It called for the immediate and unconditional independence of Guinea and the Cape Verde Islands, permanent vigilance against the return of imperialism in new forms, democratic and anti-colonial government, economic independence, justice for all, a national defence linked to the people and an international policy of non-alignment. Its clauses placed economic activity under the principles of democratic socialism, required free elections based on direct, secret and universal voting, and promised the elimination of the complexes created by colonialism and the development of indigenous languages and Creole (The PAIGC Programme).

The war created institutions before it created a state. Cabral’s New Year’s Message of January 1973, written weeks before his death, reported that general elections had been held in the liberated areas with universal suffrage and a secret vote for Regional Councils and the first National Assembly in the people’s history. Voting followed eight months of public debate. The elected councils chose representatives to a National Popular Assembly of 120 members, of whom eighty came from the popular masses and forty from party cadres, the military and technical staff. Cabral ended with a reminder that the moment called for action rather than words (Cabral, New Year’s Message, January 1973, in Return to the Source). Here ordinary people appear not as a symbolic category but as voters in villages whose names the record does not preserve. The testimony is Cabral’s report to his party, so it tells us what the leadership wanted recorded about its own achievement.

On 24 September 1973 the People’s National Assembly, meeting in the Boe region, proclaimed the State of Guinea-Bissau. The preamble stated that the Assembly rested on the principle that power derived from the people and should serve them, and that its members had been elected by universal direct suffrage and secret ballot. The proclamation defined the new state as sovereign, republican, democratic, anti-colonialist and anti-imperialist, declared Portuguese colonial authority in the territory illegal, affirmed that the struggle was against Portuguese colonialism and not the Portuguese people, and adhered to non-alignment (Proclamation of the State of Guinea-Bissau, in Guinea-Bissau: Towards Final Victory!, Liberation Support Movement, 1974). It was the practical answer to the question Cabral had posed in Havana: a state constructed out of the liberated zones rather than handed over by a departing colonial power.

Cabral’s death#

Cabral was killed on 20 January 1973. The MIA Encyclopedia states that Portuguese agents working inside the PAIGC were involved. The editors of Return to the Source wrote that he was assassinated by Portuguese agents and that under his leadership the PAIGC had liberated three-quarters of the countryside in less than ten years of struggle (Return to the Source). The Executive Secretariat of OSPAAAL, the Organisation of Solidarity with the People of Asia, Africa and Latin America, issued a statement in Havana on 24 January denouncing the killing and rejecting attempts to explain it as an internal crisis within the PAIGC. It insisted that the assassination formed part of a wider plan to keep African countries under occupation in one form or another, naming Guinea-Bissau, Mozambique and Angola (The Cowardly Assassination of Amílcar Cabral, Tricontinental Bulletin, Year VIII, no. 82, 1973). These are movement statements issued in the immediate aftermath. They establish how allied organisations understood the event, not the findings of an independent investigation. Cabral’s colleagues continued the war and declared independence in September 1973.

Rodney: from the Upper Guinea Coast to Black Power#

Walter Rodney was born in Georgetown, Guyana, on 23 March 1942 into a working-class family. His father was a tailor and his mother a seamstress. He won a scholarship to Queen’s College and then to the University of the West Indies in Jamaica, graduating with first-class honours in history in 1963. A further scholarship took him to the School of Oriental and African Studies in London, where he received a doctorate in African history in 1966 at the age of twenty-four. His research on slavery on the Upper Guinea Coast drew on the records of Portuguese merchants in England and Portugal and was published by Oxford University Press in 1970 as A History of the Upper Guinea Coast, 1545 to 1800 (MIA Encyclopedia of Marxism, “Rodney, Walter (1942 to 1980)”).

He taught first in Tanzania and returned to the University of the West Indies in 1968. That year his groundings with the working poor of Jamaica drew the attention of the government. While he was at a Black Writers’ Conference in Montreal in October, the Jamaican government led by Hugh Shearer barred him from re-entering the country. The decision sparked riots in Kingston in which people were killed and injured by police and security forces and property was destroyed. Rodney’s conversations with Rastafarians were published as Groundings with My Brothers, a pamphlet that circulated widely in the Caribbean Black Power movement (Ibid.). This is one of the few places in the record where ordinary Jamaicans appear as interlocutors rather than as a mass. The biography says Rodney took his message into the gullies of Jamaica and shared African history with Rastafarians, a group largely excluded from respectable society. The account comes from a movement encyclopedia written after his death, so the scale of the riots and the reach of the pamphlet should be treated as claims from a sympathetic source.

After a short stay in Cuba, Rodney lectured in Tanzania from 1968 to 1974, and it was there that How Europe Underdeveloped Africa was written. In 1974 he returned to Guyana to take up a professorship at the University of Guyana, but the government rescinded the appointment. He stayed, joined the newly formed Working People’s Alliance, known as the WPA, and became the leading figure in resistance to the People’s National Congress government. On 11 July 1979 he and seven others were arrested after two government offices were burned. He was charged with arson alongside Rupert Roopnarine and Omawale. C. L. R. James, who knew him, described the cancelled appointment as a political blunder by the Guyanese leader Forbes Burnham, who did not want Rodney teaching at the university (C. L. R. James, Walter Rodney and the Question of Power, talk of 30 January 1981).

How Europe Underdeveloped Africa#

The book appeared in 1972 from Bogle-L’Ouverture in London in conjunction with the Tanzania Publishing House. A Portuguese translation by Edgar Valles was published in 1975 by Seara Nova. Rodney’s preface stated that the book concerned Africa’s present and searched the past only because the present could not otherwise be understood. He set the limits of the study at the fifteenth century and the end of the colonial period. He wrote that African development would become real only if the international capitalist system, which he called the principal factor of African underdevelopment over five centuries, was broken. He added that the book was written in Tanzania, where he thought theory had been accompanied by a more positive practice than elsewhere on the continent. Its purpose, he said, was to reach Africans who wanted to understand the nature of their own exploitation rather than to satisfy the standards of their oppressors and their academic spokesmen (Rodney, preface to Como a Europa Subdesenvolveu a África, Seara Nova, 1975; English renderings from the Portuguese). The chapter headings ran from the question of what development and underdevelopment meant, through Africa before European arrival, Africa’s contribution to European capitalist development, the European roots of African underdevelopment up to 1885, and the colonial and neo-colonial systems that perpetuated it.

Rodney’s English essays state the same argument. In a 1971 review of disengagement from imperialism he endorsed the claim that poverty and underdevelopment were caused by the drainage of wealth towards the developed capitalist nations, and that political independence did not halt an exploitation that was essentially economic. He noted that Tanzania had given this position full support in the Arusha Declaration (Rodney, Some Implications of the Question of Disengagement from Imperialism (1971)). In a 1970 essay on partition he wrote that Africa could not escape the expansion, domination and partition that had their roots in monopoly capital. He refused to treat partition and imperialism as the same thing. Imperialism, he explained, arose from the expansion of the capitalist economy, while partition was shaped by three factors: the nature of African social formations, racism within the capitalist superstructure, and African opposition to European incursion (Rodney, The Imperialist Partition of Africa (1970)). Those three factors explain the link between his economic argument and Cabral’s cultural one. Racism and resistance belonged to the superstructure and to political life, not merely to the balance of trade.

Scholars have spent fifty years testing the book’s reach and limits. Immanuel Wallerstein, reviewing Rodney’s three major histories in 1986, observed that How Europe Underdeveloped Africa was unlike his other scholarly books. It was not based on archival research, had no footnotes, and was openly didactic, addressed to students and educated Africans and published cheaply by popular presses. Wallerstein identified a persistent question in the reception of Rodney’s early work: critics thought it implied a docile Africa shaped by an aggressive Europe, and African historians debated whether Africans were the agents of their own history or merely reacted to others (Immanuel Wallerstein, review of Rodney’s books, American Ethnologist 13, no. 2, May 1986, pp. 330 to 337). A special issue of African Economic History marked the book’s fiftieth anniversary in 2022. Richard Drayton traced its roots to the Caribbean, to Cheddi Jagan’s account of how British Guiana’s plantation and mining economy created wealth abroad and poverty at home, and to Eric Williams’s Capitalism and Slavery. Ana Lucia Araujo examined the claim that enslaved people were purchased with goods of little value, testing one of the book’s supporting arguments against later research (Richard Drayton, “The Caribbean Origins of How Europe Underdeveloped Africa,” African Economic History 50, no. 2, May 2022, pp. 17 to 21; Ana Lucia Araujo, “Did Rodney Get It Wrong? Europe Underdeveloped Africa but Enslaved People Were Not Always Purchased with Rubbish,” African Economic History 50, no. 2, May 2022, pp. 22 to 32). In a 2023 Small Axe issue, Adom Getachew analysed Rodney’s image of the historian as a diagnostician who reads the present through the past, and Peter James Hudson examined his pedagogy and method in the setting of Black intellectual activism (Adom Getachew, “The Present as History: Walter Rodney and the Search for Usable African Pasts,” Small Axe 27, no. 3, 2023; Peter James Hudson, “History, Method, and Myth: Walter Rodney and the Geographies of Black Radicalism,” Small Axe 27, no. 3, 2023). The argument that Rodney paid too little attention to African agency, and the counter-argument that he combined external and internal factors, remain live positions in this literature.

The intellectual and the working poor#

Rodney’s 1971 essay contains his most explicit tribute to Cabral. He defended the term petty bourgeoisie as a neutral analytical description of a local stratum living privileged lives in a colonial or post-colonial society. He praised Cabral for treating that stratum not as a stereotype but as a group with several possible futures, and for including himself within it. Rodney singled out Cabral’s concern with the nationalist capacity of the petty bourgeoisie and its revolutionary capacity after independence, and he defined the revolutionary petty bourgeoisie as the section that had joined the liberation struggle and was already pushing it towards socialist reconstruction in the liberated zones (Rodney, Some Implications of the Question of Disengagement from Imperialism (1971)). A footnote in the same essay quoted Cabral’s dictum that the only social sector capable of understanding imperialist domination was the petty bourgeoisie.

Rodney’s own position refined rather than rejected this analysis. He thought the decisive ideological battle took place within the petty bourgeoisie, not directly between peasants and imperialists or inside the peasantry. The progressive intellectual had to go beyond the limits capitalism imposed and reach the essence of the system. The revolution, he wrote, required that the millions who had been silenced throughout history should speak and choose, and it was the task of revolutionaries to show peasants and workers how socialist ideas bore on their daily lives (Ibid.). This was Rodney’s version of Cabral’s return to the source. The intellectual did not bring consciousness from outside as a gift. He dissolved his own stratum into the working classes so that the masses could speak. In a 1974 congress paper Rodney put the class point sharply: the African petty bourgeoisie as a ruling class used state power against socialist ideology, against the material interests of workers and against the political unity of the African masses (Rodney, Aspects of the International Class Struggle in Africa, the Caribbean and America, written 1974, published 1975). The paper’s eight-point platform called for disengagement from international monopoly capital, the construction of a socialist society, the removal of colonial borders, and a Pan-Africanism that was internationalist, anti-imperialist and socialist.

Socialism and national liberation#

In his 1975 address Marxism and African Liberation, Rodney argued that nationalism and socialism could not be separated. A scientific socialist position was revolutionary because it aimed consciously to undermine the system of production and the political relations that flowed from it. Ideas and debate did not drop from the sky, he said, and were not simply the product of a conspiracy. African people, like other people of the global South, had a vested interest in scientific socialism because it offered itself as a weapon of theory, a tool at the level of ideas for dismantling the capitalist and imperialist structure. The phrasing deliberately echoed the title of Cabral’s 1966 speech. Rodney criticised Kwame Nkrumah for refusing to admit that Ghana contained classes and class contradictions. His conclusion was direct: the petty bourgeoisie could not fulfil the historical tasks, because national liberation required a socialist ideology and the two could not be separated (Rodney, Marxism and African Liberation (1975)).

At the end of that address he turned to Cabral. He quoted a paraphrase of the 1966 sentence, with explicit attribution: there might be revolutions that had a revolutionary theory and still failed, but there had certainly been no revolution that succeeded without a revolutionary theory. Rodney’s wording was his own, not a verbatim repetition of Cabral’s sentence. It establishes that Rodney had read Cabral and used him as an authority. It does not establish that Cabral read Rodney, and no document cited here supports that claim (Ibid.). Rodney had used a longer tribute four years earlier, quoting Cabral’s “revolutionary petty bourgeoisie” and “nationalist capacity” as analytical tools. The connection between the two projects runs in one direction, from Cabral to Rodney.

Rodney’s insistence on mass agency appears again in a 1972 talk on the African Revolution. He said that what had been used as a badge of servility could be turned into a bond of unity and a weapon, and that the revolution was made by and for the mass of workers and peasants and such leadership as emerged from their struggle. In the same talk he argued that identity was both affirmation and negation. Black became relevant to an African at the point of contradiction with white men, and the continuing sharpness of that contradiction made Black identity a weapon of emancipation (Rodney, The African Revolution (talk, 31 March 1972; published 1981)).

People’s Power, No Dictator#

Rodney’s last years in Guyana produced People’s Power, No Dictator, published by the WPA in 1979. It opens with a definition of dictatorship as rule accountable to no one, then argues that the Burnham government disguised itself as its own opposite. Rodney treated this as a cultural question in Cabral’s sense. Politics was the making of choices about collective welfare and was one of the highest aspects of a people’s culture. One-man rule reduced politics to manipulation and destroyed initiative, frustrating even the class from which the dictator came (Rodney, People’s Power, No Dictator (Georgetown: Working People’s Alliance, 1979)).

The pamphlet was not only a denunciation. It developed a theory of mass power. The regime panicked at the thought of a general strike, Rodney wrote, because no amount of violence could replace the labour power of workers. Mass withdrawal of labour was the ultimate weapon. He advocated non-cooperation, which he defined as citizens refusing to cooperate in their own oppression, and he described a strategy of national unity across race and class. He reported that Africans and Indians were standing side by side as they had not since 1953, that Indian sugar workers and African bauxite workers were making common cause, and that the WPA stood within the ranks of workers and peasants. The demand for Burnham’s removal had to be joined to an alternative, a Government of National Unity (Ibid.). These were claims by a party about its own support, and the judgement about racial unity was a political claim rather than a neutral measurement.

Rodney’s last speech, Sign of the Times, was delivered on 6 June 1980 and printed in the WPA’s memorial booklet the following year. He said his confidence came from the demonstrated capacity of the people as a whole. “The revolution is made by ordinary people, not by angels,” he said, made by people from all walks of life and especially by the working class, and he celebrated the moment when a street force member became a fighting cadre. He joined the material and the constitutional in one sentence: no milk and no constitution were not two separate things, because a person could not grumble about the price of milk while forgetting the absence of a free constitution (Rodney, Sign of the Times (6 June 1980; WPA, 1981)). This is the clearest statement in his own words of why he thought culture, food and political rights belonged to one struggle.

Rodney’s death and a disputed date#

Rodney was killed by a bomb in Georgetown on 13 June 1980. The MIA Encyclopedia says that after a period of constant harassment and at least one attempt on his life, he was assassinated in the middle of the city on the evening of 13 June. Tim Hector, a Caribbean contemporary, recalled in 1997 that the anniversary fell on 13 June and remembered the telephone call from his comrade Conrad Luke with the news (MIA Encyclopedia; Tim Hector, “Walter Rodney Was Most Rare,” Fan the Flame / Outlet, 20 June 1997).

C. L. R. James gave a fuller account in his memorial lecture of 30 January 1981, but he dated the killing to 13 October 1980. He described Rodney as trapped by a member of Guyana’s army into handling a device that exploded. He recalled warning him: “Therefore, I told him, ‘Beware of assassination, because Burnham will kill you. You are someone who could represent the people against him.’ But they did not beware sufficiently.” James thought a political leader should never have been in that position (C. L. R. James, Walter Rodney and the Question of Power). The conflict over the date is unresolved in the sources available here. The MIA Encyclopedia, Tim Hector’s dated anniversary and the date of Rodney’s last speech all point to June, and this chapter uses 13 June. James’s testimony is valuable for the circumstances he described, but his date does not agree with the other accounts.

What joined them, and what did not#

Cabral and Rodney never wrote a joint text. Rodney’s 1971 essay quoted Cabral approvingly and took over his categories of nationalist capacity, revolutionary capacity and revolutionary petty bourgeoisie. In 1975 he used Cabral’s dictum about revolutionary theory as the authority for joining national liberation to scientific socialism. That is a documented one-way link. It does not justify calling the two projects identical.

They converged on several points. Both treated theory as a material weapon rather than a decoration. Both refused to regard national independence as the end of the struggle. Both located the danger in the educated middle stratum that inherited the colonial state. Both held that this stratum could redeem itself only by dissolving into the working masses. Both treated culture as one of the places where the struggle was fought, not as a reflection of economic life. Both grounded their politics in the working poor, in the peasants of Guinea-Bissau whom Cabral said had to be won over, the workers and Rastafarians of Jamaica among whom Rodney lived and taught, and the sugar workers, bauxite workers and street cadres of Guyana whose unity he described in his last writings.

The differences were of emphasis. Cabral’s centre of gravity was the mode of production and the historical personality of a people. His cultural analysis explained why a population resisted and how a liberation movement could become its organised political expression. Rodney’s centre of gravity was the international economy and the drainage of surplus from Africa to the capitalist metropoles. His cultural analysis explained how a historian could become accountable to the people whose exploitation he studied. Cabral wrote as the general secretary of a movement at war and built a party school, liberated zones and an assembly. Rodney wrote as a historian who became an organiser and, in Guyana, as the leading figure of an alliance against a government that had cancelled his appointment. Cabral was killed in January 1973, months before the proclamation he had helped make possible. Rodney was killed in June 1980, a week after his last speech. In both cases the movements carried on and published the words of the dead, the PAIGC in its proclamation and the WPA in its memorial booklet.
