Elinor Ostrom: Governing the Commons Beyond State and Market

A research profile of the political economist's evidence for self-governed common-pool resources and its relevance to socialist economic alternatives.

Elinor Ostrom showed that people who share a resource can often govern it well without either privatising it or handing it to a central state. Trained as a political scientist, she spent much of her career at Indiana University studying fisheries, forests, irrigation systems and groundwater basins in which users made and enforced their own rules. She set out the conditions under which such arrangements lasted, and she rejected the idea that markets and governments are the only two possible forms of economic organisation. In 2009 she received half of the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences, the first woman to do so. This profile states what her evidence showed and where critics thought it too narrow. It also states plainly what it did not show: no source opened for this profile has Ostrom calling herself a socialist or setting out a socialist blueprint. Her relevance here lies in collective governance and economic alternatives, not in a socialist programme.

Biography#

Elinor Ostrom was born Elinor Clair Awan in Los Angeles on 7 August 1933 and died in Bloomington, Indiana, on 12 June 2012. The Nobel Foundation’s facts page records that she grew up in a family of simple means, studied political science at the University of California, Los Angeles, took her doctorate there in 1965, worked at Indiana University in Bloomington and was also affiliated with Arizona State University and Virginia Tech. It records her marriage to the political scientist Vincent Ostrom in 1963. The Royal Swedish Academy awarded her half of the 2009 prize “for her analysis of economic governance, especially the commons”; Oliver Williamson received the other half. The Ostrom Workshop and the Nobel interview transcript identify her as the first woman to receive the economics prize (Nobel facts; Workshop Nobel page).

Her autobiographical essay, written for the award, gives the detail behind that record. She grew up during the Depression, attended Beverly Hills High School because her mother arranged it, and joined the debate team; debate taught her that public questions have at least two sides and that a good argument must be made for each. No one in her immediate family had been to university. She worked in a library, a dime store and a bookshop, and finished her undergraduate degree in three years. When she looked for a job, employers asked about typing and shorthand, so she took a correspondence course in shorthand, later finding it useful for note-taking in interviews rather than for dictation. She worked for a year as an export clerk and then became an assistant personnel manager at a firm that had not previously employed a woman outside secretarial work (Nobel biographical essay).

The economics department at UCLA discouraged her from doctoral study in economics because she had not taken mathematics beyond algebra and geometry, and political science was also reluctant to admit women. She says she was one of four women in a class of forty and was told that the faculty had criticised the admissions committee for admitting women and offering them assistantships. These are her retrospective recollections, published in 2009–10, not independently checked records. In graduate work she joined a team studying the Southern California water industry, framed by Vincent Ostrom, Charles Tiebout and Robert Warren, and was assigned the West Basin groundwater basin. She says she did not know at the time that she was studying a common-pool resource problem: a shared stock, such as a groundwater basin, fishery, pasture, lake or forest, from which many people withdraw units, so that what one person takes reduces what remains for the others. Hardin’s “Tragedy of the Commons” and Olson’s The Logic of Collective Action appeared only after she defended her dissertation in 1965 (Nobel biographical essay).

In 1965 Vincent Ostrom took a professorship at Indiana University and she followed, as she puts it, because it was hard for a department to hire a woman. She was first appointed as a visiting assistant professor to teach American government at 7:30 on Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday mornings, then became graduate adviser and moved to a regular appointment. In the Nobel interview she says her dissertation was titled “Public entrepreneurship”; no dissertation record was opened to confirm that title. The first fifteen years of her research at Indiana concerned police industries, not commons. In the interview she describes comparing small and large departments in 80 metropolitan areas and finding 84 crime labs, because departments contracted with hospitals rather than duplicating expensive facilities. She returned to the commons in the mid-1980s after serving on a National Research Council committee that revealed how much case research was scattered across disciplines and sectors (Nobel interview transcript).

The Workshop’s own history says that Vincent and Elinor Ostrom founded the Center for Political Theory and Policy Analysis at Indiana University in 1973, and that the name “workshop” reflected their experience of apprenticeship in woodworking with the furniture maker Paul Goodman. Ostrom told Adam Smith that the centre was established in 1973–74 and deliberately put economists, political scientists and other scholars around one table. The Workshop says she was the first woman to win the economics prize and that the prize is displayed in Bryan Hall at Indiana University Bloomington (Workshop history).

Intellectual tradition#

Ostrom belonged to the Bloomington School of political economy and to the new institutional economics, but her central intellectual move was to refuse the neat division between “the market” and “the state” that dominated both traditions. In her 2010 American Economic Review article she writes that mid-twentieth-century economics treated the market as the optimal institution for private goods and “the” government as the institution needed for nonprivate goods. Her work, and that of the Workshop, inserted associations, user groups, special districts and other intermediate forms into that scheme. The Academy’s press release described the result as showing that economic analysis can illuminate most forms of social organisation, not only markets and firms (AER article record; Nobel press release).

She was trained as a political scientist rather than an economist and repeatedly stressed interdisciplinary method. Her Nobel autobiography says the modern university fragments knowledge by discipline and by method, and that economists using national statistics, laboratory experimenters and field researchers too often criticise one another rather than combine evidence. The 2009 interview and her 2010 paper both defend multiple methods: case comparison, field work, laboratory experiments and statistical analysis. This places her close to institutional economics and public-choice analysis in method, while her conclusion that users can self-govern challenged the standard collective-action and common-property models.

She rejected the label of someone offering a general privatisation or anti-state doctrine. Sarker and Blomquist argue in a 2019 journal article that Governing the Commons has been misread as anti-Hardin, anti-tragedy, anti-statist or anti-privatisation, and that this reading is wrong (Sarker and Blomquist, 2019). The opened sources contain no passage in which Ostrom calls herself a socialist, a Marxist or a libertarian. What they do contain is a refusal to treat any single ownership form as a universal answer, and a preference for analysing how particular rules fit particular resources and communities.

Major contributions#

The first contribution is the empirical challenge to the claim that shared resources are necessarily destroyed. The Academy’s press release says she “challenged the conventional wisdom that common property is poorly managed and should be either regulated by central authorities or privatized”, and that outcomes in user-managed fisheries, pastures, woods, lakes and groundwater basins were “more often than not, better than predicted by standard theories”. In her 2010 article she describes a meta-analysis in which the Workshop team screened more than 500 case studies and coded 44 inshore fishing groups and 47 irrigation systems. Of the irrigation systems, 12 were government managed and 40 per cent of those performed well; of the 25 farmer-managed systems, over 70 per cent performed well. She also reports laboratory results: subjects overharvested when they could not communicate, but face-to-face “cheap talk” sharply increased joint pay-offs, and when subjects could pay to sanction others, they used the option despite game-theory predictions and reached about 94 per cent of the optimum. These are her reported results; the underlying data were not re-analysed for this profile (Nobel press release; AER article, PDF copy).

The second contribution is the set of eight design principles presented in chapter 3 of Governing the Commons. Table 3.1 on printed page 90 of the book lists them as:

  • Clearly defined boundaries. Those with rights to withdraw resource units, and the boundaries of the resource itself, must be clearly defined.
  • Congruence between appropriation and provision rules and local conditions. Rules limiting time, place, technology or quantity must fit local conditions and be related to rules requiring labour, materials or money.
  • Collective-choice arrangements. Most people affected by operational rules can participate in changing them.
  • Monitoring. Monitors who audit resource conditions and user behaviour are accountable to the users or are the users themselves.
  • Graduated sanctions. Rule-breakers are likely to face sanctions that escalate with the seriousness and context of the offence, imposed by other users or officials accountable to them.
  • Conflict-resolution mechanisms. Users and officials have rapid access to low-cost local arenas for resolving disputes.
  • Minimal recognition of rights to organise. Outside governmental authorities do not challenge the right of users to devise their own institutions.
  • Nested enterprises. For resources within larger systems, appropriation, provision, monitoring, enforcement, conflict resolution and governance are organised in multiple layers.

In her 1999 paper she defines a design principle as “a conception used consciously or unconsciously by those constituting and reconstituting a continuing association of individuals about a general organizing principle”, and says that seven principles characterise most robust systems while an eighth covers the larger and more complex cases. In the book itself she warns that the list is “still quite speculative” and that she was “not yet willing to argue that these design principles are necessary conditions for achieving institutional robustness in CPR settings”. In the Nobel interview she similarly rejects the idea that she was prescribing “should”: she was asking what the long-lasting systems had in common, and expected application to differ from case to case (1999 workshop paper; Nobel interview transcript).

The Royal Swedish Academy’s “Illustrated Presentation” lists seven “keys to successful cooperation”. These are the Academy’s summary of the research it was rewarding, not Ostrom’s own list, and no source opened for this profile explains the difference. They should not be substituted for her eight design principles, which come from her book and her own 1999 paper (Nobel illustrated information; Workshop design principles).

A third contribution is the Institutional Analysis and Development framework, set out in Understanding Institutional Diversity (Princeton University Press, 2005). The publisher’s description says the framework lets a scholar choose the relevant level of interaction and examine the arena, the rules participants use, the attributes of the biophysical world and the attributes of the community. It concentrates on rules, showing how participants change rules and what design principles characterise robust self-organised governance (Princeton University Press).

A fourth contribution is the idea of polycentric governance: many centres of decision making that are formally independent of one another but can nevertheless function as a coherent system through competition, contract and conflict resolution. That is the definition by Vincent Ostrom, Tiebout and Warren which she quotes in the 2010 article. A fifth is the idea of property rights as a bundle of access, withdrawal, management, exclusion and alienation rights, rather than a single right. On this reading a resource can be common-pool in its physical characteristics while being governed by private, government, community or no-property regimes. That distinction bears directly on the questions raised in this book about ownership and control and the limits of central planning.

Analysis of capitalism#

Ostrom did not write a general critique of capitalism as a mode of production, and the opened sources contain no systematic account of exploitation, class or the labour process. Her object was narrower: the claims made by economists and policymakers about how economic goods must be governed. In the 2010 paper she identifies the “two optimal organizational forms” picture of the mid-twentieth century, in which markets were held to be optimal for private goods and hierarchy for nonprivate goods, and quotes Robert Sugden’s complaint that modern economics sees the world through the eyes of a government ready to rescue markets when they fail. Her own position is that markets are one governance form among several and that many outcomes are produced by private, public and community arrangements operating together at several scales. She therefore treats both privatisation and central regulation as hypotheses to be tested against cases, not as universal remedies.

Her empirical work supplies the substance of the objection. In the Academy’s “Popular information” account, modern concrete-and-steel dams built with foreign aid in Nepal often performed worse than locally maintained earth-and-wood systems, because durable dams weakened the ties between head-end and tail-end users. The same account says that a failure to privatise oil pools in Texas and Oklahoma in the 1930s caused massive waste, and states plainly that the lesson is not that user management is always preferable: there are many cases where privatisation or public regulation works better. In her 1999 paper she lists “blueprint thinking” as the first threat to commons governance and criticises donor projects that ignore indigenous knowledge, imported rules and top-down organisational charters. In the 2010 article she says externally imposed decentralisation can turn stable forests into deforested ones, and that what matters is how rules fit local ecology and whether users see the system as legitimate and equitable. This is close to the concern of this book’s chapter on the environment with what central direction does to local knowledge (Nobel popular information).

The implication for socialist economic alternatives is indirect but important. Her work supplies a case against assuming that either private ownership or state ownership is the necessary form of collective ownership. She does not argue that capitalist markets are efficient by definition; she argues that the market/state dichotomy is too poor a tool to explain or design economic governance. Critics on the right, such as Walter Block and Ivan Jankovic, read her as too hostile to privatisation; critics on the left, such as Derek Wall as reported by Herzberg, read her emphasis on direct participation as support for radical policies. Both readings treat a method for matching rules to conditions as if it were a general system, which the opened sources do not support.

Analysis of socialism#

No source opened for this profile shows Ostrom calling herself a socialist, and none shows her offering a blueprint for a socialist society. No opened source shows her advocating workers’ control of industry, central planning, public ownership of the means of production or any comprehensive socialist institution. What she did offer was evidence about collective ownership and self-governance of specific resources, together with a strong caution against centralised state management as a universal answer.

The clearest statement relevant to socialism comes from the Royal Swedish Academy’s “Popular information” page, not from Ostrom’s own pen. Discussing grasslands in Mongolia and neighbouring parts of China and Russia, the Academy says that state-owned collectives imposed by central government encouraged permanent settlement and degraded the land, and that when China dissolved the People’s Communes in the early 1980s and privatised much of the grassland of Inner Mongolia, permanent settlement and degradation continued. The page concludes: “As satellite images clearly reveal, both socialism and privatization are associated with worse long-term outcomes than those observed in traditional group-based governance.” This is the Academy’s summary of the research it was rewarding; it should not be quoted as Ostrom’s own words, and the underlying satellite evidence was not opened here (Nobel popular information).

In her own 2010 article the parallel claim is more carefully stated. Reviewing comparative forest research, she writes that forests under government, private and communal regimes “sometimes” meet goals such as biodiversity protection, carbon storage and improved livelihoods, and “at other times” fail; she adds that it is not the general type of governance that explains the result but the fit between rules, ecology, legitimacy and equity. That is the positive institutional content relevant to collective ownership: durable common property depends on boundaries, participation, accountable monitoring, graduated sanctions, cheap dispute resolution and recognition by outside authorities, with larger systems nested in several layers. Her 1999 paper adds the negative side: blueprint thinking, overreliance on simple majority or unanimity voting, rapid external change, failure to transmit principles between generations, too frequent resort to outside donors, aid that ignores local institutions, corruption and the absence of larger-scale supporting arrangements. This body of work belongs beside, and not inside, the economic calculation debate: that chapter notes that Mises, Hayek, Lange, Lerner and Ostrom are absent from its source base and should be read directly before any verdict is attempted on allocating capital without capital markets.

Recommended reading#

These five selections are starting points drawn from the research dossier, not a complete account of her output.

  • Governing the Commons (1990) — the founding book: the three models she rejected, the case material and Table 3.1 with the eight principles in her own words.
  • “Beyond Markets and States: Polycentric Governance of Complex Economic Systems” (2010) — the mature statement of polycentricity, the market/state dichotomy and the empirical record, in a revised version of her Nobel lecture.
  • “Collective Action and the Evolution of Social Norms” (2000) — the short version of her behavioural argument, including conditional cooperators and willing punishers.
  • Understanding Institutional Diversity (2005) — the methodological book: the IAD framework and how rules are classified and changed.
  • “Design Principles and Threats to Sustainable Organizations that Manage Commons” (1999) — her own later wording of the principles together with the eight threats that can destroy even a well-designed commons.

Intellectual influences#

The influences documented in her own writing and interviews are unusually specific. Vincent Ostrom, Charles Tiebout and Robert Warren supplied the framing of the Southern California water study and the 1961 concept of polycentricity, which she quotes in the 2010 article. Vincent Ostrom also supplied the idea of science as artisanship, which she and her husband turned into the Workshop’s name and organisation; the Workshop history quotes her on learning what a workshop was from the furniture maker Paul Goodman. Herbert Simon and Vincent Ostrom are named in the 2010 paper as writers who challenged the assumption of full rationality, and Douglass North is cited for the claim that institutions reduce uncertainty in complex environments. John R. Commons’s definition of property rights as bundles of rights underlies her five-rights scheme, via Edella Schlager’s work and her own with Commons (Workshop history).

Two writers were foils whose questions shaped the research rather than mentors whose conclusions she adopted. In her Nobel biography she records that Hardin’s “Tragedy of the Commons” and Olson’s The Logic of Collective Action appeared just after her doctorate, and that neither had been framed from the case material she had already gathered. In the 2010 article she quotes Hardin’s open pasture and the predicted inexorable overuse, then sets the accumulated case evidence against it. Margaret Levi’s idea of “quasi-voluntary compliance” is explicitly borrowed in the 1999 paper to explain why users monitor one another: compliance is willing as long as others also comply and violators can be caught. Robert Sugden’s criticism of government-centred economics appears as an epigraph in the 2010 article. Later collaborators named in her own acknowledgements and workshop records include Roy Gardner, James Walker, William Blomquist, Edella Schlager, Shui Yan Tang, Amy Poteete, Marco Janssen, Michael McGinnis, Charlotte Hess and Arun Agrawal (Nobel biographical essay).

Influence and legacy#

Ostrom’s most visible institutional legacy is the Workshop she founded with Vincent Ostrom at Indiana University in 1973. Its own history says it became a centre of commons governance research, expanded from water and forestry into areas such as opera sustainability and space governance, and celebrated fifty years in 2023. It maintains a large library and digital collections, runs a memorial lecture and an Ostrom Research Award, and retains a close relationship with the International Association for the Study of the Commons, which she helped to build. The Workshop publicises “Ostrom’s Law” — “A resource arrangement that works in practice can work in theory” — as a summary of her method (Workshop history).

Her academic influence is measurable in the scale of the literature around the design principles. Cox, Arnold and Villamayor-Tomás coded 91 studies and 77 cases in 2010 and found that every principle had more supportive than unsupportive cases, with the strongest critiques being theoretical rather than empirical. A later Lincoln Institute paper by the same authors reports a meta-analysis of 112 studies and concludes that the principles are robust but should be read probabilistically, not as blueprints. Baggio and others re-coded 69 cases in 2016 and found that configurations of principles, not individual rules, explain success. The Nobel prize in 2009 made her the first woman to win the economics award, and the Academy’s press release treated her work as having moved economic governance from the fringe to the forefront of research (Cox, Arnold and Villamayor-Tomás, 2010; Baggio and others, 2016).

Beyond environmental governance, the framework was taken into knowledge commons, cybersecurity, climate policy and urban and international governance. The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, the National Research Council and donor agencies all appear in the case literature as users or critics of the approach. Her work also became contested political territory: Herzberg records that classical liberals such as Aligica, Boettke and Tarko read her local-governance analysis as a check on state power, while Derek Wall reads it as support for radical, participatory politics. Both claims rest on selections from a framework whose author insisted that rules must fit local conditions (Herzberg, 2020).

Criticisms and controversies#

The first criticism concerns the scope of her result. Eduardo Araral’s 2014 paper in Environmental Science & Policy, whose abstract and author’s slides were opened, argues that Ostrom’s critique of Hardin is valid for small, locally governed commons but not for large, national, regional or global commons, where he holds Hardin’s account to be better supported. His slides list the alleged faults in the studies that generalise her principles: “Confirmatory bias / halo effect”, specification and identification problems, multicollinearity, “Selection bias” and endogeneity. They also argue that the successful cases are closer to excludable private-property regimes than to open-access commons, while the unsuccessful groundwater and fishery cases fit the standard model. This is the clearest claim that her cases are unrepresentative. His paper was published in an environmental-policy journal and classified in economics, but he is a public-policy scholar at the National University of Singapore; the full article was paywalled here, so the detail rests on the abstract, section snippets seen in search results and his own conference slides (Araral, 2014 slides).

A second criticism concerns whether the cases and meta-analyses establish what their authors claim. Araral’s slides say a study by Cox and colleagues used 168 case studies and suffered from the faults listed above. A 2016 response by Cox and colleagues, seen only as a search-result endpoint, says Araral was wrong about both: the review analysed 91 cases and used cross-tabulations and mostly bivariate tests, not regressions. The figures should therefore be reported with the source and the dispute attached, not merged. Baggio and colleagues add a separate methodological caution: in their 69-case re-coding, only 27 cases had all principles coded, and the presence of a single principle said little; success required clusters of principles and varied by irrigation, fisheries and forestry. Cox and his co-authors themselves concede that the principles are incomplete, that external socioeconomic and biophysical factors matter, that the design principles may not scale up, and that the approach may sit awkwardly with more constructionist accounts of embedded social relations.

A third criticism is normative. Herzberg reports that Walter Block and Ivan Jankovic, in a 2016 American Journal of Economics and Sociology article whose full text was not opened, worry that Governing the Commons argues too strongly against privatisation and adds little that cannot be achieved by individual contracting at community level. Herzberg also notes that Ostrom’s apparently neutral treatment of which commons succeed can mask conflicts between a commons community and outsiders, and that power asymmetries and rights of exit affect whether a “successful” commons is desirable. From another direction, Araral argues that her critique of the state is justified but her critique of private property and markets needs rethinking. Sarker and Blomquist reply that much of this criticism targets a misreading: her work was not, they argue, anti-statist or anti-privatisation. Ostrom’s own interview supports the qualification: she said local conflict-resolution arrangements differ between Valencia and Los Angeles and that experts who want to impose a single mechanism should first ask what they are imposing. Readers who want to see how such disputes over evidence are handled in this book should consult the editorial source policy.

Related thinkers#

No published profile of the figures below is assumed to exist in this collection, so no links to individual profiles are given here.

  • Vincent Ostrom, Charles Tiebout and Robert Warren — the closest intellectual predecessors; their 1961 article gave her the concept of polycentricity and the Southern California research setting.
  • Oliver Williamson — co-recipient of the 2009 prize and her fellow interviewee; both worked on economic governance, he on firms and hierarchies, she on commons and associations.
  • Garrett Hardin and Mancur Olson — the theorists whose predictions her case comparisons were designed to test, not writers whose conclusions she adopted.
  • Herbert Simon, Douglass North, John R. Commons and Margaret Levi — concepts she used explicitly: bounded rationality, institutions as uncertainty-reducing rules, bundles of rights and quasi-voluntary compliance.
  • Roy Gardner, James Walker, William Blomquist, Edella Schlager, Shui Yan Tang, Amy Poteete, Marco Janssen, Michael McGinnis, Charlotte Hess and Arun Agrawal — collaborators who extended the framework into experiments, forests, irrigation and social-ecological systems.
  • Eduardo Araral, Walter Block and Ivan Jankovic — critics representing the claim of limited external validity and the private-property objection. Sarker and Blomquist defend her against misreading, and Derek Wall represents an attempted radical appropriation of her emphasis on direct participation.
