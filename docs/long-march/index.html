The Long March and the Chinese Civil War

In the autumn of 1936 Edgar Snow sat with Mao Tse-tung in Pao-an, the small town in the Communist-held Northwest that served as the Party's base.

In the autumn of 1936 Edgar Snow sat with Mao Tse-tung in Pao-an, the small town in the Communist-held Northwest that served as the Party’s base. They worked at night. Snow wrote Mao’s answers in English, an interpreter read them back in Chinese, and Mao corrected the result. Snow described the method himself: “My interviews were written in full in English, and then re-translated into Chinese and corrected by Mao, who is noted for his insistence upon accuracy of detail.” (Edgar Snow, interviews written July–September 1936, published November 1936) This is the closest surviving thing to a participant’s voice at the end of the march, and it reaches us through two languages, an editor and a foreigner’s choice of questions. It was said before the retreat had hardened into a legend.

The retreat in contemporary words#

Weeks after the First Front Army reached northern Shensi, Mao addressed Party activists at Wayaopao. He conceded that the old base areas were gone and that the army had been considerably weakened, then refused the word defeat. “the Long March is the first of its kind in the annals of history, that it is a manifesto, a propaganda force, a seeding-machine.” (Mao Tse-tung, “On Tactics Against Japanese Imperialism,” 27 December 1935) In the same report he gave the distance in his own idiom: “by using our two legs we swept across a distance of more than twenty thousand li through the length and breadth of eleven provinces.” (same report, 27 December 1935) A li is roughly a third of a mile.

Snow, who was not on the march but interviewed survivors and read their handouts within a year of the arrival, supplied the texture that a leadership speech leaves out. He recorded a First Army Corps itinerary of 18,088 li, heavy losses before Guizhou, distraction marches through the province, a forced night march to an unguarded ferry on the Jinsha River, and the rush to an iron-chain bridge over the Tatu. P’eng Teh-huai put the stakes in a sentence Snow kept: “‘Victory was life,’ said P’eng Teh-huai; ‘defeat was certain death.’” (P’eng Teh-huai, quoted by Edgar Snow, Red Star Over China, “The Heroes of Tatu,” written 1936–37) Mao’s own explanation of survival was blunter and more political. “Tactics are important, but we could not exist if the majority of the people did not support us. We are nothing but the fist of the people beating their oppressors!” (Mao Tse-tung, autobiographical interview recorded 1936, English translation in Red Star Over China) Each of these statements was made in public by a man with a stake in the outcome. They are evidence of what participants chose to say, not a neutral measurement of the road.

Zunyi and the numbers that do not agree#

The Party’s own chronicle dates the meeting at Zunyi, a town in Guizhou province, to 15 and 17 January 1935. It records that the army fell from more than 86,000 at the start of the year to just over 30,000 after the fighting at the Xiang River, that Mao was co-opted onto the Standing Committee, and that a three-man group of Mao, Zhou Enlai and Wang Jiaxiang was formed. The chronicle calls the meeting a turning point that saved the Party. Historians working with the same fragmentary record read it differently. Benjamin Yang argued that Mao’s rise was gradual and that the Communists moved from revolutionary idealism to political realism on the road. Thomas Kampen, using a 1983 Party investigation report and participant memoirs, qualified that account: Mao was neither party chairman nor chairman of the military commission when the meeting ended. The dispute is over how much one meeting could have decided, not over whether it was held.

The distances and losses resist consolidation in the same way. Snow took 18,088 li from a First Army Corps itinerary and noted that the Reds themselves spoke of a “25,000-li March.” Mao said “more than twenty thousand li” through eleven provinces in December 1935. A Xinhua report published in 2026 used roughly 12,500 kilometres. The Party chronicle gives 86,000 at the start and just over 30,000 after the Xiang River. These are claims from different bodies using different measures, not a single measured series. The iron-chain bridge over the Tatu has two counts as well: Snow wrote of sixteen heavy chains, a poster catalogue of thirteen, with an advance unit of twenty-two men crossing on 29 May 1935 of whom fifteen survived. The second set of figures comes from a catalogue narrative built on later veteran interviews, not from a document of the crossing.

Xi’an: two governments’ accounts#

On 12 December 1936 the generals Zhang Xueliang and Yang Hucheng detained Chiang Kai-shek at Xi’an, demanding an end to the campaign against the Communists and a war against Japan. Four days later the American ambassador Nelson T. Johnson cabled Washington from Nanking. “Situation created by detention of General Chiang Kai Shek has been rendered exceedingly obscure by lack of any information from Sian as to purposes of Chang Hsueh Liang.” (Nelson T. Johnson, telegram to the Secretary of State, Nanking, 16 December 1936) Johnson set out two competing explanations, a mutiny over pay and policy or a communist-inspired seizure, and passed on the Nationalist government’s public line that Zhang’s action had come as a complete surprise. He noted that Zhang’s Northeastern troops in Shensi were known to be unhappy with the government’s Japan policy. Much of his information came from the Foreign Minister and, as he labelled it, a newspaper source. The telegram records what a government could not yet know, not what happened inside Xi’an.

Mao’s version was written two days after Chiang’s release. “Chiang should remember that he owes his safe departure from Sian to the mediation of the Communist Party, as well as to the efforts of Generals Chang and Yang, the leaders in the Sian Incident.” (Mao Tse-tung, “A Statement on Chiang Kai-shek’s Statement,” 28 December 1936) The statement lists six terms it says Chiang accepted and claims the Communist mediation was decisive. The political aim behind the claim had been stated in Pao-an three months earlier: “The fundamental point of unity which we insist upon is the national liberation anti-Japanese principle.” (Mao Tse-tung, interview answer, Pao-an, 23 September 1936, recorded and translated by Snow with Wu Liang-p’ing) A united front here means a temporary alliance in which each party keeps its own organisation and aims. Steve Tsang has challenged the Communist version. Working from Chiang’s diary at the Hoover Institution, T. V. Soong’s papers and Zhang Xueliang’s oral history, he argues that Chiang’s decisive calculation concerned possible Soviet support rather than any signed deal, and that the second united front followed the fighting in Shanghai in August 1937. The settlement remains a live argument about evidence, not a settled scene.

The march in memory#

The journey was being turned into a story while its survivors were still alive. A poster series of 1961 painted Ruijin, Zunyi, the Jinsha River, Luding Bridge, snow mountains, grasslands, Lazikou and northern Shensi, and put no leaders in the frame, Mao included. A twelve-sheet series of 2006 gave Mao a clear presence while stressing the collective character of the undertaking. The catalogue’s commentary connects the omission in 1961 to Mao’s weakened position after the Great Leap Forward. That is an interpretation offered by the catalogue, not something the posters alone establish.

Family memory survives at the other end of the road. In Yudu County, Jiangxi, Sun Guanfa, seventy-seven, described a father adopted as the son of a soldier who left from the riverbank and never came back. The local museum records that more than 17,000 Yudu residents joined the army and that 277 are recorded as returning, though no method for the figures is given. Zhong Min, a museum guide whose own family lost men in the encirclement campaigns and on the march, described the departure: local people made straw sandals overnight and took doors and roof beams for pontoon bridges. “We are not telling other people’s stories,” Zhong said. “We are telling our own families’.” (Zhong Min, museum guide in Yudu, interviewed by Xinhua, October 2026) Such memory tells us how the march is held in one county; it cannot tell us what the road was like.

What the evidence cannot hold#

Almost none of the English-language testimony comes from ordinary marchers directly. Snow wrote from interviews and handouts; Salisbury, Young and Sun Shuyun interviewed veterans decades later; the Xinhua pieces report their findings rather than reproducing the testimony. Two foreign eyewitness books from the 1930s, Alfred Bosshardt’s The Restraining Hand and Agnes Smedley’s China’s Red Army Marches, exist in scans that are not generally available, so no quotation from them appears here. The Zunyi documents are mostly Chinese-language collections known through translation. Chiang’s diary is at the Hoover Institution and Zhang’s oral history at Columbia. The Xi’an photographs are modern reproductions with thin provenance. Official chronicles are retrospective judgements produced for political purposes. We can establish what participants said in public, what journalists chose to record and how the event was pictured in 1961 and 2006. We cannot recover the private experience of the grasslands, the number who died at any river crossing, or the motives of Chiang and Mao in December 1936 with the confidence a full archive would allow.

How historians have read it#

  • Edgar Snow, Red Star Over China (1937; revised editions 1938, 1944). Reportage built on interviews; the source that created the subject. Its method, letting participants tell their own story, is also its limit, since Snow had no independent check on what he was told. Foundation for all later work, not a neutral account. Internet Archive: in.ernet.dli.2015.52426
  • Benjamin Yang, From Revolution to Politics: Chinese Communists on the Long March (Westview Press, 1990). Drawing on previously inaccessible Chinese materials, argues that Mao’s rise was gradual and that the Communists shifted from revolutionary idealists to political realists. Source: en.theorychina.org.cn
  • Thomas Kampen, “The Zunyi Conference and Further Steps in Mao’s Rise to Power,” The China Quarterly 117 (1989), 118–134. Uses the 1983 Party investigation report and participant memoirs to qualify Yang’s account of Zunyi, noting that Mao was neither party nor military commission chairman afterward. Cambridge University Press: 579DF7CF9A9D2A5DF6F9F3072EEBC84C
  • Harrison E. Salisbury, The Long March: The Untold Story (Harper & Row, 1985). Retraced 7,400 miles of the route and interviewed scores of ordinary veterans as well as leaders; the main English-language oral-history synthesis. Source: en.theorychina.org.cn
  • Helen Praeger Young, Choosing Revolution: Chinese Women Soldiers on the Long March (University of Illinois Press, 2001). Interviews with 22 women veterans, four followed in detail; shows how women’s experience diverges from the official, almost mythic version. Source: en.theorychina.org.cn
  • Steve Tsang, “Chiang Kai-shek’s ‘secret deal’ at Xian and the start of the Sino-Japanese War,” Humanities and Social Sciences Communications 1 (2015), 14003. Challenges the view that the kidnapping forced the united front, using Chiang’s diary, presidential papers and Soong’s diary. Source: nature.com

Broad agreement: the retreat happened in 1934–36; the Red Army survived with heavy losses; Zunyi in January 1935 brought Mao into the top military leadership; the Xi’an settlement ended the priority of suppressing the Communists first. Live disagreement: how decisive Zunyi was, whether the anti-Japan turn followed Stalin’s instruction or a domestic calculation, and what secured Chiang’s release. These are differences over evidence and method, not equally supported alternatives.
