Manchester 1945: The Congress That Changed the Language of Empire

In October 1945, in a town hall in Manchester, a Gold Coast railway worker stood up to describe the kind of politics the gathering wanted.

In October 1945, in a town hall in Manchester, a Gold Coast railway worker stood up to describe the kind of politics the gathering wanted. He was J. S. Annan, an officer of the Gold Coast Railway African Employees’ Union, and he told the congress that what it needed was “a man who wields tools, a man who knows no colour.” (J. S. Annan, Gold Coast Railway Civil Servants and Technical Works’ Union, 16 October 1945; 1947 proceedings). The Fifth Pan-African Congress had met in Manchester from 15 October to 21 October. A Pan-African congress is a meeting of organisations and individuals from Africa, the Americas and the Caribbean who hold that people of African descent share a common political cause. The first was held in London in 1900. This one is remembered for the famous men who attended, W. E. B. Du Bois, George Padmore, Kwame Nkrumah and Jomo Kenyatta, but the published proceedings show a meeting built around trade unions and colonial organisations, with figures who never became heads of state doing most of the speaking. Peter Abrahams, the congress’s publicity secretary, reported “some two hundred delegates holding mandates”; there is no independent roll against which to check that figure, and Abrahams dates the congress from 13 October while the session headings give 15 October.

The meeting grew out of another conference. In February 1945 colonial delegates to the World Trade Union Conference in London, the first international labour forum where colonial workers had been able to speak, were invited to Manchester. Padmore, who organised the congress, presided over its Standing Orders Committee and signed the preface to the proceedings, recorded a debate in which colonial delegates challenged a telegram to Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin that applied self-determination only to Europe. I. T. A. Wallace-Johnson, a Sierra Leonean trade unionist, supported an amendment that all peoples be allowed to choose their own form of government; the telegram was redrafted to include the Atlantic Charter principle. Wallace-Johnson put the stakes in terms that joined race and class: “Imperialism is for the Colonial workers as great a menace as Fascism is to the workers of the metropolitan countries of Europe.” (I. T. A. Wallace-Johnson, February 1945, quoted by Padmore in his foreword to The Voice of Coloured Labour; Marxists Internet Archive). That foreword is Padmore’s selection of speeches, not the conference minutes. Back in Manchester, Padmore, Peter Milliard, T. R. Makonnen, Kenyatta, Wallace-Johnson and others issued the call through the Pan-African Federation, a British organisation of Black groups, including an open letter to the prime minister, Clement Attlee, and a proposal for a “Colonial and Coloured Peoples’ Freedom Front”.

The sessions moved between life in Britain and conditions in the colonies. The opening session on 15 October dealt with the colour bar, the informal exclusion of Black people from jobs, housing and ordinary social life: the insecurity of Black seamen, children of American servicemen turned away by white homes, and discrimination in Liverpool, Cardiff and Hull. Milliard, president of the Pan-African Federation, called the congress “Dr Du Bois’s child”. The North and West Africa sessions gave the gathering its working-class character. Annan proposed a permanent London office and said his members would spend their last penny on it. Chief A. Soyemi Coker of the Nigerian Trades Union Congress asked for living wages, cooperatives and the nationalisation of transport and mines, and referred to a strike involving 500,000 people, a delegate’s claim rather than an audited total. Nkrumah, acting as rapporteur, set out political and economic trends and called imperialism a major cause of war. Kenyatta reported on six East African territories and described the kipande, a certificate African workers had to wear around their necks, linking low wages to the impossibility of building a standard of living that could sustain self-government. The session on South Africa listed land dispossession, poll and hut taxes, migrant labour contracts and pass restrictions. The record is awkward here: the proceedings say South African delegates could not obtain passports, yet list Marko Hlubi and Peter Abrahams under the African National Congress. The two statements should be read together, not smoothed over.

Women appear in the published volume, but briefly. Amy Ashwood Garvey, a Jamaican organiser, told the Caribbean session: “Very much has been written and spoken of the Negro, but for some reason very little has been said about the black woman.” (Amy Ashwood Garvey, Jamaica, 19 October 1945; 1947 proceedings). She pointed to the women teachers and postal workers who held Jamaican organising together. Alma La Badie spoke on children and water supply. These are among the few substantial interventions by women in the record, and their brevity reflects what the publication preserved as much as what was said. Du Bois, the congress’s International President, defended self-government against the colonial claim that Africans were not ready for it: “we are asserting that we must have self-government even if we make mistakes.” (W. E. B. Du Bois, 19 October 1945; same proceedings). Such a line answers a doctrine of tutelage, the idea that imperial rule was a temporary training period, in the simplest possible terms.

The declarations put the programme into words. “We demand for Black Africa autonomy and independence, so far and no further than it is possible in this “One World” for groups and peoples to rule themselves subject to inevitable world unity and federation.” (the congress, “The Challenge to the Colonial Powers”, October 1945; 1947 proceedings). The same text joined nationalism to an attack on private property: “We condemn the monopoly of capital and the rule of private wealth and industry for private profit alone. We welcome economic democracy as the only real democracy.” (same declaration; same URL). A second call to colonial workers named the weapons of struggle: “Your weapons-the Strike and the Boycott-are invincible.” (“Declaration to the Colonial Workers, Farmers and Intellectuals”, October 1945; 1947 proceedings). The resolutions were more concrete than the declarations: complete independence for West Africa, self-government for East Africa, ten democratic rights for South Africa, federation and adult suffrage for the West Indies, equal pay and the criminalisation of racial discrimination in Britain, and an end to South Africa’s incorporation of South-West Africa. A memorandum to the United Nations, presented by Du Bois as International President, asked for representatives of African colonial peoples to take part in UN business concerning them and listed the organisations that endorsed the demand. Nothing in the petition shows whether the UN acted.

What the congress caused is harder to say than what it adopted. C. L. R. James, speaking in 1973, argued that “it was from the Manchester Conference that Nkrumah, Jomo Kenyatta and various others went back to Africa and began the struggles for independence which ended as they did.” (C. L. R. James, retrospective speech, 20 November 1973; Marxists Internet Archive). James was in the United States in the 1940s and does not appear in the 1947 roll of delegates; he was an advocate for Padmore’s reputation. In the same speech he quotes a letter from the former Labour colonial minister Creech-Jones denying that the congress shaped Labour policy, and rejects it. Both men had an interest in the answer, and the documents assembled here do not settle the causal claim. The record closes with a programme and a committee structure, not with a transfer of power.

How historians have read it#

  • Hakim Adi and Marika Sherwood, The 1945 Manchester Pan-African Congress Revisited: with, Colonial and Coloured Unity, edited by George Padmore (1995). Reissues Padmore’s proceedings with an assessment of the congress’s importance; the citation is verified in the Marxists Internet Archive volume index (Marxists Internet Archive: index). This is the starting point for any chapter and the natural counterweight to Padmore’s own editing.
  • George Padmore, Pan-Africanism or Communism? The Coming Struggle for Africa (1956). The organiser’s book-length retrospective; listed in the Marxists Internet Archive Padmore index (Marxists Internet Archive: index). It should be read against Adi and Sherwood rather than as a neutral account.
  • Immanuel Geiss, The Pan-African Movement (1974). Cited by Varela (2017) for the view that the declarations divided the movement into pre- and post-1945 eras and that the declaration to the colonial powers was a compromise between radicals and Du Bois. Broadly shared by other surveys; the exact pages are in Varela’s footnotes.
  • Colin Legum, Pan-Africanism: A Short Political Guide (1962). Cited by Varela (2017) for “Colonial and subject peoples of the world, unite!” as the congress’s internationalist closing. A contemporary political survey rather than an archival study.
  • E. U. Esedebe, Pan-Africanism: The Idea and the Movement, 1776–1991 (1982; later editions). Cited by Varela (2017) alongside Geiss for the congress’s representative character. Useful as a broad survey where the specialist studies disagree.
  • Chris Varela, “Africa Finds its Voice in the Halls of Manchester”, History in the Making 10 (2017). An undergraduate survey that gathers the standard citations and argues the congress has been overlooked; its abstract is available online (Source: scholarworks.lib.csusb.edu). Weight it below the monographs above.

There is broad agreement that the 1945 congress marked a shift from petitioning to mass-based anticolonial politics and that its delegates included future national leaders. Disagreement continues over how much the congress itself caused later decolonisation and how radical its socialist language was in practice.

Limits of the record#

The surviving published record is a 1947 movement pamphlet edited by the congress’s chief organiser, transcribed for the web by a volunteer decades later. It is not a neutral minute book, and the original stenographer’s notes are not among the sources used here. No contemporary newspaper report, British, African or African-American, is included, nor is any colonial-office or police reaction. French-speaking territories were absent, South African delegates were reported unable to obtain passports, and the voices of rural women and peasants reach us only through delegates speaking about them. The volume is elite and male in its voice. The statistics in the speeches, on mine wages, school spending and strike numbers, are advocacy figures and should not be reused as established facts. The phrase “Workers of the World” cannot be matched to a distinct congress document among the surviving proceedings; the nearest verified first-hand material is the trade-union material from 1945 and the closing call for “Colonial and Subject Peoples of the World-Unite!” in the declaration to colonial workers. Whatever happened at Accra in 1958 belongs to a separate body of sources, already cited in the chapter on Nkrumah and Lumumba, and is not established by this record.
