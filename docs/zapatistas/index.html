The Zapatistas: Indigenous Rebellion and Another Politics

On 1 January 1994, the day the North American Free Trade Agreement took effect, indigenous rebels in the southern Mexican state of Chiapas seized six towns and declared war on the federal army. The document they released that morning was the First Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle, dated 1993 and issued in the name of the General Command of the EZLN, the Ejército Zapatista de Liberación Nacional, or Zapatista Army of National Liberation, named after the revolutionary Emiliano Zapata. It invoked Article 39 of the Mexican Constitution, the people’s right to alter their government, and it ended with a claim of ancestry: “Pero nosotros HOY DECIMOS ¡BASTA!, somos los herederos de los verdaderos forjadores de nuestra nacionalidad” (EZLN General Command, First Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle, 1993, released 1 January 1994). But today we say enough, the declaration continued in this English rendering, we are the heirs of the true builders of our nationality. The uprising was the first major armed rebellion against a neoliberal state in Latin America after the collapse of governments calling themselves socialist in Europe, and it refused the model those governments had followed. The EZLN did not set out to capture the state. It insisted on mandar obedeciendo, leading by obeying, and built autonomous municipalities in the years that followed.

Eleven demands and a twelve-day war#

The First Declaration was a list of demands as well as a declaration of war. It set out eleven of them in a single line: “apoyando este plan del pueblo mexicano que lucha por trabajo, tierra, techo, alimentación, salud, educación, independencia, libertad, democracia, justicia y paz” (First Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle). Work, land, housing, food, health, education, independence, liberty, democracy, justice and peace. The text named the PRI, the Partido Revolucionario Institucional or Institutional Revolutionary Party, which had governed Mexico since 1929, and President Carlos Salinas de Gortari as its enemy. It claimed the heritage of five hundred years of indigenous and Mexican struggle and set out six military orders. After twelve days of fighting, mass demonstrations in Mexico City helped bring about a ceasefire. On 10 June 1994 the EZLN extended that ceasefire unilaterally, said its base communities had rejected the government’s peace proposals, and explained why it would not lay down its arms: “Chiapas no tendrá solución real si no se soluciona México.” (Second Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle, 10 June 1994). Chiapas would have no real solution without a solution for Mexico. The declaration opened with a 1914 speech attributed to Zapata at the Aguascalientes convention, which is where the movement later built a gathering place of that name. The claim to have consulted the base communities is the movement’s own account of its procedure, with no independent record attached.

Another politics#

In August 1994 the EZLN built a convention centre in the Chiapas jungle and called it Aguascalientes, after the revolutionary convention of 1914. Comandante Tacho, speaking for the clandestine indigenous leadership, formally handed it over to the National Democratic Convention at 8.20 in the morning of 3 August: “A partir de hoy, Aguascalientes ya no es del EZLN, ahora es de su propiedad de la Convención Nacional Democrática.” (Comandante Tacho, speech at Aguascalientes, 3 August 1994). From today, he said, Aguascalientes was no longer the EZLN’s but belonged to the Convention. The handover was a political act: transferring a site to civil society does not by itself establish who controlled it in practice. It was also a statement of what the movement meant by another politics. Subcomandante Marcos, its best-known spokesperson, wrote in an open letter in 1996 that the Zapatistas were not trying to take power. “Nosotros no luchamos por tomar el poder; luchamos por democracia, libertad y justicia.” (Marcos, “Lo que nos hace diferentes es nuestra propuesta política,” 30 August 1996). We do not fight to take power, he wrote, we fight for democracy, liberty and justice. Mandar obedeciendo meant that the indigenous commanders held authority but exercised it by following the decisions of their communities, a claim made concrete in 2003 when the movement created local Good Government Juntas and later the meeting places known as caracoles, snail shells.

The movement’s communiqués were central to this politics, and Marcos described how they were made. In a preface written in June 1994 he said that some originated when members of the indigenous clandestine committee decided to speak and instructed him to draft, while others he proposed himself; the committee revised, amended and could reject his drafts. This is his own account, written for publishers who wanted an introduction to a collection of his texts, and it answers one side of the argument about how much he wrote and how much the committee decided. It should be reported as his claim rather than treated as settled.

The women’s law and the women who made it#

Weeks before the uprising, in December 1993, the EZLN published a Revolutionary Law of Women. Its ten articles gave women the right to take part at the rank their ability determined, to work for a fair wage, to decide how many children they would have, to hold elected community office, to primary health care and food, to education, to choose a partner, to be free from physical abuse and rape, to hold leadership and military rank, and to the full rights of revolutionary law. “Ninguna mujer podrá ser golpeada o maltratada físicamente ni por familiares ni por extraños.” (EZLN, Revolutionary Law of Women, December 1993). No woman, it says, may be beaten or physically abused by family members or strangers. A law states an intention, not a condition already achieved, and the text does not record the consultation that produced it. It also leaves out the right of women to own land, a gap the commanders themselves later acknowledged.

In March 1994, inside the cathedral in San Cristóbal de las Casas during the peace dialogues, Comandante Ramona and Major Ana María told two interviewers how they had joined and how women had sustained the uprising. Ramona, a Tzotzil speaker, explained that she had left her community because there was no work there. “I left my community out of necessity in order to find work; there was no longer any way to earn a living there.” (Comandante Ramona and Major Ana María, interview by Matilde Perez U. and Laura Castellanos, Doble Jornada, 7 March 1994; translated from Tzotzil to Spanish by Comandante Javier and from Spanish to English by Judith and Tim Richards). Ana María said that women had been among those who opened the prison gates on 1 January, and she described a limit on what could be said about women’s bodies: “It did not occur to them … the fact is that there is a belief among the indigenous people that there should not be abortion.” (Ramona and Ana María interview). Both said the Women’s Law arose after women protested that they had been given a voice in assemblies but no law of their own. The interview was conducted with a EZLN commander translating and with journalists whose questions sometimes led the witnesses, and the English is a double translation from Tzotzil through Spanish. Tzotzil is one of the Mayan languages spoken in Chiapas.

On 28 March 2001, at the end of the March of the Colour of the Earth, Comandanta Esther addressed the Mexican Congress in the Palacio Legislativo de San Lázaro. “SOY INDÍGENA Y SOY MUJER, Y ESO ES LO ÚNICO QUE IMPORTA AHORA.” (Comandanta Esther, address to the Congress of the Union, 28 March 2001). I am indigenous and I am a woman, and that is the only thing that matters now. She pointed out that Marcos was a subcommander while the indigenous commanders mandamos obedeciendo to their peoples, and she described the absence of water, electricity, schools, clinics and land, along with forced marriage, illiteracy and domestic abuse. This is a formal speech published by the movement and preserved as a transcript. It records what Esther said to the legislature, and it is an argument for a particular indigenous-rights bill, not an independent survey of conditions.

A globalization of rebellion#

The Sixth Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle, issued in June 2005, reviewed twelve years of war and failed negotiation. It described the transfer of civil power to elected local authorities and the Good Government Juntas, and it launched the Other Campaign, a non-electoral, anti-capitalist effort to listen to ordinary Mexicans and build a national programme of struggle. It placed the Zapatistas inside a wider field: “as there is a neoliberal globalization, there is a globalization of rebellion” (Sixth Declaration of the Lacandon Jungle, June 2005; translated by irlandesa). The declaration addressed movements in Latin America, Europe, Africa, Asia and Oceania. It also made claims that need independent measures: that living conditions had improved in Zapatista territory, and that President Zedillo ordered the 1997 killing of forty-five people at Acteal. No survivor testimony from Acteal appears in the material used here.

The Other Campaign reached people beyond Chiapas. At Chablekal in Yucatán in January 2006, an indigenous participant named Carmelo Aké told a plenary session: “We don’t want any more of this, I hope they realize that we are not animals, we are indigenous, we also have the blood of Christ.” (Carmelo Aké, speaking at Chablekal, reported by Rafael Gómez Chi, 19 January 2006; translator not named on the page). The report is a sympathetic journalist’s rendering of a single day in one state, not a sample of the whole campaign. The global dimension is documented chiefly by the movement and its supporters. A guide produced by the Chiapas95 solidarity editors records that at the 1996 Encounter in Chiapas “over 3,000 grassroots activists and intellectuals from 42 countries on 5 continents came together” (Acción Zapatista / Chiapas95, Zapatistas in Cyberspace). That figure comes from a participants’ guide last updated in 2003 and is given without a source list, so it should be attributed to the movement rather than treated as an independent count.

How historians have read it#

  • Neil Harvey, The Chiapas Rebellion: The Struggle for Land and Democracy (Duke University Press, 1998). Source: openlibrary.org. Treats the uprising as the product of long-running agrarian and indigenous struggles over land and local power rather than as a sudden event or a purely cultural revolt. The broad scholarly baseline for the other works here.
  • George A. Collier, with Elizabeth Lowery Quaratiello, Basta! Land and the Zapatista Rebellion in Chiapas (Food First Books, 1994; revised 1999). Source: openlibrary.org. An anthropologist’s account built on fieldwork and campesino testimony, stressing land hunger and the structure of the Chiapas countryside; agrees with Harvey on material causes while giving more weight to local voices.
  • June C. Nash, The Explosion of Communities (1995). Source: openlibrary.org. An anthropological study of the rebellion and its effects on indigenous communities, useful as a near-contemporary counterweight to the movement’s own communiqués.
  • Tom Hayden, ed., The Zapatista Reader (Nation Books/Thunder’s Mouth Press, 2002). Source: openlibrary.org. A documentary anthology of EZLN texts and commentary; convenient for English translations, but its selection and framing are editorial and should not replace the originals cited above.
  • John Holloway, Change the World Without Taking Power: The Meaning of Revolution Today (Pluto Press, 2002). Source: openlibrary.org. Reads the Zapatistas as an example of revolution understood as the making of autonomous spaces rather than the capture of the state. This is a sympathetic interpretation, not a scholarly consensus, and should be set beside Harvey’s materialist account rather than adopted in place of it.

Broad agreement exists on the importance of land, indigenous dispossession and the one-party PRI regime in producing the rebellion. Live disagreement concerns how far the Zapatistas represent a new kind of politics and how far their autonomy project actually changed power relations in Chiapas.

Limits of the record#

No Tzotzil, Tzeltal or other Mayan-language source was read directly, and no physical archive was visited. The surviving English-language record is heavily filtered: most documents were written in Spanish by the EZLN leadership, then translated by movement sympathisers, often without a named translator. The named voices, Marcos, Tacho, Ramona, Ana María and Esther, are commanders and members of the clandestine committee. Ordinary support-base villagers, people displaced by the fighting, and opponents of the EZLN in Chiapas are largely absent; the single exception is Carmelo Aké in the Chablekal report, and even that is a journalist’s rendering of a few sentences. Three gaps stand out. No survivor testimony about Acteal was located, so the only account here is the EZLN’s in the Sixth Declaration. The 1994 to 1995 period is documented mainly by the movement’s own communiqués, with no contemporary Mexican government record, Red Cross report or independent newspaper file consulted. And the global solidarity claim rests partly on a participants’ guide last updated in 2003, with no independent attendance record for the 1996 Encounter checked. Corroboration would have to come from the Fray Bartolomé de las Casas Human Rights Center archives and Mexican press files, or the claims must be attributed explicitly to the movement. A movement’s account of its own successes is not proof. The Sixth Declaration’s claim that only Zapatista communities improved their living conditions needs an independent measure before it can enter the narrative as fact.
