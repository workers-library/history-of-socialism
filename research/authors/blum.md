# William Blum (1933–2018) — research dossier

This dossier records what could be established about William Blum from sources opened during this research. It follows `research/AUTHOR-PROFILES-STANDARD.md` and `research/MASTER-STANDARD.md`. Every quotation below was read in the page or document cited; no quotation, date, page number or URL has been reconstructed from memory. Where a publisher page was returned only in search results and blocked a direct fetch, that is stated at the end. No Wikipedia, Goodreads, Scribd or Google Books material was used as evidence.

## Biography

William Henry Blum was born on 6 March 1933 in Brooklyn and died on 9 December 2018 in Virginia, aged 85. The birth and death dates come from the Library of Congress name authority record as surfaced in search results; the death date is confirmed on his own website, which posted a farewell saying "Bill died on December 9th, 2018" and reproducing Louis Wolf's account of a fall in his apartment on 4 October 2018, a 65-day fight for life, and kidney failure as the immediate cause ([Primary Official — LC authority record, content seen in search results, direct fetch returned 403](http://id.loc.gov/authorities/n86117568); [Primary Theoretical — Blum site, farewell](https://williamblum.org/farewell)). I did not open a birth certificate, and details given in obituaries that I did not open, such as hospital and parentage, were not independently verified here.

His own account of his political development is brief and consistent. He worked with computers, including for IBM and then for the State Department, and left the State Department in 1967 because he opposed what the United States was doing in Vietnam. He abandoned an ambition to become a Foreign Service officer, then helped found and edit the *Washington Free Press*, described on his site as the first alternative newspaper in the capital. He worked as a freelance journalist in the United States, Europe and South America. A period in Chile in 1972–73, writing about the Allende government's "socialist experiment" and what he calls its "tragic overthrow in a CIA-designed coup", made intervention a permanent subject for him. In the mid-1970s he worked in London with the former CIA officer Philip Agee and Agee's associates on projects exposing CIA personnel and operations ([Primary Theoretical — About William Blum](https://williamblum.org/about)).

The memoir *West-Bloc Dissident* fills in the social history of that period from the inside. Its publisher description says Blum spent four years at IBM and more than two at the State Department before the Vietnam War and the 1960s moved him into anti-war and counter-cultural politics, and that it recounts the underground press, government informers, his life in Chile under Allende, and an unsuccessful Hollywood documentary project with Oliver Stone. The book's opening line, quoted on the author's page, places the reader at CIA headquarters in Langley on 4 August 1969, where he began recording employees' licence plates ([Primary Theoretical — West-Bloc Dissident book page](https://williamblum.org/books/west-bloc-dissident)). This is his retrospective account, not an independent record of the events.

From 2002 to 2003 he wrote a regular column for *The Ecologist*. From 2003 until shortly before his death he published the free monthly newsletter *The Anti-Empire Report*, of which 160 issues are listed on his site, the last dated 20 September 2018. He also gave talks and radio and television interviews, discussed in the section on lectures below ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about); [Primary Theoretical — Anti-Empire Report #160](https://williamblum.org/aer/read/160)).

## Intellectual tradition

Blum wrote from the tradition of anti-imperialist journalism rather than from academic history or party theory. He called himself a "historian" on his own site, but his training was in accounting and his method was that of an investigative reporter assembling a documentary record against official denials. In an interview he said plainly, "I'm a socialist", while adding that an American form of socialism would not arrive in his lifetime or his son's; for the foreseeable future he thought the realistic demand was the "highly increased democracy" associated with Ralph Nader ([Primary Theoretical — David Ross interview, *The Bloody Road to Empire*](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html)). He disowned both patriotism and nationalism as attachments to a flag rather than to principles: "I don't love any country. I love certain principles, like human rights and civil liberties and the society which puts people before profits and society which practices genuine democracy" ([Primary Theoretical — Truthout interview, 12 March 2014](https://truthout.org/articles/william-blum-discusses-americas-deadliest-export-democracy)).

He rejected the label "conspiracy theorist" as a dismissive device, while insisting that real conspiracies existed. In the Truthout interview his distinction was procedural: "I draw the line where it makes sense, and when I cannot present evidence, I stop." He complained that some of his readers were more conspiracy-minded than he was and saw 9/11 or the "Israeli lobby" behind everything ([Primary Theoretical — Truthout interview](https://truthout.org/articles/william-blum-discusses-americas-deadliest-export-democracy)). That position should be read alongside his claim in the *Killing Hope* introduction that denial of what he called the "American holocaust" was comparable to denial of the Nazi Holocaust. That comparison is his rhetoric, not a settled historical finding, and it is one of the features of his work that academic reviewers have found hardest to accept ([Primary Theoretical — Killing Hope introduction](https://williamblum.org/chapters/killing-hope/introduction); [Scholarly Secondary — Muravska, LSE Review of Books, 2015](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2015/01/21/book-review-killing-hope-us-military-and-cia-interventions-since-world-war-ii-by-william-blum/)).

His intellectual affinities lay with Noam Chomsky and Edward Herman's analysis of media and propaganda, Michael Parenti's writing on imperialism, Gabriel Kolko's historical work, and the dissident intelligence officers Philip Agee and John Stockwell. Adam Jones, reviewing *Rogue State* in *Millennium: Journal of International Studies*, placed Blum explicitly in that company, saying he was covering "ground well-trodden by Noam Chomsky, Edward Herman, Gabriel Kolko, Michael Parenti, and legions of independent journalists and activists" ([Scholarly Secondary — Jones, *Millennium*, 2002, hosted on the reviewer's own site](http://adamjones.freeservers.com/blum.htm)). Blum was a "West-Bloc dissident" in the literal sense of the memoir's title: an American who used documents released by his own government against that government's public account of itself.

## Major contributions

Blum's main contribution was compilation. *Killing Hope* assembled country-by-country accounts of United States military and CIA activity from China in the 1940s to the American empire after the Cold War. The author's site lists 56 chapters and three appendices: a note on how money circulates, a list of instances of United States armed forces abroad from 1798 to 1945, and a list of United States government assassination plots. *Rogue State* reorganised the same material thematically and added a "concise history of United States global interventions, 1945 to the present". On his site he described the earlier book as a "forensic overview" and the later one as a "mini-encyclopedia" ([Primary Theoretical — Killing Hope book page](https://williamblum.org/books/killing-hope); [Primary Theoretical — Rogue State book page](https://williamblum.org/books/rogue-state)).

His second contribution was a definition of "intervention" broad enough to cover more than invasions. In the David Ross interview he said he confined himself to "serious interventions": attempts to overthrow governments, whether successful or not; suppression of popular revolutions or movements against very repressive governments; assassinations; serious interference in foreign elections; large-scale manipulation of the media; and subversion of labour unions. He claimed "well over a hundred separate and serious interventions" in "maybe seventy or eighty countries", even though the book had 54 chapters, because some chapters covered several cases ([Primary Theoretical — David Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html)). The short list on his site, "Overthrowing other people's governments: The Master List", applies the same definition to 57 dated entries from China in 1949 to Syria in 2012, with an asterisk marking a "successful ouster" and no asterisk marking an attempt. A companion page, "Suppressing revolt and revolution", lists 20 further cases ([Primary Theoretical — Master List](https://williamblum.org/essays/read/overthrowing-other-peoples-governments-the-master-list); [Primary Theoretical — Suppressing revolt and revolution](https://williamblum.org/essays/read/suppressing-revolt-and-revolution)).

**Documented interventions and political interpretation.** The list is not the same thing as the evidence for it. Two examples show where the line falls. In Iran, the declassified record is explicit. A CIA memorandum of 8 March 1954, "CAMPAIGN TO INSTALL PRO-WESTERN GOVERNMENT IN IRAN", states that the objective was, "Through legal, or quasi-legal, methods to effect the fall of the Mossadeq government", describes the propaganda campaign, the "war of nerves", and the covertly developed military apparatus, records that "The ouster of Mossadeq was successfully accomplished on 19 Aug 1953", and gives a grand total of $5,330,000 ([Primary Official — FRUS 1952–1954, Iran, document 363](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951-54Iran/d363)). In Guatemala, a CIA position paper of 24 April 1954 on PBSUCCESS lists the psychological, military and paramilitary assets available against the Arbenz government, discusses "plausible denial", and concludes that "The best chance of removing the Arbenz regime is to proceed as energetically as possible along the lines of the present plan" ([Primary Official — FRUS 1952–1954, Guatemala, document 133](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1952-54Guat/d133)). Those are documented operations. Blum's chapter titles — Iran as "Making it safe for the King of Kings", Guatemala's later period as "A less publicized 'final solution'" — and his claim that the purpose was to prevent a successful alternative to capitalism are his interpretation of why the operations happened ([Primary Theoretical — Killing Hope table of contents](https://williamblum.org/books/killing-hope); [Primary Theoretical — David Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html)). The interpretation is arguable; the operations are not in doubt. Other entries on the Master List, particularly "attempts" such as East Germany in the 1950s, France in 1965 or Fiji in 1987, are presented on that page without any supporting citation, and I did not verify them against official records here.

## Analysis of capitalism

Blum's account of capitalism is an account of United States foreign policy as its enforcement arm. In the *Rogue State* introduction he reduced the "engine of American foreign policy" to three imperatives: "the care and feeding of American corporations", the prevention of "the rise of any society that might serve as a successful example of an alternative to the capitalist model", and the expansion of American political, economic and military hegemony. He argued that when moral language coincides with those ends, officials "walk the high moral ground", but that it rarely does ([Primary Theoretical — Rogue State introduction](https://williamblum.org/chapters/rogue-state/rogue-state-introduction)). The essay "What do the Imperial Mafia really want?" breaks the same claim into motives for the 2003 invasion of Iraq: strategic expansion, ideological missions, oil, privatisation through the IMF, World Bank and WTO, profits for arms manufacturers, and the interests of supporters of Israel. These are listed as causes "make the hearts of the imperial mafia beat faster", not as a formal model of capitalist economics ([Primary Theoretical — What do the Imperial Mafia really want?](https://williamblum.org/essays/read/what-do-the-imperial-mafia-really-want)).

Domestically he saw the same class interest operating through institutions. He told David Ross that the personnel filters of the State Department, Pentagon and newspapers ensured that people who questioned foreign-policy orthodoxy did not reach positions of authority, and that newspapers owned by multinational corporations had an interest in fixed outcomes. In the Truthout interview he described the two parties as one "on the out" and one "on the in", so that their apparent differences were effects of office rather than of principle ([Primary Theoretical — David Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html); [Primary Theoretical — Truthout interview](https://truthout.org/articles/william-blum-discusses-americas-deadliest-export-democracy)). The table of contents of *America's Deadliest Export* includes a chapter titled "The Problem with Capitalism"; I did not open that chapter, so the account above rests on his introduction, essays and interviews, not on a reading of the chapter itself ([Primary Theoretical — America's Deadliest Export book page](https://williamblum.org/books/americas-deadliest-export)).

## Analysis of socialism

Blum wrote no institutional blueprint for socialism. His defence of socialism was negative and historical: he argued that no socialist experiment had been allowed to develop on its own merits. "Virtually every socialist experiment of any significance in the twentieth century", he wrote in a 1997 essay, had been "overthrown, invaded, or bombed … corrupted, perverted, or subverted … sanctioned, embargoed, or destabilized … or otherwise had life made impossible for it, by the United States" ([Primary Theoretical — Will humans ever fly?](https://williamblum.org/essays/read/will-humans-ever-fly-smashing-socialism-in-the-20th-century)). The same essay used the Wright brothers analogy: if sabotage had grounded every early flight, people would have concluded that humans could never fly. He pointed to socialist and former communist parties winning elections across Eastern Europe and elsewhere in the 1990s as evidence that socialism had not died of its own failure.

In the David Ross interview he went further than many Western leftists in rejecting Chomsky's view that the Soviet Union had ceased to be socialist. Blum's position was that "there hasn't been a real good example because the U.S. government and some of its allies have made sure there hasn't"; every significant attempt, including the Soviet Union and Cuba, had been subjected to invasion, destabilisation or hostile isolation ([Primary Theoretical — David Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html)). This makes him a defender of actually existing socialist experiments against external destruction rather than a theorist of their internal organisation. When asked what alternative model activists should aim for, he answered in stages: he personally wanted an American socialism, but thought increased democracy was the achievable task in the near future ([Primary Theoretical — David Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html)). No detailed economic programme for such a socialism appears in the sources opened here.

## Intellectual influences

Influence is claimed here only where Blum quotes, cites or praises another writer. The *Killing Hope* introduction opens with a line from Michael Parenti's *The Anti-Communist Impulse* and cites Parenti twice in its notes; it also cites the Cold War historians D. F. Fleming and Frederick Lewis Schuman, quotes Kwame Nkrumah's *Dark Days in Ghana*, draws on John Stockwell's *In Search of Enemies*, and cites Frank Kofsky, Walden Bello, Doug Henwood and *Multinational Monitor* ([Primary Theoretical — Killing Hope introduction](https://williamblum.org/chapters/killing-hope/introduction)). The same notes identify the *Declassified Documents Reference System* and *Foreign Relations of the United States* as sources he used, describing FRUS as "internal documents published about 25 to 35 years after the fact" ([Primary Theoretical — Killing Hope introduction](https://williamblum.org/chapters/killing-hope/introduction)).

Noam Chomsky appears both as a quoted source and as a personal point of agreement. Blum's site reproduces Chomsky's judgement that *Killing Hope* was "far and away the best book on the topic"; in the Ross interview Blum said that Chomsky's idea of the "threat of a good example" matched his own reading of Vietnam, Cuba and Chile ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about); [Primary Theoretical — Ross interview](http://www.thirdworldtraveler.com/Blum/Bloody_Road_Empire.html)). Philip Agee and John Stockwell mattered in two ways: as collaborators and sources of inside information about CIA practice, and as authors on whom the CIA reviewer says Blum relied heavily ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about); [Primary Official — CIA review](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Intel-Officers-Bookshelf-59.3.pdf)). Michael Parenti's influence is visible again in *Rogue State*, where Blum quotes his argument that public bewilderment at foreign policy does not mean policymakers are themselves fools ([Primary Theoretical — Rogue State introduction](https://williamblum.org/chapters/rogue-state/rogue-state-introduction)).

## Influence and legacy

Blum's books reached readers well outside academia. His site says they were translated into more than 15 languages, and its pages list editions in Swedish, French, Italian, Korean, Arabic, Spanish, German, Russian, Iranian, Chinese, Finnish, Greek, Dutch, Polish, Japanese, Turkish, Malayalam, Bulgarian and Portuguese translations for individual titles ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about); [Primary Theoretical — Killing Hope book page](https://williamblum.org/books/killing-hope); [Primary Theoretical — Rogue State book page](https://williamblum.org/books/rogue-state)). In 1999 he received a Project Censored award for "exemplary journalism" for an article on United States assistance to Iraq's chemical and biological weapons capability, according to his own site ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about)).

In January 2006 an audiotape attributed to Osama bin Laden said it "would be useful" for Americans to read *Rogue State*. Blum's site records this and notes the surge of attention that followed; interviewers later returned to it repeatedly, and the *New York Times* obituary headline led with it ([Primary Theoretical — About](https://williamblum.org/about); [Primary Theoretical — Truthout interview](https://truthout.org/articles/william-blum-discusses-americas-deadliest-export-democracy)). Blum told Truthout that the attention brought interviews with outlets that otherwise ignored him, including Tucker Carlson, and he remained careful to say that he deplored the 11 September attacks ([Primary Theoretical — Truthout interview](https://truthout.org/articles/william-blum-discusses-americas-deadliest-export-democracy); the reporting of the bin Laden quote also appeared in contemporary journalism, opened only as search-result text and not used here as evidence).

His "Master List" has had a second life after his death, circulating on activist and alternative sites as a compact reference for regime change, including a republication in 2019 that added Ukraine 2014 in an editorial note ([endpoint seen in search results, not opened — Global Research, 13 May 2019](https://www.globalresearch.ca/overthrowing-other-peoples-governments-the-master-list-of-u-s-regime-changes/5400829)). *Killing Hope* and *Rogue State* were reissued by Bloomsbury Academic in 2022, which is a measure of continuing demand, though I could not open those publisher pages directly ([Primary Official — publisher pages, content seen in search results, direct fetch returned 403](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/killing-hope-9781350348196)). Julia Muravska's 2015 review observed that after the 1995 revision the books largely passed under the radar of mainstream academia while remaining popular on the left and among what she called "conspiracy theorists and fringe commentators" ([Scholarly Secondary — Muravska](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2015/01/21/book-review-killing-hope-us-military-and-cia-interventions-since-world-war-ii-by-william-blum/)). That is a useful description of his reach, not a verdict on the evidence.

## Criticisms and controversies

The most specific factual objection I found comes from the CIA's own professional journal. Hayden Peake, reviewing the 2014 edition of *Killing Hope* in *Studies in Intelligence* 59(3) of September 2015, recorded that the 1986 first edition contained 49 cases and the revised edition 56, and said the operations discussed were "all well-known covert actions, viewed from the left". He acknowledged that Blum's judgements were open to debate but identified one flat error: "in the case of Operation Splinter Factor and the defection of Polish intelligence officer Jozef Swiatło, he is just wrong: there never was an Operation Splinter Factor." Peake also noted that the book is "documented with sources that support Blum's position, including several books by former CIA officers John Stockwell and Philip Agee" — a criticism of source selection rather than of citation practice. The journal's standard disclaimer says the views are the reviewer's own and not official United States government positions ([Primary Official — CIA, *Studies in Intelligence* 59(3), 2015, PDF opened and text extracted](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/Intel-Officers-Bookshelf-59.3.pdf)).

Julia Muravska's review in *LSE Review of Books* (21 January 2015) accepted that the cases were "heavily and meticulously footnoted", drawing on contemporary media, declassified archives and, at page 423, field research that produced "personal experience" and "observations". Her objection was to analysis. She described the post-Cold War chapter as reading "more like a series of hastily penned notes than academic discourse or historical narrative", containing "unsubstantiated claims and colloquialisms" with little transition or argument. She criticised what she saw as a patronising account of post-communist Eastern Europeans, an unquestioning treatment of NATO bombing in the Balkans, reliance on the Russian state-funded channel RT for the Ukraine crisis, and a failure to place the Cold War narrative in the context of superpower rivalry. Her conclusion was that "academic readers may not be satisfied" by the analysis, and she recommended William Keylor's *A World of Nations* and Robert Litwak's *Regime Change* as more rigorous alternatives; I did not open those books ([Scholarly Secondary — Muravska](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/lsereviewofbooks/2015/01/21/book-review-killing-hope-us-military-and-cia-interventions-since-world-war-ii-by-william-blum/)).

Adam Jones's review of *Rogue State* in *Millennium: Journal of International Studies* (2002) made a related methodological point from a sympathetic position. He praised the "cumulative power" of the documentation, called the book "a useful reference work" and "an informative if polemical read", and quoted Blum's argument about a shared "class consciousness" between Washington and its unsavoury allies as "to-the-point and convincing". But he found the didactic structure at times "bordering on a clip-job" and wrote that Blum showed a "relentless desire to interpret every action of the U.S. government in the worst possible light", which sometimes produced "leaden prose". His example of overreach was a chapter that included instances of "political correctness" alongside torture and drug trafficking: "Blum tosses everything but the kitchen sink into his critique" ([Scholarly Secondary — Jones](http://adamjones.freeservers.com/blum.htm)).

Two further controversies are recorded rather than adjudicated. First, Blum's comparison of United States interventions to the Holocaust, made in the 1995 introduction and in the Ross interview, is a moral analogy that he used to argue American crimes are systematically denied; it is not supported here by a demographic comparison, and I found no opened scholarly study that accepts it. Second, the concise intervention lists on his website are themselves a source of interpretive risk. Chapter 17 of *Rogue State* calls itself "the most extensive compilation ever of serious post-World War II American interventions", says it covers many more cases than *Killing Hope*, and directs readers elsewhere "for sources not indicated below" ([Primary Theoretical — *Rogue State* 2002 text, PDF opened and extracted](https://www.cia.gov/library/abbottabad-compound/F5/F596A5C4611298BA662A56CDB4548E35_Blum_William_Rogue_State.pdf)). That is an explicit admission that the short entries are not individually sourced on the page. The same is true of the Master List. A reader using Blum as a source must therefore separate a documented act — the coup in Iran, the PBSUCCESS plan in Guatemala — from an entry in a list that asserts an attempt without citation, and from the political claim that all such acts served one imperial purpose.

## Related thinkers

The nearest connections are to Noam Chomsky and Edward Herman, whose propaganda critique Blum adopted and whose work he praised; to Michael Parenti, whom he quoted and whose account of American imperialism he shared; and to Philip Agee and John Stockwell, former CIA officers whose disclosures he used and, in Agee's case, with whom he worked in London. Gabriel Kolko is named by Adam Jones as another writer covering the same ground. Kwame Nkrumah appears in Blum's introduction as a source on non-alignment, and Ralph Nader as the figure whose "highly increased democracy" Blum proposed as an achievable near-term goal. These are connections of agreement and influence, not a shared organisation or school. No published profiles of these thinkers exist yet in this repository's `research/authors/` directory, so no internal links are available.
