## Biography

Verso, Davis's main publisher, records his dates as 1946–2022 and describes him as a writer, political activist, urban theorist and historian, best known for investigations of power and class in *City of Quartz*, *Late Victorian Holocausts* and *Planet of Slums* [S1]. Its obituary, written by Tariq Ali and published on 26 October 2022, calls him an American working-class intellectual who was radicalised in the 1960s by the US Communist Party, and says he never ceased to acknowledge the people in the Party who educated him [S2]. The same obituary says he taught classes at UC Irvine, worked with Michael Sprinker on a US list at Verso and the *American Socialist Yearbook* published between 1985 and 1988, and remained intellectually active to the end, commenting on articles submitted to *New Left Review* [S2]. A later Verso podcast account adds that he came to writing late, after periods as a truck driver and a meat cutter, and that the civil rights movement was part of his radicalisation [S16]. The archive record of a 2014 radio interview says he had been an SDS organiser in Austin in the late 1960s and was then a Distinguished Professor in the Department of Creative Writing at the University of California, Riverside [S17]. Verso records that he held a MacArthur Fellowship and a Lannan Literary Award [S1]; the radio record dates the MacArthur fellowship to 1998 [S17].

In a statement quoted by Verso in 2022, Davis rejected consoling endings: "I'm writing because I'm hoping the people who read it don't need dollops of hope or good endings but are reading so that they'll know what to fight, and fight even when the fight seems hopeless" [S2]. The publisher's obituary says he had been ill for many years, was survived by his partner, the artist Alessandra Moctezuma, and four children, and that shortly before his death rumours of his condition produced what he described as surprisingly warm public tributes [S2]. This dossier does not use a precise birth date, birthplace or date of death: none was confirmed in an institutional or publisher record that was opened. Verso's archived book page from 2009 already noted that he had been awarded a MacArthur Fellowship, which corroborates the award but not the biographical details just mentioned [S8].

## Intellectual tradition

Davis wrote from within the Marxist and New Left tradition, but his work is not easily reduced to a single school. Verso's obituary emphasises his education in the American Communist Party and his lifelong location on the Left, while noting that his writing targeted the economic, political and military apparatus of the US state and the brutality of empires generally [S2]. His long association with *New Left Review* is documented by the journal's own author record, which lists more than twenty articles between 1980 and 2020 on labour, cities, US politics, climate science and Marx [S14]. Two of these show the range: "Who Killed Los Angeles? A Political Autopsy" (1993) and "Marx's Lost Theory" (2015), the latter a re-reading of Marx's political writings on class, nation and the world market [S14]. Verso describes *Old Gods, New Enigmas* as his first book on Marxism itself and as an attempt to clarify "proletarian agency" for the present [S10].

He did not write as an academic specialist in one discipline. Publisher descriptions place him across urban history, political economy, ecology and labour history [S1][S5][S6]. He was also willing to argue with the tradition he came from. In "Who Will Build the Ark?" he structured the essay as a debate with himself between "analytic despair and utopian possibility", and argued that the urbanisation of humanity, the main cause of warming in his account, might also be part of the solution if cities were remade from below [S19]. A review quoted on Verso's book page described him as "part polemicist, part historian, and all Marxist" [S3]. That combination of commitment and argument is the safest way to describe his tradition: Marxist in affiliation, political-ecological in method, and resistant to any official socialist label as a substitute for evidence.

## Major contributions

The most consequential contribution was to join climate history to imperial political economy. Verso's description of *Late Victorian Holocausts* says Davis examined El Niño-induced droughts and the famines that followed in India, northern China and north-eastern Brazil, and argued that drought's effects were magnified by destructive policies imposed by different ruling elites [S3]. The publisher summarises his conclusion that the seeds of underdevelopment were sown in the era of high imperialism, "as the price for capitalist modernization was paid in the currency of millions of peasants' lives" [S3]. Robert B. Marks, reviewing the book in the *Journal of Economic History*, described the thesis as a conjunction of droughts, El Niño, Western imperialism and the later wealth gap, in which droughts became famines because of a London-centred international capitalism [S24]. That framing made the book a reference point for arguing that famines are historical outcomes rather than purely climatic events [S22][S24].

The second contribution was an urban political economy of Los Angeles and of cities more generally. Verso says *City of Quartz* reconstructs the city's "shadow history", dissects its economy, and asks "who has the power and how they hold on to it" [S5]. *Ecology of Fear* paired the city's imaginary destruction in film and fiction with its real history of disasters and social inequality, and the current edition carries a 2021 afterword on the twenty-first century [S6]. *Planet of Slums* extended the approach worldwide: Verso describes its argument that urbanisation has been disconnected from industrialisation and even from economic growth, leaving a vast informal proletariat "warehoused in shantytowns and exiled from the formal world economy" [S4]. In his own 2004 article Davis wrote that the urban population of the earth was about to outnumber the rural one and that cities had absorbed nearly two-thirds of global population growth since 1950 [S20].

A third contribution concerned labour and ethnicity in the United States. *Prisoners of the American Dream* asks why the most industrially advanced country never produced a mass working-class party, surveying US political economy from the Jacksonian period to the New Right [S9]. *Magical Urbanism* argued that Latinos were converting demographic weight in US cities into social power; the NLR version of the argument used census data to show Latinos overtaking Black Americans as the second-largest ethno-racial group in New York and other large cities [S7][S21]. Verso notes that the book won the 2001 Carey McWilliams Award [S7].

## Analysis of capitalism

Davis's account of capitalism starts from the claim that markets are made by power, not found in nature. Chapter one of *Late Victorian Holocausts*, published by the *New York Times* as a book excerpt, describes how older systems of "household and village grain reserves regulated by complex networks of patrimonial obligation" had been replaced by "merchant inventories and the cash nexus" [S18]. In the Madras famine of 1876–77, the railways that were praised as safeguards against famine were used by traders to move grain to central depots for hoarding, and the telegraph allowed price rises to be coordinated across towns regardless of local supply [S18]. His summary of the contemporary finding was that "the dearth ... was one of money and of labour rather than of food" [S18]. Grain was exported to Europe while people starved; a British official noted that "Londoners were in effect eating India's bread" [S18].

The political mechanism was laissez-faire doctrine backed by the state. Davis quotes Lord Lytton's order that "there is to be no interference of any kind on the part of Government with the object of reducing the price of food", and contrasts this with Adam Smith's earlier claim that famines arose from government interference with dearth [S18]. He writes that "India like Ireland before it had become a Utilitarian laboratory where millions of lives were wagered against dogmatic faith in omnipotent markets overcoming the 'inconvenience of dearth'" [S18]. Relief was organised around a punitive "distance test" and a ration — the "Temple wage" — that Davis says provided less sustenance for hard labour than the diet in a concentration camp, a comparison he makes explicitly and which should be reported as his argument, not as an independent finding [S18]. He calls Sir Richard Temple "the personification of free market economics as a mask for colonial genocide" [S18]. The publisher's blurb frames the whole book in the same terms: imperial arrogance and natural incident combined to produce tragedies, and underdevelopment was the price paid for capitalist modernisation [S3].

Sen, while broadly endorsing the exposure of imperial policy, refined the mechanism. He argued that a drought damages incomes and employment as well as food supply, so famine cannot be solved simply by opening markets or building railways: the victims lack the means to buy food that is physically available [S22]. Sen quoted Davis's own question about "smug claims about the life-saving benefits of steam transportation and modern grain markets" when millions died "alongside railroad tracks or on the steps of grain depots" [S22]. Davis's capitalism is therefore a system in which market integration, fiscal policy and coercive relief rules determine who eats, with the formal freedom of the market concealing the political choices that create hunger.

On contemporary capitalism, *Planet of Slums* argues that post-colonial urban growth produces a huge population outside formal employment and formal housing [S4]. Davis's 2004 article gives his own figures for urban growth and treats megacities and second-tier cities as the main sites of the coming inequality [S20]. *The Monster Enters*, the expanded edition of his 2005 book on avian flu, extends the same structure to pandemics: Verso summarises him as exposing the roles of agribusiness, fast-food industries, corrupt governments and a capitalist global system in creating the conditions for plague [S11]. *Buda's Wagon* makes a parallel argument about urban warfare and state intelligence services [S13]. Across these works the constant is not simply that capitalism is unequal, but that its inequalities are built into infrastructure, relief rules, housing markets and security systems.

## Analysis of socialism

Davis left no institutional blueprint for socialism. What can be verified is a commitment to working-class organisation and a recurring search for forms of popular power capable of checking capitalist destruction. Verso's obituary describes him as a working-class intellectual formed in the Communist Party, and his NLR record shows decades of writing on why the US working class has not formed a mass party and on the history of its struggles [S2][S9][S14]. *Set the Night on Fire*, written with Jon Wiener, is a movement history of 1960s Los Angeles built from archival research, interviews with participants and the authors' own recollections; Verso's description emphasises Black Power, the Chicano walkouts, the antiwar movement and struggles over housing, schools and police violence [S12]. That is his socialism as historical practice rather than constitutional design.

In his later work he looked for an ecological socialism of cities. In "Who Will Build the Ark?" he wrote that a new Ark would have to be built "out of the materials that a desperate humanity finds at hand in insurgent communities, pirate technologies, bootlegged media, rebel science and forgotten utopias" [S19]. The NLR summary of the essay says he explored whether green urbanism, joining social equality to ecological sustainability, could offer a starting point [S14]. *Old Gods, New Enigmas* returns to Marx to ask what social forces and conditions could transform the existing order, with "proletarian agency" explicitly named as a problem for a new theory of revolution [S10]. These are programme fragments, not a fully specified alternative. Sen's caution belongs here too: in his review he noted that the largest twentieth-century famines occurred in countries outside liberal capitalism, including China in 1958–61, the Soviet Union in the 1930s, Cambodia and North Korea, and argued that the underlying problem was economic inequality combined with political disempowerment wherever it occurred [S22]. Davis's work should be read as a critique of capitalist empire. The opened sources do not show him offering a defence of socialist states, and Sen's warning about famine under other systems should not be reported as Davis's own claim.

## Intellectual influences

Davis's own chapter one names the intellectual architecture behind his argument about famine. He quotes Adam Smith's claim that famine had never arisen except from government attempts to remedy dearth, and identifies Smith's influence on the East India Company college at Haileybury where the viceroy was trained [S18]. He invokes Thomas Malthus, "former Chair of Political Economy at Haileybury", and the Social Darwinist updating of his doctrine as the justification used by colonial officials for inaction [S18]. Karl Marx appears in the same paragraph as an early observer of the transforming effects of railways in India [S18]. The chapter also quotes the historian Jairus Banaji on indebted peasant households and uses Florence Nightingale's 1877 remark on famine as its epigraph [S18]. These are named in Davis's text itself and can be treated as influences without inference.

Sen's review reports two further influences. It says Davis's notion of "political ecology" cites Michael Watts's 1983 book *Silent Violence*, and that Davis quotes Karl Polanyi approvingly on the destruction of the Indian village community [S22]. Sen's own disagreement with the Polanyi claim is discussed below; the attribution to Davis is secure because Sen reproduces Davis's words. The NLR record adds that Davis wrote on Kropotkin's contribution to climate and earth science from a Tsarist prison and on the stratigraphers of the Geological Society of London who declared the Anthropocene, both of which he treated as resources for a historical materialist environmental history [S14][S19]. The 2014 radio description lists Drake and Cayton's *Black Metropolis* as a reference in his work on Latino urban politics [S17][S21]. What cannot be established from the opened sources is a single line of intellectual descent: Davis used Marxists, economists, climatologists and urbanists as tools, and argued with several of them in print.

## Influence and legacy

The clearest measure of influence is that the book became a model for connecting climate events to colonial political economy. Susanna Hecht's 2024 article in *Human Geography* describes Davis as foundational for global environmental history because of his method of tracing a particular climate event and then parsing out its social effects, and argues that his work provided early historical analysis of colonial capitalism in creating climate vulnerability [S27]. Sandeep Banerjee's 2025 introduction to a forum in *Global Nineteenth-Century Studies* says the forum extends Davis's insights into aesthetics, politics and the environment and marks the continuing relevance of the text after his death [S28]. Both are scholarly assessments; the full articles were not opened, so these are their published abstracts, not independent verification of their arguments.

His standing among fellow radicals is recorded by the publisher. The Verso obituary says tributes to him were widespread and that the breadth of them surprised and pleased him [S2]. The 2024 podcast discussion with Ruth Wilson Gilmore and Owen Hatherley treats his work as a guide to "what power looks like, how does it operate, and how can it be wrestled back into the hands of the global working classes" [S16]. The 2026 reading group, with sessions built around scholars including Mrinalini Sinha, Rebecca Karl, Geoff Mann, Sunil Amrith, Richard Seymour and Cormac Ó Gráda, shows that the book is still being read across history, climate science and political economy [S15]. These are records of reputation and use. They do not establish that Davis's numerical estimates are correct, which is a separate question addressed next.

## Criticisms and controversies

The demographic figures are estimates with a wide published range, not a settled count. Sen's 2001 review says Davis estimated that "between 32 and 61 million people died" in famines in China, India and Brazil, with other tropical countries also badly affected [S22]. Marks's review extract summarises the death toll as "30 to 50 million" [S24]. Malchow's review abstract gives a range of "thirty-one million to sixty-one million" [S25]. The figures differ because the underlying events, baselines and causes of death differ; none of these sources provides a single audited count, and no opened source offers a verified independent recalculation. This dossier therefore reports the ranges as published estimates and does not present any one number as established.

Sen's methodological criticism is the most substantial from a scholar broadly sympathetic to the book. He argues that a drought destroys incomes and employment as well as crops, so the relevant question is what people can command, not only how much food exists [S22]. He says the book's concentration on markets and transport misses the need for public action that creates incomes, such as emergency employment, and warns that markets and railways can add to security when combined with economic means [S22]. He rejects what he calls Davis's approval of Polanyi's claim that the Indian village community was demolished: "this is an enormous exaggeration. In exploding one myth, we have to be careful not to fall for another" [S22]. Sen also insists that the imperial failure Davis describes was not unique to liberal capitalism, pointing to famines in twentieth-century socialist and authoritarian states [S22]. Marks's review extract is more favourable but still frames the book as an interpretation in which natural events became famines because of a London-centred capitalism, rather than a demographic demonstration [S24].

Tirthankar Roy's 2016 LSE working paper supplies the sharpest economic-history critique of method. Roy says Davis "uses the word 'manmade' to mean made-by-the-colonial-state" and attributes mass death to adherence to capitalist ideology by imperial states [S23]. Roy tests that framework against the timing of Indian famines: mortality fell and famines became rarer after 1900, with the transition around 1915, but colonialism did not end then, so he concludes that "these terms do not really explain anything" without qualification [S23]. His alternative stresses limited state capacity — poor information, thin administration, meagre infrastructure and small fiscal resources — and the growth of knowledge about relief, while noting that disease control accounted for more deaths than starvation in the earlier famines [S23]. He also criticises the assumption behind both "natural" and "manmade" accounts that a pre-colonial golden age of welfare or altruism existed, calling such arguments "speculative" [S23]. Roy's paper is itself a review of theories; it is not a recalculation of Davis's totals, and this dossier does not treat it as one.

There are further reviews whose full texts were not opened and from which no argument is quoted here: Laxman D. Satya in *Environment and History* (2003), Indur M. Goklany in *Environmental History* (2003), Matthew Gandy in *Progress in Development Studies* (2002) and Richard Leitch in *Review of Radical Political Economics* (2008), all identified in publisher or Crossref records [S29]. The widely repeated claim that the historian Alexander Morrison described Davis's "ignorance of Indian history" as "profound" was traced to a tertiary encyclopedia entry whose cited source, on inspection, is a 2025 review essay about a different book [S30]. It is not used here as evidence. The controversy over whether the Indian famines constituted genocide is likewise not resolved in the opened sources; Davis's own chapter uses the phrase "colonial genocide" in describing Temple [S18], while Sen and Roy address causation rather than the legal or genocidal label [S22][S23].

## Related thinkers

Karl Marx is the central figure: Davis's late book returns to Marx's political writings to recover a theory of revolution and proletarian agency [S10][S14]. Karl Polanyi appears through Sen's report that Davis quoted him on the destruction of the village community, a claim Sen rejects [S22]. Michael Watts's *Silent Violence* is named by Sen as the source of Davis's phrase "political ecology" [S22]. Adam Smith and Thomas Malthus are not allies but the objects of Davis's critique of famine policy, quoted in his own chapter [S18]. Amartya Sen is both a reviewer who found the book "highly informative" and a theorist whose entitlement approach challenged Davis's emphasis on market integration and the decline of village protection [S22]. Tirthankar Roy is an economic historian whose critique of the political explanation of Indian famines directly contests Davis's causal framework [S23]. Robert B. Marks and H. L. Malchow are reviewers whose published summaries establish the range of the death estimates and the broad shape of the thesis [S24][S25]. Ruth Wilson Gilmore, Owen Hatherley, Susanna Hecht and Sandeep Banerjee appear in records of Davis's legacy and continuing influence [S16][S27][S28]. No published profile of these figures is linked here because none was available in this repository at the time of writing.
