### A. Replacement for the Cambodia paragraph

Chomsky's writing on Cambodia is the sharpest dispute in this collection, and it can now be reported more precisely than the first version of this profile allowed. In the 1977 review "Distortions at Fourth Hand", written with Edward Herman, he gave weight to analyses that put executions "at most in the thousands", called one book based on refugee testimony a "third-rate propaganda tract", found François Ponchaud's book "serious and worth reading" but accused it of playing "fast and loose with quotes and with numbers", and concluded that the authors did "not pretend to know where the truth lies amidst these sharply conflicting assessments" ([Chomsky and Herman, 1977](https://chomsky.info/19770625/)). Chomsky's own article index dates the piece 25 June 1977 while the text page gives 6 June 1977, so only the year is asserted here.

The most detailed published criticism is Bruce Sharp's long, footnoted essay "Averaging Wrong Answers". Sharp argues that the underlying theme of the 1977 review and of *After the Cataclysm* was that Khmer Rouge Cambodia was not as bad as its detractors claimed, and that Chomsky's work on the subject is "marred by omissions, dubious statistics, and, in some cases, outright misrepresentations" ([Sharp](https://www.mekong.net/cambodia/chomsky.htm)). He objects in particular to the favourable treatment given to Hildebrand and Porter's book alongside the dismissal of books based on refugee testimony, and reports that 33 of the 50 citations in one chapter of that book come from Khmer Rouge sources and six from the Chinese news agency Hsinhua. The essay is self-published on a specialist Cambodia archive, is not peer reviewed, and is recorded here as a charge rather than a finding. Sophal Ear's 1995 political science honours thesis at Berkeley goes further, placing Chomsky and Herman in a "Khmer Rouge Canon" of works that supported the regime explicitly or implicitly ([Ear, 1995](https://phdn.org/archives/www.ess.uwe.ac.uk/genocide/cambear1.htm)); it is an undergraduate thesis, not a peer-reviewed study. In a 1991 exchange with Chomsky, the Oxford historian Jamie Metzl argued that the doubt created by the argument over Cambodia "somewhat inhibited decisive action", while conceding that Chomsky had written only one or two pieces on the subject ([Metzl correspondence](https://jamiemetzl.com/noam-chomsky-and-the-cambodian-genocide/)). That is Metzl's interpretation, not a demonstrated causal finding, and Chomsky disputes it.

The strongest independent scholarly evidence in the opened record comes from Ben Kiernan, a historian of Cambodia. In 1979 he told readers of the *Bulletin of Concerned Asian Scholars* that he had "changed my mind": he had been "late in realizing the extent of the tragedy in Kampuchea after 1975 and Pol Pot's responsibility for it", had been wrong to explain the violence after 1973 simply as a grassroots reaction, and had been "too willing to believe" that refugee testimony and the Phnom Penh radio could be reconciled ([Kiernan, 1979](https://gsp.yale.edu/sites/default/files/bkbcaoct.1979vietnam_kampuchea.pdf)). In a 2001 journal article he concluded that "Massive Khmer Rouge brutality in the repression of Cambodian 'treason' resulted in genocide", that more than one and a half million Cambodians perished under the regime, and that absolute control lay with a small party centre around Pol Pot ([Kiernan, 2001](https://gsp.yale.edu/sites/default/files/myth_nationalism_and_genocide.pdf)). Sharp reports that Chomsky and Herman drew on Kiernan's earlier, more qualified work; that link is Sharp's account, not a separately verified finding. Chomsky's own position is stated in his letter of 3 February 1991 to Metzl: that in 1977 the facts were "extremely sparse and uncertain", that he expressed no firm conclusion and never denied that atrocities were taking place, and that with hindsight he would "emphasize more strongly that atrocities were taking place on a massive scale" while retaining his criticism of double standards in Western reporting ([Chomsky, 1991](https://jamiemetzl.com/noam-chomsky-and-the-cambodian-genocide/)). What the opened sources support is a disagreement about the weight to give to uncertain reports and about the use Chomsky and Herman made of them. The stronger charge that Chomsky supported the Khmer Rouge or denied the genocide is a critic's characterisation which he rejects and which Sharp himself treats as unsettled; it is not adopted here.

### B. Replacement for the propaganda-model paragraph

The propaganda model has also drawn substantial academic criticism. The full text of the 2010 review by Andrew Mullen and Jeffery Klaehn records a first wave of hostile reviews in the late 1980s and 1990s whose authors charged that the model overstated the power of the propaganda system and downplayed popular opposition to elite preferences, presented a "conspiratorial" view of the media, was deterministic, functionalist and simplistic, and neglected journalistic professionalism ([Mullen and Klaehn, 2010, full text](https://aklatangbayan.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/mullen-klaehn-sociology-compass-propaganda-model.pdf)). A second wave included John Corner's six objections in 2003, among them doubt that a model built for the United States could apply to countries with different media systems, the complaint that the five filters were "assumed to function without much, if any, need for further specification or qualification", and the question whether filtering produces media messages or only modifies existing ones; Kurt and Gladys Lang's 2004 objections to the absence of sampling and coding information and to the assumption that information existed but had been screened out; and Oliver Boyd-Barrett's call for a way to weigh the relative power of the filters and for a sixth filter covering intelligence-agency influence over journalists. These are Mullen and Klaehn's summaries of other writers, whose original articles were not opened for this profile. The two authors' own evidence concerns reception rather than truth: in a sample of 3,053 articles from ten media and communication journals over 1988–2007, only 79 (2.6 per cent) attended to the model, and of 48 course textbooks surveyed over 1990–2007 only 11 engaged with it at all. That supports a claim of marginalisation within the field. It does not settle whether the model is correct.
