# Michael Hudson — research dossier

This is a research-stage evidence record, not a published profile. It follows `research/AUTHOR-PROFILES-STANDARD.md` and `research/MASTER-STANDARD.md`. Every factual claim below is tied to a source that was opened during this research, except where the text explicitly says that a source was seen only as a search-result endpoint. No quotation, date, page number or URL has been reconstructed from memory. Wikipedia, Goodreads, Scribd and Google Books were excluded as evidence, including where a search result happened to surface them.

## Biography

Michael Hudson was born and raised in Minneapolis in a household shaped by the American Trotskyist movement. In an autobiography interview conducted at Peking University on 7 May 2018 by Lau Kin Chi, Hudson said that his father was one of the leaders of the Minneapolis general strikes of 1934 to 1936 and was jailed under the Smith Act when Hudson was three years old, and that his parents had worked with Leon Trotsky in Mexico. He said that visitors to the family home included veterans of the Russian Revolution, and that he grew up expecting, in his words, to "lead a revolution if conditions were right." He also said that his own early interest lay in physics, chemistry and music rather than politics; he studied piano and trained as a conductor, and at the University of Chicago he combined a degree in Germanic philology and the history of culture with full-time music study. The same interview records that after Trotsky's widow Natalia died, the executor Max Schachtman assigned Hudson the copyrights to Trotsky's writings and identified him as Trotsky's godson. These are Hudson's own retrospective statements, not independently corroborated records. A separate interview by D. L. Jacobs for *Platypus Review*, conducted on 15 July 2022 and published in November 2022, repeats the account of a Marxist household and a father among the "Minneapolis 17." I did not verify an exact birth date from an admissible source, and I have not used the date that circulates in tertiary catalogues.

The author's own biography page gives the formal record: B.A. at the University of Chicago in 1959 in philology with a minor in history; M.A. at New York University in 1963 in economics, with a thesis on the World Bank's philosophy of development; and Ph.D. at New York University in 1968 in economics, with a dissertation on nineteenth-century American economic and technological thought. A conflicting M.A. date appears in tertiary material that is not admissible here, so 1963 is reported as the author's own record rather than as a settled external fact. His work history, as he presents it, moved between banking and academia. He worked at the Savings Banks Trust Company from 1962 to 1964, at Chase Manhattan Bank as a balance-of-payments economist from 1964 to 1967, at Arthur Andersen from 1968 to 1969, at Continental Oil from 1970 to 1971, and at the Hudson Institute from 1972 to 1976, where he published studies on monetary reform with Herman Kahn. He taught at the graduate faculty of the New School for Social Research from 1969 to 1972. From 1985 to 1998 he was a research associate at Harvard's Peabody Museum and a visiting scholar at New York University, organising colloquia on the ancient Near East. He became president of the Institute for the Study of Long-Term Economic Trends (ISLET) in 1996 and Distinguished Research Professor of Economics at the University of Missouri–Kansas City in 2002. The Levy Economics Institute of Bard College lists him as a research associate, and the Democracy Collaborative lists him as a fellow and author of its *Creating a New International Order* series.

Two episodes from the autobiography matter for how his economics should be read. At the Savings Banks Trust Company he observed savings accumulating at compound interest and being recycled into mortgages that bid up housing prices, and concluded that textbook accounts of bank lending bore little relation to what he saw. At Chase Manhattan he was assigned to study the balance of payments of the petroleum industry, and later the overall United States balance of payments, work that led him to argue that the entire American payments deficit of the 1960s came from overseas military spending rather than private trade and investment. He reports that this finding was unwelcome: he says Arthur Andersen declined to publish the study after Robert McNamara objected. These are first-person accounts of how he arrived at his questions. They explain his method, but the statistics behind them were not re-examined in this research.

## Intellectual tradition

Hudson describes himself as a classical economist rather than as a follower of a modern school. In the Platypus interview he traces his formation to Terence McCarthy, the translator of Marx's *Theories of Surplus Value*, who became his mentor and set him a reading programme in the bibliography of that work. He names the French Physiocrats, Adam Smith, David Ricardo, John Stuart Mill and Marx as the line of thinkers from which he took the distinction between earned income and unearned rent. In the same interview he says that in the late nineteenth century "everybody thought that industrial capitalism was evolving into socialism of one kind or more other," including Marx, Mill, Christian socialists and libertarian socialists, and that the question was which form socialism would take. He identifies the turn towards rentier economics with the period after the First World War and with the academic displacement of rent theory by John Bates Clark's claim that every income is earned.

His institutional home is heterodox rather than mainstream. The Levy Institute profile places him alongside post-Keynesian and Modern Monetary Theory economists; the Democracy Collaborative profile records that he co-hosts the *Geopolitical Economy Hour* with Radhika Desai and appears in dialogue with Richard Wolff. A 2017 interview with Adam Simpson for the Next System Project, seen only as a search-result endpoint because the author's page returned a 503 error, records him saying that "heterodox" is a recent term coined mainly at UMKC, and that he and his colleagues consider themselves classical economists who distinguish earned from unearned income and productive from unproductive labour. That formulation is consistent with the opened Platypus and autobiography interviews and is used here as a report of his position, not as an independent classification.

He has repeatedly rejected labels attached to him from other directions. He is critical of what he calls "vulgar Marxists" and of Engels's account of the origins of private property, arguing in the autobiography that property was created by palaces and temples assigning land to support corvée labour and military service rather than by individual appropriation. He is sharply critical of Henry George, by whom he is often influence-linked: in the Platypus interview he calls *Progress and Poverty* "not a very good book," argues that the single tax leaves the labour question, the banking question and economic planning untouched, and claims that Georgists became libertarian and anti-socialist, with some European followers supporting the Nazis. That last claim is Hudson's own assertion and was not checked against historical scholarship; it should be presented as his polemic, not as a finding. He also rejects the neoliberal use of Adam Smith, telling Platypus that the Adam Smith Institute "hates everything that Adam Smith stood for."

## Major contributions

Hudson's first influential contribution is the argument of *Super Imperialism*. As he restates it in the 2021 third-edition material and in interviews, closing the gold window in August 1971 left foreign central banks with few options for reserves other than United States Treasury securities. Dollars spent abroad by the American military were recycled into purchases of American government debt, so that other countries financed both the American deficit and, in his phrase, their own "economic and military encirclement." He presents the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund as institutions shaped at their inception to preserve American financial hegemony, and treats the resulting debt crises of developing countries as a structural outcome rather than a policy error. The 2004 review by the economist Sebastian Dullien, reproduced on Hudson's own site from the German journal *Intervention*, summarises the same chain of reasoning: interwar war debts, Bretton Woods, the use of the World Bank from 1952 onwards, and the conversion of Treasury debt into the world reserve asset. Because that review is hosted by Hudson, it is not an independent selection of criticism.

The second contribution is the concept of a rentier or FIRE economy. In *Killing the Host* and in his 2021 article in the *Review of Radical Political Economics*, Hudson argues that finance, insurance and real estate no longer fund industrial capital formation but instead extract economic rent: income obtained from owning assets rather than from producing goods or services. He holds that banks lend chiefly against existing collateral, especially real estate, so that mortgage credit capitalises into land prices rather than creating new productive capacity, and that interest, fees and penalties are counted in national income as if they were productive contributions. In the Platypus interview he illustrates this with his own inquiry to the Commerce Department about where credit-card late fees appear in the national accounts, and with his work with Dirk Bezemer and others on how much reported GDP is overhead. His 2021 article in the *Review of Keynesian Economics*, whose abstract was verified through Crossref, states that he reconstructs the national accounts to add asset-price gains and to treat rent extraction as a charge against income rather than a contribution to output. I did not open the full article or its underlying data, so this is a report of his stated method, not a verification of the results.

The third contribution is the history of debt cancellation. Beginning in the early 1980s, Hudson assembled a research programme on debt, land tenure and credit from Sumerian times onwards, working with Karl Lamberg-Karlovsky at Harvard's Peabody Museum and with the Assyriologist Baruch Levine. The autobiography records five colloquia, whose volumes cover privatization in the ancient Near East, urbanization and land ownership, debt and economic renewal, and the origins of account-keeping and money. His claim is that Mesopotamian rulers regularly proclaimed "Clean Slates" cancelling personal and agrarian debts while leaving commercial debts intact, in order to prevent creditors from reducing cultivators to bondage and depriving the palace of taxable surplus, corvée labour and soldiers. In *The Collapse of Antiquity* he argues that Greece and Rome adopted interest-bearing debt without this safety valve, so that creditor oligarchies formed, reformers were suppressed, and Rome collapsed into serfdom, bequeathing a pro-creditor legal philosophy to the West. In the four-part interview with John Siman in 2019 he is more cautious about transmission than some summaries suggest: he says he does not think the Greeks and Italians knew of Near Eastern royal proclamations except as a distant practice, and that debt cancellations in Greece were a pragmatic response to local demands rather than a policy borrowed from the East.

The fourth contribution is a revisionist history of economic thought. *Trade, Development and Foreign Debt* surveys the development of international trade and investment theory from the courses he taught at the New School, arguing that mainstream accounts exclude protectionist thought, the transfer problem and the financing of war. In a 2009 response to Paul Krugman on New Economic Perspectives, which was opened, Hudson extends this into a broad methodological objection: he quotes Paul Samuelson's 1939 statement that one need not be committed to the relation between abstract assumptions and reality, and William Vickrey's textbook statement that a theory's validity does not depend on correspondence with observation. He argues that this habit of mind protects rentier interests by removing the social structures that were once the substance of political economy.

## Analysis of capitalism

Hudson's account of capitalism separates a productive economy from a rent-extracting one. In *Killing the Host* and the *Destiny of Civilization* material he describes the FIRE sector as gaining control of government and using tax favours, privatization, monetary and credit policy, and monopoly rights to move income upward. The productive host is industry, labour and agriculture; the parasite lives off claims on their output. He does not argue that finance is always parasitic in every form. His position is that credit becomes destructive when it is created chiefly against assets already in place, when it is used for takeovers and asset stripping rather than new capital formation, and when its charges are recorded as output. In the 2019 Siman interview he puts the historical claim plainly: "Rentiers don't create production. They live off existing production, they don't create it."

A second mechanism is debt deflation. Debt grows at compound interest faster than an economy can grow, so that when incomes fail to keep pace, debtor households, firms and states must sell assets, cut spending or accept austerity. The 2021 RRPE article argues that labor is increasingly exploited through bank debt, student debt and credit-card debt while housing and other prices are inflated on credit, leaving less income to spend on goods and services. In the Platypus interview Hudson treats the privatisation of public utilities as part of the same process: a public utility sells below cost in order to lower the economy's overall costs, whereas a privatised one must cover interest, profit and monopoly rent, raising the living costs of labor and undermining industrial competitiveness.

A third mechanism is what he calls the inversion of classical liberalism. In the nineteenth century, he argues, the free market meant freedom from landlord and monopoly rent; in the twentieth century the same words came to mean freedom for rentiers. The Federal Reserve's creation in 1913, which he discusses in the Platypus interview, marks for him the transfer of financial policy from the Treasury to Wall Street. He dates the decisive turn to the 1890s and the years after the First World War, when, in his account, economics departments dropped the distinction between productive and unproductive labour and the study of economic rent. The result, in his telling, is not a free market but a centrally planned rentier economy in which banks and asset owners act as the central planners.

Internationally he treats the American empire as financial rather than territorial in the classical sense. The dollar's reserve role, military spending abroad, and the recycling of dollar surpluses into Treasury securities form a single circuit. He extends this to oil: in the autobiography he describes Treasury instructions to Saudi Arabia after the 1973 oil price rise that export proceeds be recycled into American stocks and bonds, and in interviews he treats petrodollars and eurodollars as variants of the same recycling mechanism. These accounts are his first-person recollections of meetings and instructions; I did not locate an independent documentary record of the specific conversations.

## Analysis of socialism

Hudson does not offer a detailed blueprint for a socialist society, and the standard requires that this be said plainly. What he does offer is a set of institutional preferences drawn from classical political economy and from what he regards as actually existing mixed economies. In the *Destiny of Civilization* material he frames the choice as between a neoliberal neo-feudalism dominated by a rentier oligarchy and a mixed-economy industrial capitalism that leads towards socialism. The mixed economy, in his account, keeps money creation, banking, land and basic infrastructure under public control so that they lower the cost of production for the whole economy instead of generating private rent. In the Siman interview he says the needed institutions are civil law with a strong democratic government, public banking and credit, and the power to cancel debts when they grow beyond the population's ability to pay; he explicitly calls this a mixed economy and says that socialism was an attempt to create one.

His concrete policies recur across opened sources. They include taxing land rent and economic rent rather than labour, keeping public utilities in public hands and pricing them below cost, establishing a public option in banking, writing down unpayable debts, and allowing states to declare bankruptcy in the way that firms and individuals can. The CEPR profile records two discussion papers with Charles Goodhart and others: *Could/Should Jubilee Debt Cancellations be Reintroduced Today?* (January 2018) and *Post-Corona Balanced-Budget Super-Stimulus: The Case for Shifting Taxes onto Land* (October 2021). These show that his rent and debt arguments have been formalised with mainstream and heterodox co-authors, although I did not open the papers themselves.

He distinguishes his position from both single-tax Georgism and what he considers state socialism of the Stalinist type. In the Platypus interview he argues that taxing land alone cannot solve the labour problem or the banking problem, and that a government strong enough to tax landlords must be a socialist government. He treats Bolshevik central planning as a failed route and points instead to China, where he says the state keeps banking and money creation in the public domain and directs credit to new means of production rather than to mergers and acquisitions. In the same interview he says he has not found an alternative political path in the United States. These are his evaluations of existing states; the claim that China is "following the industrial capitalist policy that evolves naturally into socialism" is his argument, not an established description, and the empirical record of Chinese banking and labour relations was not examined here.

## Intellectual influences

The influences best evidenced are those Hudson himself names. Terence McCarthy is the central one: in both the 2018 autobiography and the 2022 Platypus interview, McCarthy is the translator of Marx's *Theories of Surplus Value* who became his mentor and redirected him from music and publishing towards economics. The Physiocrats, Adam Smith, Ricardo, Mill and Marx are the intellectual lineage he claims repeatedly; in the Siman interview he says classical economists supported industrial capitalists against landlords, monopolists and predatory banks. Hyman Minsky appears in the autobiography as a writer assigned in a money-and-banking course whose account of savings banks Hudson contrasted with what he had seen at the Savings Banks Trust Company. Simon Patten is invoked in the Platypus interview as the source of the idea of public infrastructure as a "fourth factor of production" that lowers costs rather than seeking profit.

His non-economic intellectual formation also shaped the work. Eugen Jolles introduced him to Spengler, Egon Friedell and Nietzsche, and he studied Heinrich Schenker's music theory; he told Lau Kin Chi that these were his literary and musical background before economics. Seymour Melman and McCarthy, with Hudson, formed the "Columbia Group" that warned in the 1960s that Vietnam War spending would destroy the American monetary system, according to the autobiography. In the ancient Near Eastern work he names Karl Lamberg-Karlovsky, Baruch Levine and Marc Van De Mieroop as colleagues whose colloquia and linguistic authority made the debt-cancellation interpretation possible. Herman Kahn was an employer rather than an intellectual mentor; the autobiography says they "disagreed on everything" politically, and Kahn recruited him to the Hudson Institute after hearing his balance-of-payments lecture. Later collaborators include Charles Goodhart, Michael Kumhof and Nicolaus Tideman in the CEPR papers, Dirk Bezemer in work on GDP measurement mentioned in the Platypus interview, Radhika Desai in the *Geopolitical Economy Hour*, and Richard Wolff in public dialogues noted by the Democracy Collaborative.

## Influence and legacy

Hudson's influence is visible in several distinct channels. His early balance-of-payments work and *Super Imperialism* established a durable critique of the dollar system outside the mainstream; the third edition in 2021 and the interviews around it show that he continued to apply that framework to de-dollarisation and the rivalry between the United States, China and Russia. The reviewer Alfredo Hernandez Sanchez wrote in the *LSE Review of Books* in August 2019 that Hudson is "best known for being one of the few economists who accurately predicted the 2008 financial crisis." That is the reviewer's assessment, and I did not independently verify the prediction or its timing.

Within heterodox economics, his rentier thesis has become a live position rather than a settled result. The fact that the *Review of Radical Political Economics* published his 2021 article and a formal critical comment in the same issue is evidence that the argument is being contested inside the tradition closest to it. His article on rent-seeking and asset-price inflation appeared in the *Review of Keynesian Economics*, and his policy papers with Goodhart and others appeared under CEPR, which indicates that parts of his programme have entered policy-oriented economic discussion even where the broader thesis has not been accepted. The Levy Institute and Democracy Collaborative affiliations place him in networks that connect heterodox economics to public policy and community wealth building.

His ancient-history programme has had a different kind of legacy. The five colloquia he organised, published by the Peabody Museum and CDL Press, helped establish debt cancellation in Mesopotamia as a subject of serious Assyriological discussion. In the 2019 Siman interview Hudson says that when he first proposed the idea in the 1980s, referees treated royal debt cancellation as inconceivable, and that by the time of that interview the idea was generally accepted among Assyriologists and archaeologists. That is his account of the field's development; no independent history of the reception was opened. His trilogy on debt, from *…and forgive them their debts* through *The Collapse of Antiquity* to the projected third volume, has brought the argument to a general audience, and the LSE review records that the 2018 volume was on the *Financial Times* best-books list for that year.

## Criticisms and controversies

The most substantive scholarly objection found in this research comes from within Hudson's own theoretical neighbourhood. J. W. Mason, of John Jay College of Criminal Justice, published "Comments on Michael Hudson: Making Capitalism Great Again? A Critique of the 'Rentier Takeover' Thesis" in *Review of Radical Political Economics* 53(4), 2021, pp. 574–578, first published online on 25 August 2021. Crossref metadata and the article abstract were opened directly; the SAGE full text returned a 403 error. Mason's abstract states that Hudson's vision of financialization is "widely held, but, I argue, misleading," that it "exaggerates the differences between historical and present-day capitalism, and misses the ways in which 'finance' and 'industry' form complementary parts of a single process." This is a direct theoretical rejection of the productive/parasitic split by an economist writing in a radical journal. I could not read Mason's detailed evidence, so no claim about which particular data he uses should be made.

A second criticism concerns the form of Hudson's books. Dwight D. Murphey, a retired Wichita State University economist reviewing *Killing the Host* in the *Journal of Social, Political and Economic Studies* 41(2), Summer 2016, pp. 84–94, called the book a "disorganized potpourri" and a "hodge-podge of assorted columns and blogs," while judging parts of it "valuable and important," especially the history of the idea that much wealth is a windfall rather than the product of individual effort. The journal's openly published abstract was opened; the full review is subscribers-only. The criticism is about compilation and editing rather than the central thesis. Alfredo Hernandez Sanchez made a related point about accessibility in the LSE review, writing that the book demands "an encyclopedic knowledge of the history of the Bronze Age" and that a reader may get lost in a maze of facts and terms. Both reviewers nonetheless judged the work worth reading.

A third line of objection bears on the dollar-and-deficit claim. In BIS Working Paper 684, *Triffin: dilemma or myth?*, published 19 December 2017, Michael Bordo and Robert N. McCauley argue that the popular "current account Triffin" story — that the dollar's reserve role required American current-account deficits — is "anachronistic, and flawed in logic and fact," and that better United States policies could have kept Bretton Woods going. This paper does not name or answer Hudson, and his own claim concerns military spending and the balance of payments rather than a simple current-account identity. It is included here as relevant scholarly work that complicates a claim his account relies on, not as a refutation of *Super Imperialism*. The distinction matters and should be preserved in any published profile.

Earlier reviewers made shorter but pointed objections. William Diebold Jr., reviewing *Global Fracture* in *Foreign Affairs* in January 1978, wrote that the case for its "provocative prospects" rested on "some good and some poor arguments," and warned that readers' objections to Hudson's interpretation of the past and present should not blind them to the possibilities he raised for the future. The German economist Sebastian Dullien, in the 2004 review reproduced on Hudson's site, was broadly sympathetic, calling the book thoroughly researched and its accusations "anything but easy to dismiss," but his review is hosted by the author and cannot be treated as an independent sample of opinion.

Several of Hudson's claims remain contested or unverified. His statements that the CIA bought two thousand copies of *Super Imperialism*, that he briefed White House, State Department and Defense Department officials, and that a State Department official described a plan to attract criminal capital to finance the Vietnam War are first-person recollections in the autobiography and in interviews; I found no corroborating document. His interview statements about Obama, Biden, the Supreme Court, Georgists and Nazi sympathy are polemical judgements, not checked findings. The exact publication year of the first *Super Imperialism* edition, the M.A. date, the first-edition date of *Global Fracture*, and the exact subtitle of *Killing the Host* all differ between records; the variations are recorded above rather than smoothed over. No scholarly criticism of *The Collapse of Antiquity* by a classicist or ancient historian was found in the sources opened; the reviews located on that book were journalistic or by sympathetic public figures, and are not used here as scholarly assessment. No empirical dataset was opened in this research.

## Related thinkers

Marx is the central reference point, both through *Theories of Surplus Value* and through the claim that classical political economy reached its logical conclusion in Marx; Hudson nonetheless parts company with what he calls vulgar Marxism and with Bolshevik central planning. Henry George is a contested figure in this tradition: Hudson takes the rent concept seriously but rejects the single tax and much of the Georgist political tradition. Adam Smith, David Ricardo, John Stuart Mill and the Physiocrats are sources of the earned/unearned distinction; Simon Patten supplies his account of public infrastructure. Thorstein Veblen appears through a Levy working paper on Veblen's elaboration of rent theory, and Hyman Minsky through the autobiography's account of early money-and-banking teaching.

Among contemporaries, Radhika Desai shares his geopolitical-economy framing and co-hosts the *Geopolitical Economy Hour* with him; Richard Wolff appears in regular dialogues. David Graeber's work on debt is adjacent, and the David Graeber Institute hosted a session with Hudson on Graeber's essay "Against Economics," but this dossier does not treat influence between them as established. Charles Goodhart, Michael Kumhof and Nicolaus Tideman are co-authors on applied tax and debt-jubilee proposals. Dirk Bezemer is named as a collaborator on GDP measurement. J. W. Mason is a critic from within radical political economy, and Michael Bordo and Robert McCauley are critics of a related reserve-currency argument from mainstream monetary economics. James K. Galbraith and Martin Wolf appear only as endorsers quoted on Hudson's own *Collapse of Antiquity* page; an endorsement is not evidence of intellectual agreement with the whole argument.
