## Biography

David Rolfe Graeber is identified by his own website as "David Rolfe Graeber (1961–2020)"; Penguin gives the date of death as 2 September 2020, and LSE posted its departmental condolence on 3 September 2020 (Primary Theoretical; Primary Official). In his autobiography he writes that he was born and raised in New York, the son of Kenneth Graeber, a plate stripper in offset photolithography originally from Kansas who had fought with the International Brigades in the Spanish Civil War, and Ruth Rubinstein Graeber, born in Poland, a garment worker and homemaker who had been the female lead in the 1930s Labor Stage musical *Pins & Needles*. He grew up in the Penn South Coops in Chelsea, attended local public schools, and was "discovered by some Maya archaeologists because of an odd hobby I had developed of translating Maya hieroglyphics". A scholarship took him to Phillips Academy at Andover for three years, then he returned to state school at SUNY Purchase, graduating with a BA in anthropology in 1984. He went to the University of Chicago, lived there for over a decade apart from two years of fieldwork in highland Madagascar between 1989 and 1991, and received his PhD in 1996. He describes a series of academic jobs including some graduate teaching at Chicago, a year at Haverford, a year of unemployment, and a junior faculty post at Yale (Primary Theoretical).

The break with Yale is described in his own words. "In 2004, the Yale department voted not to continue my contract, before I could begin the process of coming up for tenure." He calls the procedure "very unusual" and says "new rules had to be invented for my case (i.e., no student or outside reviews were allowed)". Yale gave "no reason for its decision other than dissatisfaction with my scholarship but some felt it might not have been entirely irrelevant that I was by this time quite active in the Global Justice Movement and other anarchist-inspired projects". His site's press archive lists the resulting student petitions and letters of support from faculty at Chicago, Sussex and Glasgow and from Maurice Bloch at LSE, but those individual letters were not opened for this dossier, so the existence of the campaign is verified only through his own summary and the archive listings (Primary Theoretical). He moved to Goldsmiths, University of London, from 2007 to 2013 and then to the London School of Economics, where his autobiography calls him "a full professor". The LSE condolence, written by the head of the anthropology department, Professor Laura Bear, calls him "a hugely influential anthropologist, political activist and public intellectual" whose work ranged from Madagascan funerary practices to bureaucracy, financialisation, kings, puppets and pirates (Primary Official).

He dates his own political commitment precisely. "I told a magazine once that I've been an anarchist since I was 16, so I guess that must be true, but I only really became active in any meaningful way after the beginning 2000, when I threw myself into the Alter-Globalization movement." He summarises his project as "exploring the relation between anthropology as an intellectual pursuit, and practical attempts to create a free society, free, at least, of capitalism, patriarchy, and coercive state bureaucracies" (Primary Theoretical). He married the artist Nika Dubrovsky, who edited and introduced his posthumous essay collection; this is stated on the author's book page for that collection (Primary Theoretical).

## Intellectual tradition

Graeber worked in anthropology and in anarchist political thought, and he resisted the label that his most famous pamphlet earned him. Of *Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology* he wrote that it "has doomed me ever since to be referred to as 'the anarchist anthropologist' (despite the fact that the book largely argues that anarchist anthropology doesn't and probably couldn't really exist. Please don't do that. You don't call people 'the social democrat anthropologist' do you?)" (Primary Theoretical). In the pamphlet itself he treats anarchism less as a doctrine than as a practice: "Marxism has tended to be a theoretical or analytical discourse about revolutionary strategy. Anarchism has tended to be an ethical discourse about revolutionary practice." Anarchism, he writes, insists "that one's means must be consonant with one's ends; one cannot create freedom through authoritarian means", and that as far as possible one must "in one's relations with one's friends and allies, embody the society one wishes to create" (Primary Theoretical).

His relationship to Marxism was critical but not dismissive. In the same pamphlet he notes that Bakunin, despite his battles with Marx, translated *Capital* into Russian, and that tendencies such as autonomism and council communism, which are not named after individuals, are "the ones closest to anarchism". In the VOX interview he answered a question about the Marxist character of his argument by saying "No question, and I don't run away from the Marxist aspects of this", while in *Bullshit Jobs* he rejects orthodox Marxists who hold that every job under capitalism must be productive of surplus value or of the conditions of value-creation, calling that "a statement of faith" alongside the libertarian insistence that the market cannot produce social problems (Primary Theoretical). In a 2018 interview he argued that if Marxists could say wage labour was marginal for 250 years after capitalism supposedly began in 1500 and still call it central, "by that logic, capitalism could have ended in like 1950, and we'll only fully know what replaced it in 2175" (Primary Theoretical). He also wrote a short manifesto "Against Policy", reproduced in *Fragments*: "The notion of 'policy' presumes a state or governing apparatus which imposes its will on others. 'Policy' is the negation of politics" (Primary Theoretical).

## Major contributions

The first contribution is the critique of the barter origin story of money and the claim that debt, not barter, came first. In the *Debt* extract he writes that "in studying economic history, we tend to systematically ignore the role of violence, the absolutely central role of war and slavery in creating and shaping the basic institutions of what we now call 'the economy'". He argues that "Debt was the hinge that made it possible to imagine money in anything like the modern sense", because slavery turned an absolute debt into a quantifiable, saleable claim, and that coinage was invented "to pay soldiers" rather than to solve the inconveniences of barter. His periodisation alternates virtual credit money and metallic bullion across five eras, from the first agrarian empires through the Axial Age, the Middle Ages, the age of European empires and the era after 1971. His conclusion is that "neither states nor markets can exist without the constant threat of force", and that older societies repeatedly cancelled debts to prevent the social breakdown caused by debt bondage. He notes that the Sumerian word *amarga*, which he identifies as the first known word for freedom, "literally means 'return to mother'" (Primary Theoretical).

The second contribution is the concept of the bullshit job. In the 2013 STRIKE! essay he named a class of jobs that, in his formulation, "secretly believe do not really need to be performed" and concluded: "These are what I propose to call 'bullshit jobs'." In the book he refined this three times. The provisional definition was "a form of employment that is so completely pointless, unnecessary, or pernicious that even the employee cannot justify its existence." The second added the element of pretence. The final working definition is "a form of paid employment that is so completely pointless, unnecessary, or pernicious that even the employee cannot justify its existence even though, as part of the conditions of employment, the employee feels obliged to pretend that this is not the case." He pairs this with a contrasting category: "Shit jobs are usually not at all bullshit; they typically involve work that needs to be done and is clearly of benefit to society; it's just that the workers who do them are paid and treated badly" (Primary Theoretical).

**A warning on terminology.** The phrase "socially necessary work" could not be verified as Graeber's own defined term. Searches of the opened full texts of *Bullshit Jobs*, *Fragments*, *Direct Action* and *The Democracy Project* found no such phrase used by him. What is verified is his distinction between bullshit jobs and work that "needs to be done and is clearly of benefit to society", his statement that "there is such a thing as social value, as apart from mere market value, but... no one has ever figured out an adequate way to measure it", and his answer in a 2018 interview that "Any rational economic system would redistribute the necessary work in a reasonable way and everybody would work less." The term "socially necessary" is standard in Marx's theory of value, and appears in a later philosopher's description of "burdensome work", not in Graeber's definitions; it must not be attributed to him as a coined concept (Primary Theoretical; gap recorded below).

The third contribution is the theory of bureaucracy. In the Harper's excerpt from *The Utopia of Rules* he proposes the Iron Law of Liberalism: "Any market reform or government initiative intended to reduce red tape and promote market forces will ultimately increase the number of regulations and bureaucrats, as well as the amount of paperwork, that the government employs." He names the fusion of public and private rule "total bureaucratization": "the gradual fusion of public and private power, which becomes rife with rules and regulations whose ultimate purpose is to extract wealth in the form of profits". In *Fragments* he links bureaucracy to violence and what he calls structural stupidity: "violence has always been the favored recourse of the stupid", and "Contrary to popular belief, bureaucracies do not create stupidity. They are ways of managing situations that are already inherently stupid because they are, ultimately, based on the arbitrariness of force." In the Salon interview he explains the attraction: rules are like a game in which "everybody knows exactly what the rules are", but the cost is that "bureaucracy is a way of crushing the human imagination. It also makes people stupid" (Primary Theoretical).

The fourth contribution is an ethnography and theory of direct action. *Direct Action: An Ethnography* documents the organising and protests against the 2001 Summit of the Americas in Québec City. He says the book has "no particular argument... unless it's, that the movement described within is well worth thinking about", and describes his method as one in which "the general is in the service of the particular". His own definition follows: "direct action is the insistence, when faced with structures of unjust authority, on acting as if one is already free. One does not solicit the state. One does not even necessarily make a grand gesture of defiance. Insofar as one is capable, one proceeds as if the state does not exist." He links this to prefiguration: activists wish to "build a new society in the shell of the old", and "in its essence" direct action embodies the ends in the means (Primary Theoretical).

## Analysis of capitalism

Graeber treats capitalism as a system whose moral claims are sustained by violence and by disciplined work rather than by efficiency. In *Debt* he argues that the market and the state share a foundation in conquest, that the language of freedom conceals a history of slavery and debt peonage, and that the institution of wage labour "has historically emerged from within that of slavery". His periodisation ends with an "empire of debt" after 1971, in which "national economies are driven largely by consumer debt" and financialisation detaches speculation "from any immediate relation with production or even commerce". He notes that earlier ages of virtual money created institutions to protect debtors, whereas the current age has created "the first effective planetary administrative system, operating through the IMF, World Bank, corporations and other financial institutions, largely in order to protect the interests of creditors" (Primary Theoretical).

In *The Democracy Project* he links this to domestic politics: "financialization has really meant is collusion between government and financial institutions to ensure that a larger and larger proportion of citizens fall deeper and deeper in debt", including student loans and municipal fines, and he calls the resulting system "mafia capitalism" (Primary Theoretical). In the 2013 bullshit-jobs essay he calls the moral and psychological damage "a scar across our collective soul" and asks rhetorically what sort of work regime would be "perfectly suited to maintaining the power of finance capital". His answer is a stratified labour market in which "Real, productive workers are relentlessly squeezed and exploited" while a stratum of salaried employees is "basically paid to do nothing, in positions designed to make them identify with the perspectives and sensibilities of the ruling class". He states the pay anomaly as a general rule: "the more obviously one's work benefits other people, the less one is likely to be paid for it" (Primary Theoretical).

In *Bullshit Jobs* and the 2020 policy essay he traces the mechanism to what he calls "managerial feudalism": the addition of layers of intermediaries whose status depends on the number of subordinates they command. An efficiency expert at a bank told him that 80 per cent of staff in a large bank were unnecessary and that none of his fifteen years of proposed reforms was adopted because each would have cost an executive some underlings. He cites a 2017 paper by Lockwood, Nathanson and Weyl finding that medical researchers added about nine dollars of social value per dollar paid while finance workers subtracted about 1.80 dollars per dollar of compensation, and he reproduces a table of estimated social value destroyed or created by named job types. He concedes that "There can be no objective measure of social value", yet argues that the worker's own judgement is "about as close as one is likely to get to an accurate assessment" because "Social value, after all, is largely just what people think it is" (Primary Theoretical).

## Analysis of socialism

Graeber wrote no blueprint for a socialist state, and no detailed plan for a planning apparatus or a party programme was found in the opened sources. His preferred institutions are built from below. In *Fragments* he presents Marcel Mauss as the decisive example: "Mauss believed socialism could never be built by state fiat but only gradually, from below", and revolutionaries should look to existing practices for "viable alternatives to capitalism" (Primary Theoretical). He endorses the idea of creating institutions "within the shell of the old" and treats consensus process, affinity groups and spokescouncils as practical forms of self-government rather than as symbols. *Direct Action* defines the strategy as acting as if one were already free; *The Democracy Project* describes the Occupy general assemblies as an attempt "to create a New York General Assembly, a body that could act as a model of genuine, direct democracy to contrapose to the corrupt charade presented to us as 'democracy' by the US government" (Primary Theoretical).

His economic proposals are similarly anti-bureaucratic. In the policy essay he distinguishes three versions of universal basic income and prefers the left-wing one, "about entirely severing livelihood from work", which he calls "economic freedom". He rejects a job guarantee as an alternative because "historically, such programmes always create even more bullshit jobs"; at most he would allow a job guarantee on top of basic income, since no one would then be forced to take a pointless job. In the VOX interview he says: "I want a world where basic needs are provided. I call for basic income, but it doesn't have to be basic income. I simply want people to be free to decide for themselves how they want to contribute", and he looks forward to "a rebellion of what I call the 'caring class'". In the In These Times interview he calls basic income "the perfect leftist antibureaucratic policy" because it would remove officials who decide "whether you're really poor enough to deserve this" (Primary Theoretical).

His account of what institutions should do is therefore distributive and recognitional rather than statist: reduce or abolish pointless work, detach income from employment, value care and unpaid labour, and let people govern their own affairs in face-to-face assemblies. He acknowledges that "most Americans aren't anarchists" and that few would support dismantling the state, and he frames the task as widening "the sense of what was politically possible" rather than writing a constitution (Primary Theoretical).

## Recommended reading

- *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011) — the long history of debt as a relation of power, and the book that made debt cancellation a mainstream radical demand.
- *Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology* (2004) — the compact statement of his ethical anarchism, his theory of the state and his claim that anthropology and anarchism belong together.
- *Direct Action: An Ethnography* (2009) — the field evidence behind his theory of prefigurative politics and consensus decision-making.
- *The Utopia of Rules* (2015) — his account of bureaucracy, the Iron Law of Liberalism and why rule-bound systems appeal.
- *Bullshit Jobs: A Theory* (2018) — his most widely debated concept, with the final definition and the critique of "managerial feudalism".

## Intellectual influences

The opened texts support a specific list. Kropotkin's definition of anarchism is the epigraph of *Fragments*, and Graeber describes Kropotkin's account of cooperation as a challenge to social Darwinism (Primary Theoretical). Marcel Mauss and Pierre Clastres are treated as the two thinkers who "laid the groundwork for a theory of revolutionary counterpower"; Mauss's "Essay on the Gift" is described as the key text because it showed that gift economies were founded on "a refusal to calculate" rather than on primitive barter. Graeber also discusses Robert Graves, A. R. Radcliffe-Brown and Georges Sorel in the same genealogy (Primary Theoretical). In his autobiography he names Terry Turner as "one of my most inspiring teachers at Chicago" and Marshall Sahlins as "another inspiring former mentor" who invited him to contribute to the pamphlet series that became *Fragments*; he later co-wrote *On Kings* with Sahlins. Sahlins's *Stone Age Economics* is cited in his 2020 policy essay for the observation that people in many societies work three or four hours a day (Primary Theoretical). Michael Hudson is credited in the *Debt* extract with pointing out that lending at interest spread from Mesopotamia without its original checks and balances; Geoffrey Ingham's phrase "military-coinage complex" is adopted and amended there. He engages Foucault critically in *Fragments*, rejecting the idea that brute force has ceased to matter, and engages Weber, von Mises, Durkheim and Hayek in the bureaucracy essays (Primary Theoretical).

## Influence and legacy

Graeber's most concrete legacy is the vocabulary that entered movements and then scholarship. "We are the 99%" became the slogan of Occupy Wall Street; he insists it was a "collective creation" rather than his alone (Primary Theoretical). The term "bullshit jobs" generated enough academic literature that Laurent Gauthier could write in 2025 that empirical studies in sociology and economics find 10 to 40 per cent of jobs qualifying under Graeber's definition and that his paper was the first formal theoretical account, adding that "Graeber's work has not been addressed by economists" (Scholarly Secondary). A 2022 study in *Work, Employment and Society* tested his propositions against the European Working Conditions Surveys, and a 2023 article in the same journal tested them against US data (Scholarly Secondary; full journal pages not opened). His idea of a "caring class" and his call to detach income from work are cited in discussions of universal basic income and the post-work left (Primary Theoretical). LSE marked his death with a departmental condolence and a tribute seminar series in 2021 (Primary Official; Primary Theoretical registry). The *Dawn of Everything* debate with Ian Morris, Kwame Anthony Appiah, Peter Kulchyski and Jane Bassett shows that his collaboration with Wengrow reshaped argument about early inequality even among critics (Scholarly Secondary).

## Criticisms and controversies

The Yale non-renewal remains contested. Graeber's own account attributes it to dissatisfaction with his scholarship while noting that colleagues suspected his activism was relevant; the opened pages do not include the university's own explanation (Primary Theoretical). In the LSE condolence, anthropologist Stephan Feuchtwang wrote in a comment that Graeber "by choosing anarchism... avoided dealing with state power", an objection from within his own discipline that deserves to be recorded alongside the praise (Primary Official page, comment by a named academic).

Economist Tyler Cowen accepted that "not everyone is productive, or has a productive job" but argued that Graeber "too often confuses 'tough jobs in negative- or zero-sum games' with 'bullshit jobs'". Cowen doubted that Oxford needs only a dozen PR specialists and suggested that Graeber spend a year managing a 60–80 person organisation without an HR department: "At that point we'll see who really has the bullshit job" (Scholarly Secondary; opened via the author's site). The strongest empirical objection comes from Magdalena Soffia, Alex Wood and Brendan Burchell. The University of Cambridge's account reports that Graeber proposed 20 to 60 per cent of jobs being bullshit and a rapid increase, whereas the European Working Conditions Surveys found 4.8 per cent of EU workers saying they rarely or never did useful work, and 5.6 per cent in the UK and Ireland; the share fell from 7.8 per cent in 2005 to 4.8 per cent in 2015. Occupations such as refuse collectors and cleaners scored higher on the "not useful" measure than legal and administration professionals. Wood is quoted as saying "very few of the key propositions in Graeber's theory can be sustained", while Burchell concedes that Graeber "was right to link people's attitudes towards their jobs to their psychological wellbeing". The researchers explain the feeling of uselessness by poor management, lack of respect, time pressure and inability to use one's ideas, and title their paper "Alienation Is Not 'Bullshit'" (Scholarly Secondary, opened Cambridge page; journal article page returned 403). One detail in the Cambridge summary is wrong: it says the 2013 essay appeared in *The Democracy Project*, whereas the opened STRIKE! page shows it was published in that magazine. Simon Walo's 2023 article found support for Graeber's theory in US data but concluded that his assumption that workers are usually right about their social usefulness is "somewhat overstated" (Scholarly Secondary; seen in search-result excerpt, article page not opened).

Marxist criticism takes several forms. Jason Read welcomes the attack on the barter myth but concludes that "a true history of debt needs to also examine the structures that are indifferent to those ideas", because capitalism is a matter of surplus value, labour power and real abstractions rather than of motives and intentions; "communism may be the foundation of all sociability, but capitalism is often indifferent to the sociability, or, worse still, exploits it" (Scholarly Secondary). In a review of *The Dawn of Everything*, Peter Kulchyski argues that the book "dismisses key theoretical approaches (mode of production; the structure and agency dialectic) without really offering a coherent alternative", that its left-anarchist politics lead to a "dangerous naïveté" about unconstrained agency, and that its treatment of Kandiaronk and of the Jesuit Relations is not the empirical evidence the authors claim it to be (Scholarly Secondary). Jane Bassett, writing in *International Socialism*, objects to "the authors' explicit, highly problematic decision not to explore the lives of early modern humans before about 30,000 years ago" and to the failure to account for the rise of the state, class society and women's oppression, which she connects to "an underlying, though generally inexplicit, rejection of Marxist understandings of early human history" (Scholarly Secondary). Ian Morris, in the *American Journal of Archaeology*, calls *Dawn* "a well-researched book" and "probably the most important publishing event in archaeology for decades", but argues that the authors never specify a threshold at which evolutionary accounts would be falsified, avoid measurement and continuous narrative, and that "There is just no method here" — "its arguments run more on rhetoric than on method" (Scholarly Secondary).

Anthropologist Keith Hart is broadly admiring but offers a different account of capitalism. He argues that rent-seeking now trumps value added through production, that Marx and Engels tied profit to industrial production in a way that no longer fits, and that "Has the focus of political economy tipped away from industrial production... towards rents derived from political privilege?" Hart also says Graeber "by-passed all this to resurrect the Victorian polymath and the world thanked him profusely for it", distinguishing Graeber's synthesis from the anthropology of unequal society tradition in which Hart places him (Scholarly Secondary). In the *New York Review of Books* exchange, Wengrow replied to Appiah's charge that the book distorts its archaeological sources, while Appiah maintained that "when what they present as a summary of the archaeology is at variance with the scholarship they cite", the authors' conclusions about Indus cities, Teotihuacan and the Ukrainian mega-sites are overstated (Scholarly Secondary). On Occupy, political scientist John Ehrenberg argues that Graeber "articulated three interrelated positions that became central to OWS's stance" — direct democracy and horizontalism, refusal to engage established institutions, and prefiguration — and that these "antipolitical positions" meant that the movement raised the issue of inequality but "did not do much about it beyond talking about it" (Scholarly Secondary).

## Related thinkers

Marcel Mauss and Pierre Clastres are the two figures Graeber identifies as having already produced fragments of an anarchist anthropology; Mauss's gift economy and Clastres's account of societies organised against the emergence of the state are his positive models (Primary Theoretical). Kropotkin supplies the definition of anarchism he quotes, and Bakunin is invoked as a translator of Marx who demonstrates that the two traditions are not simply enemies (Primary Theoretical). Marshall Sahlins was his teacher and co-author, and the "original affluent society" argument in *Stone Age Economics* underpins Graeber's claim that shorter working hours would not destroy people (Primary Theoretical). Michael Hudson and Geoffrey Ingham are the economists whose work on credit, money and war he draws on in *Debt* (Primary Theoretical). He sets himself against Ludwig von Mises and F. A. Hayek on bureaucracy while acknowledging that they described the administrative requirements of markets accurately; Weber and Durkheim are sources he reinterprets (Primary Theoretical). Keith Hart, Jason Read and Ian Morris stand as the anthropologist, Marxist and archaeologist who have engaged his work most substantively in the opened criticism (Scholarly Secondary). David Wengrow is his co-author on *The Dawn of Everything*; their division of labour is described by Penguin as comparative archaeology and anthropology respectively (Primary Official). Within this collection, his concerns overlap with Silvia Federici on social reproduction and unpaid care, David Harvey on the urban and capitalist dynamics of accumulation, and Mark Fisher on the cultural politics of work, though those connections are interpretive and not asserted by Graeber in the sources opened here.

## Facts not verified

- **Exact date of birth.** The opened sources give "1961–2020" and age 59 at death. The 12 February 1961 date appeared in a Telegraph search-result excerpt, not in an opened page, so it is not established here. The date of death, 2 September 2020, is established by the opened Penguin author note.
- **"Socially necessary work" as his term.** No occurrence of that exact phrase as a Graeber-defined concept was found in the opened full texts. His verified formulations are "social value" as distinct from market value, "shit jobs" as work that needs to be done, and "necessary work" in the 2018 interview. The phrase must not be presented as a term he coined or defined.
- **Title of the PhD thesis.** The author's autobiography calls *Lost People* his first book and says it was effectively written in 1997; a Telegraph search-result excerpt calls it his PhD thesis. That identification was not verified in an opened institutional or publisher record.
- **Yale's formal reason for non-renewal.** Only Graeber's own account and the existence of a support campaign were opened; no letter or statement from Yale was retrieved.
- **Publication year of *Pirate Enlightenment*.** The author's index says 2019, the publisher's on-sale date is 24 January 2023. The discrepancy is recorded, not resolved. The same index lists *The Democracy Project* as 2012 while the book's own copyright page says 2013.
