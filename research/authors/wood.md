## Biography

Ellen Meiksins Wood was born in New York City on 12 April 1942, the daughter of Latvian Jewish parents who had been active in the Bund, the Jewish socialist organisation, and who had fled Latvia as political refugees. She told Christopher Phelps in 1998 that her parents arrived in the United States in 1941, after her father, a leader of a socialist youth organisation, was about to be arrested as Latvia's indigenous fascists came to power; a granduncle edited the Jewish socialist magazine *Der Vecker*, and her mother later worked for the Jewish Labor Committee and the United Nations relief effort. Robert Brenner, her friend and co-thinker, adds that her parents fled Latvia for Paris in 1940 and then France for Portugal before reaching the United States, and that her father worked as a chief interpreter at the United Nations and described himself as a Marxist, a Menshevik and a radical analyst. (Monthly Review interview, **Primary Theoretical**; York University obituary and Broadbent Institute, **Primary Official**; Brenner tribute, **Primary Theoretical**.)

She received a BA in Slavic languages from the University of California, Berkeley in 1962 and a PhD in political science from UCLA in 1970. She arrived in Canada in 1967 with her first husband, Neal Wood, and taught political science at York University in Toronto until 1996. She later said that York's political science department became, "for some peculiar reason", probably the most Marxist department in North America, and that she could not have done her work in an American university. She served on the editorial committee of *New Left Review* from 1984 to 1993 and, after leaving it, joined *Monthly Review* as coeditor in March 1997 with Harry Magdoff and Paul Sweezy, becoming its first new editor in thirty years. She was inducted into the Royal Society of Canada in 1996. Neal Wood died in 2003; she married Ed Broadbent, the former New Democratic Party leader, in 2014. She died of cancer at her home in Ottawa on 14 January 2016, aged 73. (York University obituary and Monthly Review Press notice, **Primary Official**; Monthly Review interview, **Primary Theoretical**.)

A date conflict should be recorded. Both the York obituary and the 1999 interview give 1988 for the Isaac Deutscher Memorial Prize for *The Retreat from Class*. A tertiary summary gives 1986; that source is not used as evidence here. The 1988 date is the one supported by the opened first-party records.

## Intellectual tradition

Wood's sustained intellectual project was to separate capitalism from the longer history of markets, commerce and "modernity". In the 1999 interview she said: "If there has been one single project in all my intellectual work and in my teaching, it has been to identify the specificity of capitalism." She wanted readers to absorb the idea that capitalism is "a historically specific social form, which means that it has a beginning and an end." The same interview describes her as "closer to an earlier left" than to her own generation: she found the British first New Left of E. P. Thompson and John Saville, and the *Monthly Review* of Sweezy and Magdoff, more convincing than the second New Left or the later postmodernist left, because they kept the working class and capitalism at the centre. (Monthly Review interview, **Primary Theoretical**.)

The label "political Marxism" was not originally hers. According to Brenner, the Marxist historian Guy Bois coined it as a criticism of Brenner and Wood, accusing them of a "voluntarist vision of history" in which class struggle was detached from objective conditions. Wood accepted the term: Brenner records that she replied that if Bois wanted to call their approach political Marxism, that was his prerogative; what mattered was the different way the economic and the political connect in capitalist and pre-capitalist societies. The term therefore names a dispute as much as a school. Wood's own position was that in pre-capitalist societies economic reproduction was politically constituted — peasants held land through village communities that defended possession, and lords extracted surplus through political, judicial and military power — whereas under capitalism both appropriators and producers depend on markets and the state's role is to secure the property order within which market dependence operates. (Brenner tribute, **Primary Theoretical**; Wood, "A Response to Critics on Capitalist Origins", **Primary Theoretical**.)

She was sharply critical of post-Marxism and postmodernism. *The Retreat from Class* attacked what she called the New "True" Socialism: the attempt to detach politics and ideology from class, to replace the working class with new social movements as the revolutionary subject, and to treat "democracy" as a socially indeterminate principle compatible with capitalism. In a 1987 reply to Alex Callinicos she insisted that there is "no such thing as a socially indeterminate 'democracy'", and that socialism requires a "river of fire" between capitalist and socialist democracy rather than an extension of the one into the other. At the same time she rejected the charge of ignoring race and gender: "Socialists have to have a deep-seated commitment to all forms of human emancipation, period." (Wood, "A reply to *Looking for alternatives to reformism*", *International Socialism* 2:35, Summer 1987, **Primary Theoretical**; Monthly Review interview, **Primary Theoretical**.)

## Major contributions

Wood made four arguments that have shaped subsequent debate. The first is the specificity thesis: capitalism is not the maturation of trade but a distinct social form with its own "rules for reproduction". She defined it in *The Origin of Capitalism* as "a system in which goods and services, down to the most basic necessities of life, are produced for profitable exchange, where even human labour-power is a commodity for sale in the market, and where all economic actors are dependent on the market." (Wood, *The Origin of Capitalism: A Longer View*, Verso 2002, **Primary Theoretical**.)

The second is her account of how that system began: not in towns and long-distance commerce, but in the transformation of English agrarian property relations. Landlords and tenants became dependent on competitive rents and on the market for their own reproduction; market imperatives preceded industrialisation and a mass proletariat rather than following from them. In her 2002 reply to critics she summarised the thesis in one sentence: "Capitalism is a system in which producing and appropriating classes are dependent on the market for the conditions of their self-reproduction." (Wood, "A Response to Critics on Capitalist Origins", **Primary Theoretical**.)

The third concerns democracy. Capitalism, she argued, made universal citizenship possible while making it less important, because exploitation now occurs through the "economic" sphere rather than through the direct political power of a lord. Formal political rights therefore do not touch the coercion of the market. (Monthly Review interview; Wood, *Democracy Against Capitalism*, Cambridge University Press 1995, **Primary Theoretical**.)

The fourth concerns empire. In *Empire of Capital* and her 2003 essay she distinguished older empires, which ruled by direct coercion and tribute, from a distinctly capitalist imperialism that works mainly through market dependence, finance, debt and control by states. She called the United States "the first truly capitalist empire" and described its global project as a "global economic order, administered by a system of multiple local states", sustained by a doctrine of "war without end, in purpose or time". (Wood, "Capitalist Empire and the Nation State", *Against the Current* 106, September–October 2003, **Primary Theoretical**; Verso publisher description, **Primary Official**.)

## Analysis of capitalism

Wood's central target was what she called the "commercialization model". In the opening chapter of *The Origin of Capitalism* she described it as the view that capitalism is "the natural outcome of human practices almost as old as the species itself", an "expansion of markets and the growing commercialization of economic life" in which the forms of exchange practised since antiquity simply mature once political and cultural obstacles are removed. Her objection is not that trade is irrelevant. It is that this model treats the market as an opportunity rather than an imperative. "In other words, the commercialization model made no acknowledgement of imperatives specific to capitalism", including competition, profit maximisation and compulsory reinvestment, and so "no need in the commercialization model to explain the emergence of capitalism at all. It assumed that capitalism had existed, at least in embryo, from the dawn of history." That makes the argument circular: it presupposes a universal profit-seeking rationality in order to explain the historical appearance of profit-seeking compulsion. (Wood, *The Origin of Capitalism*, chapter 1, **Primary Theoretical**.)

She followed Robert Brenner against Paul Sweezy in the Dobb–Sweezy transition debate, but argued that Dobb and Hilton had not fully escaped the same trap by locating capitalism in the "interstices" of feudalism waiting to be released. The successful critics, in her account, explained how producers were deprived of non-market access to the means of reproduction, so that both landlords and tenants had to respond to competition. "The great strength of Brenner's historical argument", she wrote, "is that it emphasizes the specificity of the process that brought capitalism into being, with its new and historically specific economic logic." She accepted that England's insertion into international trade mattered, and criticised Brenner for leaving that and the European state system under-explained, but insisted that "no amount of trade, however extensive and profitable, would have produced capitalism without the transformation of social property relations". (Wood, *The Origin of Capitalism*, chapters 2 and 3, **Primary Theoretical**; "A Response to Critics on Capitalist Origins", **Primary Theoretical**.)

The positive account is agrarian. Capitalism arose in the countryside, in the specific English system of leasehold, competitive rents and improving landlords, well before industrialisation. Market dependence came first; the mass of propertyless wage labourers was the outcome, not the starting point. She drew on E. P. Thompson's account of the imposition of market society and on Karl Polanyi's analysis of non-capitalist trade to show that commerce could be extensive without generating capitalist imperatives. Racu, summarising her argument, notes that Florence and the Netherlands were more commercially advanced than England and did not produce capitalism. Her conclusion draws political consequences from the history. Capitalism is "a late and localized product of very specific historical conditions"; its expansion is driven by its own need for self-expansion, not by human nature or "the West's" superiority. Its unique productive capacity has always been bound to dispossession, environmental damage and the subordination of use to profit. (Wood, *The Origin of Capitalism*, chapters 4–5 and Conclusion, **Primary Theoretical**; Racu's scholarly reading of the same argument, **Scholarly Secondary**.)

In *Democracy Against Capitalism* she called for the same separation in theory: against technological determinism and against what she saw as Weber's teleology, historical materialism should start from the systemic unity *and* the historicity of capitalism. One of her formulations in the conclusion ties the economic and political arguments together: "The imperatives of competition, profit maximization and accumulation were set in train, for example, the moment English tenant farmers were denied non-market access to land". The market is a political space as well as an economic one. (Wood, *Democracy Against Capitalism*, Introduction and Conclusion, **Primary Theoretical**.)

## Analysis of socialism

Wood wrote no blueprint for a socialist constitution or a fully specified economic plan, and she said so. What she offered was a set of negative criteria and a direction of travel. In the conclusion to *Democracy Against Capitalism* she argued that the old choice between market and central planning was "barren", because both had been driven by accumulation rather than by the producers' own purposes, and neither had made production democratically accountable. She rejected "market socialism" for the same reason she rejected the commercialisation model: if the market remains the regulator of social reproduction, it imposes competition, redundancy and self-exploitation even on workers who own the means of production collectively. In *The Origin of Capitalism* she put the point bluntly: "there can be no such thing as a truly 'social' or democratic market, let alone a 'market socialism'." (Wood, *Democracy Against Capitalism*, Conclusion; *The Origin of Capitalism*, Conclusion, **Primary Theoretical**.)

Her positive proposal was to "reconceive" democracy "not simply as a political category but as an economic one" — democracy as the driving mechanism of the economy, not merely a distributional add-on. She took Marx's "free association of direct producers" as a starting point, insisted that reappropriation of the means of production by producers was necessary but not sufficient, and posed the unresolved question of how democratically organised enterprises could be coordinated with one another and with the wider community. In the 1999 interview she stated the political premise in a single sentence: "if it doesn't happen by means of the self-emancipation of the working class, it's not going to happen at all." She defended the welfare state and environmental regulation as struggles worth fighting, while denying that a capitalism with a human face was a stable alternative; her book ends by saying that "there remains the real alternative of socialism." (Wood, *Democracy Against Capitalism*, Conclusion; Monthly Review interview; *The Origin of Capitalism*, Conclusion, **Primary Theoretical**.)

## Intellectual influences

Her parents' Bundism and her father's Marxism are the earliest influences she named herself, alongside her granduncle's socialist editorship and her mother's work for the Jewish Labor Committee; she said she "grew up surrounded by left-wing ideas" and that her father remained a Marxist and a *Monthly Review* reader. (Monthly Review interview, **Primary Theoretical**; Brenner tribute, **Primary Theoretical**.)

Within Marxism, she acknowledged a long list in the acknowledgements to *Democracy Against Capitalism*: Neal Wood, George Comninel, Robert Brenner, Perry Anderson, Ralph Miliband, E. P. Thompson's circle including John Saville, Harvey Kaye, Norman Geras, Leo Panitch and others. Brenner read three or four of the original essays and was, in her words, an "invaluable" interlocutor; Miliband's "firm and lucid socialist commitment" was an example she explicitly invoked. In *The Origin of Capitalism* she thanked Brenner and Comninel for "safety checks" on the revised edition, and recorded her debt to Dobb, Hilton, Sweezy, Polanyi, Thompson and Weber as arguments to adopt or answer. (Wood, *Democracy Against Capitalism*, Acknowledgements, **Primary Theoretical**; *The Origin of Capitalism*, Acknowledgements and notes, **Primary Theoretical**.)

The theoretical lineage she claimed was Marx's critique of political economy rather than "stagist" Marxism or classical political economy; she repeatedly treated Adam Smith, Weber and Enlightenment progress theory as representatives of the naturalising assumptions she opposed, and Marx's chapter on "the so-called primitive accumulation" as the key text for reading the transition as a transformation of social property relations. (Wood, *Democracy Against Capitalism*, Introduction; Wood, "A Response to Critics on Capitalist Origins", **Primary Theoretical**.)

## Influence and legacy

Wood's book-length argument became a reference point for the "political Marxist" current associated with Brenner, and the label itself was retained by its opponents and practitioners. The Broadbent Institute's annual Ellen Meiksins Wood Prize and Lecture, established after her death, awards $10,000 to an academic, labour activist or writer and describes its criteria as her "two-fold belief" that democracy is fought for from below and that democratic equality conflicts with capitalist outcomes. Lecturers listed by the institute include Paul Mason (2018), Barbara Ehrenreich (2019), Leo Gerard (2020), Armine Yalnizyan (2023), Isabella Weber (2024), Grace Blakeley (2025) and Bhaskar Sunkara (2026). (Broadbent Institute, **Primary Official**.)

Her work has continued to generate scholarly assessment. Larry Patriquin edited *The Ellen Meiksins Wood Reader* for Brill in 2012, with a bibliography of her work from 1970 to 2012. Frances Abele, George Comninel and Peter Meiksins published "Socialism and democracy: the political engagements of Ellen Meiksins Wood" in *Studies in Political Economy* in 2016 (DOI 10.1080/07078552.2016.1249124; only the abstract and metadata were opened). Samuel Knafo and Benno Teschke's "Political Marxism and the Rules of Reproduction of Capitalism: A Historicist Critique" (*Historical Materialism*, 2020, DOI 10.1163/1569206x-00001441) and Teddy Paikin and Sam Salour's "In the Tracks of Political Marxism: Eurocentrism, Imperialism, and Capitalist Development" (*Historical Materialism*, 2026, DOI 10.1163/1569206x-bja10111) both take the current she helped define as their object; abstracts were opened, full texts were not. (Crossref and Semantic Scholar registration metadata, **Primary Official** for the records; journal articles, **Scholarly Secondary** by category.)

Corey Robin wrote in *Jacobin* that reading *The Origin of Capitalism* was an "Aha!" moment and that Wood "breathed life into Marxist political theory". The Verso page reproduces tributes from Adrienne Rich, Vivek Chibber, David McNally, Eric Hobsbawm and Robert Brenner. These are tributes by named writers, not independent verification of her conclusions. (Jacobin obituary and Verso publisher page, **Scholarly Secondary** and **Primary Official** respectively.)

## Criticisms and controversies

The most detailed scholarly criticism opened for this dossier is Alexandru Racu's "Four Ways of Viewing Modernity: A Critical Reading of Ellen Meiksins Wood's *Origin of Capitalism*", published in the SEEJPS journal at volume IV, number 1 (the full expansion of the journal name was not verified from the opened page). Racu accepts that Wood destroys the commercialisation account but argues that her book "explains more the way in which capitalism did not appear (dismantling the dominating conception of its supposed commercial origin) than the way in which it did appear". If the English landed aristocracy's relatively weak extra-economic powers explain the turn to economic surplus extraction, he asks, what explains that weakness? His second objection is logical: from the fact that capitalism is not written into human nature it does not follow that it can be superseded; it might still be an "unsurpassable cage". Third, he argues that her contrast between a good French democratic modernity and a bad English capitalist modernity is too neat, and that she does not confront the record of actually existing socialist states. (Racu, SEEJPS article, **Scholarly Secondary**.)

A second controversy concerns Eurocentrism and the role of colonialism. Christopher McAuley, writing in *Against the Current*, accepted much of the "Brenner-Wood thesis" but argued that it understated medieval and early modern trade, misdated English agricultural innovation by reading eighteenth-century developments into the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, and treated capitalism's "first appearance" in a way James Blaut called mythical — arriving "complete and entire, as though it were a god descending from Olympus". He also argued that the account excluded the labour, resources and "forward linkages" of the non-European world and ultimately accused Brenner and Wood of "intellectual racism". Wood replied that the accusation was a non-sequitur and "disgraceful", that trade was a necessary condition of English capitalism but not a sufficient one, that enclosure and competitive rents were visible in the sixteenth century, and that the contrast she drew was between England and the rest of Europe as well as the non-European world, not Europe versus non-Europe. Peter Drucker, in the same exchange, argued that she should have examined the contingent role of colonial wealth in capitalism's later industrial triumph and misread Marx on primitive accumulation; Wood replied that her subject was the origin, not the maturation, and quoted Marx's chapter 26 in support of her reading. (McAuley, "A Comment on Capitalist Origins", and Wood, "A Response to Critics on Capitalist Origins" and "A Response to Christopher McAuley's *On Capitalist Origins*", *Against the Current* 94, 97 and 101, **Scholarly Secondary** for McAuley and Drucker, **Primary Theoretical** for Wood.)

Two earlier disputes concern *The Retreat from Class*. Gavin Kitching replied in *New Left Review* I/163 (May–June 1987) that her criticisms of his *Rethinking Socialism* were "fundamentally misconceived", and used 1984 British labour-force and Family Expenditure Survey figures to argue that a large majority of workers had a material stake in the system and that a democratic socialist transformation would require their support. He also argued that her treatment of material interest versus morality was too abstract because it did not specify the historical conditions in which the two conflict. The underlying statistics come from the 1984 *Family Expenditure Survey*, which was not opened directly here. Alex Callinicos reviewed the book in *International Socialism* 2:34; Wood's reply says he accused her of conflating proletarian and capitalist democracy in "centrist" fashion, of misunderstanding working-class militancy and of targeting marginal theorists. Callinicos's review itself was not opened, so his case is reported here only as Wood represented it. (Kitching, NLR I/163, **Scholarly Secondary**; Wood, *International Socialism* 2:35, **Primary Theoretical**.)

Within political Marxism itself, Knafo and Teschke argue that the tradition contains an unresolved tension between a structural account of capitalism's laws of motion and a historicist account centred on power and conflict, and call for a more agency-based theory. Paikin and Salour trace the criticism that political Marxism ignores colonialism back to the 1970s transition and dependency debates, and defend Brenner and Wood against it. Only abstracts were opened, so these positions are stated at the level of the abstracts. (Crossref/Semantic Scholar records and abstracts, **Scholarly Secondary**.)

## Related thinkers

- **Robert Brenner** — close friend, co-thinker and the main source for her agrarian account of the transition; he coined the public framing of their shared project in his tribute, which records that Guy Bois's pejorative "political Marxism" was accepted by Wood. (Brenner tribute, **Primary Theoretical**.)
- **Neal Wood** — her first husband and coauthor on ancient political theory and English political thought; the 1978 *Class Ideology and Ancient Political Theory* and 1997 *A Trumpet of Sedition* were joint work. (Monthly Review interview, **Primary Theoretical**.)
- **Paul Sweezy and Harry Magdoff** — fellow editors at *Monthly Review*; she defended the journal's record on class and labour politics against the charge that it denied changes in capitalism. (Monthly Review interview, **Primary Theoretical**.)
- **E. P. Thompson, John Saville and Ralph Miliband** — the British first New Left whose retention of class politics and labour movement commitment she explicitly endorsed; Thompson's *The Making of the English Working Class* supplied her account of the implantation of market society. (Monthly Review interview; *Democracy Against Capitalism*, Acknowledgements and chapter 6, **Primary Theoretical**.)
- **Perry Anderson** — an interlocutor whom she thanked for reading several essays, and whose "deep paradox" objection to Brenner's account of merchants and bourgeois revolution she rejected as question-begging. (*Democracy Against Capitalism*, Acknowledgements; *The Origin of Capitalism*, chapter 3, **Primary Theoretical**.)
- **George Comninel** — former student and colleague whose work on the French Revolution supported her rejection of the "bourgeois revolution" concept. (*The Origin of Capitalism*, Acknowledgements and notes, **Primary Theoretical**.)
- **Karl Polanyi** — used for the argument that non-capitalist trade was regulated and socially embedded, so that commercialisation did not itself generate capitalist imperatives. (*The Origin of Capitalism*, chapter 1, **Primary Theoretical**.)
- **Adam Smith, Max Weber and Enlightenment progress theory** — treated as sources of the naturalising assumptions her work attacks, not as influences she adopted. (*The Origin of Capitalism*, Introduction and chapter 9; *Democracy Against Capitalism*, chapter 5, **Primary Theoretical**.)
- **Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri** — targets of her critique of "Empire"; she argued that their claim that sovereignty has no "place" politically disables organized working-class opposition. (Wood, "Capitalist Empire and the Nation State", **Primary Theoretical**.)
- **Alex Callinicos, Gavin Kitching, Christopher McAuley, James Blaut and Peter Drucker** — critics whose objections are recorded in the controversies section above. (Various, **Scholarly Secondary**.)
