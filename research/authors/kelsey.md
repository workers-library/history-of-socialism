## Biography

Jane Kelsey is a New Zealand legal academic and public intellectual. The University of Auckland lists her as Emeritus Professor in Law, with an ORCID identifier 0000-0003-1437-0080; that profile page did not render for this research and was seen only as a search excerpt (**Primary Official**, endpoint). The university's own news service says she taught at Auckland for four decades from 1979, describes her as a "self-described legal subversive", and records that a $2,500 annual award, Te Whainga i te Tika Award for Social Justice, was established on her retirement ([University of Auckland news](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2022/09/02/in-pursuit-of-social-justice-.html), **Primary Official**). Her Conversation profile gives the same starting year and says she retired at the end of 2021 ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/profiles/jane-kelsey-114083), **Primary Official**). The university's September 2022 news item speaks of a "recent retirement", and her valedictory lecture was delivered on 28 July 2022; the exact retirement date is therefore recorded differently in these institutional accounts (**Primary Official**).

Her own account supplies the biographical detail that publisher copy omits. In the 2022 valedictory lecture she says her father was from Hokianga and left school at fourteen at the start of the Depression to work as a barrow boy, later becoming Director of the Tourist Department; her mother's forebears were settlers in Taranaki. She describes growing up in Mt Wellington, a law degree at Victoria University where women were a small minority, a BCL at Oxford funded by her parents, and an M Phil in Criminology at Cambridge that she calls "the intellectual game changer". There she was taught by Colin Sumner and Alison Young and read Stuart Hall's *Policing the Crisis*, Carol Smart on women, crime and criminology, Alan Hunt on Marxism and law, and Foucault's *Discipline and Punish*. A sabbatical in 1984 placed her in Britain during the miners' strike and the Greenham Common peace camp at the same time as the Rogernomics revolution began in New Zealand ([valedictory lecture](https://e-tangata.co.nz/reflections/jane-kelsey-truth-to-power-the-critical-legal-academic-as-licensed-subversive), **Primary Theoretical**).

She was recruited to Auckland Law School in mid-1979 to teach criminology. She describes replacing Alan Nixon, a prison abolitionist whose teaching she later came to regard as compromised by eugenic ideas, and helping to transform the first-year legal system course with David Williams and later Ani Mikaere so that students met contemporary issues from contrasting theoretical perspectives, grounded in history and Te Tiriti o Waitangi. The same lecture records that she joined the Auckland Committee on Racism and Discrimination after a challenge from Ripeka Evans, worked with Māori liberation theologians on Tiriti-based analysis, and provided legal support to people facing prosecution over the Springbok tour and Bastion Point. Moana Maniapoto, who introduced the lecture, recalls Kelsey on the frontline of the 1981 anti-apartheid protests and filming the 2005 anti-WTO protests in Hong Kong (same source, **Primary Theoretical**). No birth date or birth year was found in any institutional, publisher or author record opened here.

## Intellectual tradition

Kelsey writes from what she calls a Gramscian school of Marxist theory. In her valedictory lecture she states directly: "My own standpoint draws heavily on the Gramscian school of Marxist theory — a nuance lost on adversaries who toss the 'M' word around as a form of invective." She explains that common law is a historical construct of the capitalist state whose material function is to maintain private property rights, and that for "contemporary Gramscians like myself" the class relations of capitalism are overlaid by colonialism, imperialism and the intersection of patriarchy, class, heteronormality, European culture and Christian belief (same source, **Primary Theoretical**). She does not write as a doctrinal economist or as a party theorist. Her method is socio-legal: the Bridget Williams Books author page describes her as committed to "socio-legal scholarship which brings law into contact with politics, economics, social justice, colonisation and international relations" ([BWB author page](https://www.bwb.co.nz/authors/jane-kelsey), **Primary Official**).

She rejects the idea that scholarship and activism are opposites. The title of her valedictory lecture came from a contribution to a festschrift for William Twining, who used the phrase "licensed subversive" for a scholar who questions the assumptions of liberal legalism. Kelsey argues that the role must go further than debate inside universities, and adopts Ranginui Walker's term "academic activism" for the combination of analysis, partisan commitment and organised work. She calls the roles of analytical scholar, research-informed partisan and academic activist "mutually reinforcing and fully consistent with the mission of a university" (same source, **Primary Theoretical**). Her institutional home remained a law school, but her subject matter was the political economy of the state, trade agreements and colonisation.

Bruce Curtis, a sociologist at the University of Waikato, places her in a specifically New Zealand radical tradition while criticising its limits. He calls Kelsey and the journalist Bruce Jesson "the paramount journalistic and scholarly critics of neoliberalism in New Zealand", and says Kelsey "was the author to bring the new 'New Zealand experiment' ... to international attention" ([Curtis](https://socialspacejournal.eu/11%20numer/Bruce%20Curtis%20-%20Class,%20neoliberalism%20and%20New%20Zealand.pdf), **Scholarly Secondary**). Brian Easton, a New Zealand economist who was himself a critic of the reforms, describes her less as a socialist theorist than as someone unwilling to prescribe a grand design from above: "she is not a technocrat or technopol" ([Easton](https://www.eastonbh.ac.nz/1997/07/ithe_new_zealand_experiment_a_model_for_world_structural_adjustmenti_rev/), **Scholarly Secondary**).

## Major contributions

The first contribution is the concept of the "New Zealand experiment" as a warning to other countries. Kelsey's 1995 book took the language of the IMF and World Bank's structural adjustment programmes and applied it to a rich OECD country that had voluntarily adopted the same fundamentals. In her 1999 summary she writes: "This was a classic structural adjustment program of the kind traditionally imposed on poorer countries of the Third World by the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. New Zealand did it voluntarily." She argues that the result was then promoted as "a success story and a model for the rest of the world" ([PMA article](http://www.converge.org.nz/pma/apfail.htm), **Primary Theoretical**). The book's chapter list moves from "Setting the Scene" and "Capturing the Political Machine" through "Empowering the Technocrats" and "Embedding the New Regime" to separate audits headed "The Economic Deficit", "The Social Deficit", "The Democratic Deficit" and "The Cultural Deficit", ending with "There Are Alternatives" and an appendix described as "A manual for counter-technopols" ([BWB book page](https://www.bwb.co.nz/books/the-new-zealand-experiment), **Primary Official**). Easton identifies the core thesis as the seizure of opportunity by technocrats and "technopols", or technocrats who take political responsibility, acting broadly independently of the people's wishes ([Easton](https://www.eastonbh.ac.nz/1997/07/ithe_new_zealand_experiment_a_model_for_world_structural_adjustmenti_rev/), **Scholarly Secondary**).

The second contribution is the analysis of how reforms are made permanent. Her 1996 lecture "The closure of critique" draws on Stephen Haggard and Robert Kaufman's distinction between the initiation and consolidation of structural adjustment. Initiation can be carried out by insulated change teams; consolidation requires an "ideational consensus", a coalition of private beneficiaries and what Haggard and Kaufman call "embedded autonomy". Kelsey argues that New Zealand reached consolidation: party politics, the senior public service, the media, the trade union leadership and sections of Māori society had been drawn into or silenced by the new regime. She describes the shift as being "from the colonial, corporatist welfare state to a colonial neoliberal one" bringing "new techniques of governance and new ways to police the silence" ([lecture](https://www.bmartin.cc/dissent/documents/Kelsey.html), **Primary Theoretical**). The 1995 book's "democratic deficit" chapter and the later *FIRE Economy* chapter on an "economic constitution" develop the same claim.

The third contribution is a doctrine of legal and constitutional embedding. In *The FIRE Economy* she calls finance, insurance and real estate the principal source of wealth creation and treats the neoliberal regime as its political condition: "Neoliberal ideology, rules and institutions acted first as the midwife and then as the guardian of this new economic order." She identifies the Reserve Bank of New Zealand Act 1989, the Public Finance Act 1989 and the Fiscal Responsibility Act 1994 as pieces of legislation through which the regime was made hard to reverse (author's introduction, [Scoop](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/HL1507/S00101/the-fire-economy-new-zealands-reckoning-by-jane-kelsey.htm), **Primary Theoretical**; a reviewer's assessment of the same chapters, [McDonagh](https://www.crimejusticejournal.com/article/view/885), **Scholarly Secondary**). McDonagh reports her phrase "embedded neoliberalism" and quotes her account of intellectuals who set out "to anticipate and neutralise" the "commitment problem", "political slippage" and "despotic democracy" (same review, **Scholarly Secondary**).

The fourth contribution connects domestic restructuring to international trade rules. *Reclaiming the Future* argues that globalisation and domestic restructuring were "flipsides of the same coin" and that the choices were made "on behalf of New Zealanders since 1984, sometimes without our knowledge and often without our consent" (Rankin review and Humphries review, **Scholarly Secondary**). Her later work on the General Agreement on Trade in Services and the Trans-Pacific Partnership extends that argument into the technical detail of treaty text, investor rights and the role of the executive in treaty negotiation. The Conversation profile lists *Serving Whose Interests?* (2008), *No Ordinary Deal* (2010) and *Hidden Agendas* (2013) as the books in that phase of her work ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/profiles/jane-kelsey-114083), **Primary Official**).

The fifth contribution is the analysis of Treaty consequences. Her first book dealt with the interface between Te Tiriti and the neoliberal revolution and was released in February 1990, the 150th anniversary of Te Tiriti, according to her own lecture; the title is not named in that passage, so the identification with *A Question of Honour?* is an inference, not a verified statement. Her doctoral research examined "the law's role in mediating the contradiction between the fourth Labour government's neoliberal project and its promise to honour the Treaty of Waitangi" (same source, **Primary Theoretical**). In the 1996 lecture she treats Māori resistance as the one sustained challenge the new regime found hard to control, and notes that Māori entrepreneurs and Māori nationalists were both drawn into the politics of the period (same source). In 2022 she describes a Waitangi Tribunal claim on the TPPA that brought together mātauranga Māori, constitutional analysis from Moana Jackson and her own legal analysis, and says the mediation result was MFAT support for an independent Māori entity, Ngā Toki Whakarururanga (same source, **Primary Theoretical**).

## Analysis of capitalism

Kelsey's account begins with the half-century shift from regulated industrial capitalism to financialised capitalism. In the introduction to *The FIRE Economy* she dates the fundamental shift to the 1970s, says finance displaced industry as the driver of wealth creation in affluent countries, and describes the global financial crisis as the event that exposed the system's dependence on borrowed money and asset inflation. She argues that New Zealand is especially exposed because successive Labour and National governments placed it "at the pure end of the neoliberal spectrum" and it was once known as "the Wild West of financial markets" ([author's introduction](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/HL1507/S00101/the-fire-economy-new-zealands-reckoning-by-jane-kelsey.htm), **Primary Theoretical**). McDonagh summarises her view of the mechanism: debt leveraging lets traders borrow, speculate and profit or lose at scales far beyond other economic activity, while the state's institutional fabric has been rebuilt to protect that arrangement ([McDonagh](https://www.crimejusticejournal.com/article/view/885), **Scholarly Secondary**).

In the 1980s and 1990s case she identifies five "fundamentals": market liberalisation and free trade, limited government, narrow monetarist policy, a deregulated labour market and fiscal restraint. She argues these were treated as common sense and beyond challenge. Her own figures in the 1999 article are presented as evidence of failure: between 1985 and 1992 OECD economies grew by an average 20 per cent while New Zealand's economy shrank by 1 per cent; productivity growth from 1984 to 1993 averaged about 0.9 per cent a year, largely through labour cutbacks; foreign debt quadrupled; and the number of people estimated to be living in poverty grew by at least 35 per cent between 1989 and 1992. These are Kelsey's compiled figures, not figures independently verified in this research ([PMA article](http://www.converge.org.nz/pma/apfail.htm), **Primary Theoretical**). She translates the political language into its effects: "short-term pain for long-term gain" meant "pain for the poor to achieve gain for the rich", while "price stability" meant interest-rate rises and suppressed growth and "fiscal responsibility" meant cuts to income support (same source, **Primary Theoretical**).

The power mechanism in her early work is a coalition of politicians, Treasury officials and business interests. The "blitzkrieg" strategy of the 1980s, she argues in 2022, was designed to burn off the academics, economists and journalists who would challenge the reforms, while think tanks such as the Business Roundtable and Centre for Independent Studies and the *National Business Review* supplied the opposing intellectual infrastructure. She names Brian Easton as someone who paid a career price, Susan St John as a persistent critic who was never made professor, and Bruce Jesson as a journalist whose early death deprived New Zealand of its leading critical voice ([valedictory lecture](https://e-tangata.co.nz/reflections/jane-kelsey-truth-to-power-the-critical-legal-academic-as-licensed-subversive), **Primary Theoretical**). In *The New Zealand Experiment* she gives the same coalition a theoretical form through the ideas of technocrats and technopols, and Easton reports that her quotation from John Williamson at a 1993 Institute for International Economics colloquium includes the claim that reform is easiest in the "honeymoon stage" after taking power and where opposition can be "discredited and disrupted, or repressed" ([Easton](https://www.eastonbh.ac.nz/1997/07/ithe_new_zealand_experiment_a_model_for_world_structural_adjustmenti_rev/), **Scholarly Secondary**).

She also treats law itself as part of the mechanism. In the 1996 lecture she argues that the shift from state regulation to contract law is presented as a technical change but is in fact a strategy of control: law reorganises the ownership and priorities of state agencies, turns citizens into private consumers, commodifies information and research, and redistributes the power to take part in decisions. Economic science, managerialism and contract law together form what she calls an intellectual barrier that confines debate to variations within the neoliberal paradigm ([lecture](https://www.bmartin.cc/dissent/documents/Kelsey.html), **Primary Theoretical**). Keith Rankin, reviewing *Reclaiming the Future*, identifies a related point: Kelsey distinguishes internal sovereignty, held by Parliament, from external sovereignty, exercised by the executive in secret treaty negotiation, and calls the existing conventions "the Achilles' heel of our nation's democracy" ([Rankin](http://keithrankin.co.nz/1999924revKelseyEP.html), **Scholarly Secondary**).

## Analysis of socialism

Kelsey does not provide a blueprint for a socialist state. *The FIRE Economy* says explicitly that it is "not so presumptuous as to offer a blueprint for the future", though it identifies where work must be done for a "socially progressive post-neoliberal world" ([author's introduction](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/HL1507/S00101/the-fire-economy-new-zealands-reckoning-by-jane-kelsey.htm), **Primary Theoretical**). Easton makes the same point about the much-criticised final chapter of *The New Zealand Experiment*: her alternatives are "grass (and flax) roots strategies which build up from the actions of individual people to lead to the new society" rather than a technocratic plan ([Easton](https://www.eastonbh.ac.nz/1997/07/ithe_new_zealand_experiment_a_model_for_world_structural_adjustmenti_rev/), **Scholarly Secondary**). This absence of a design is a feature of her argument: she distrusts reforms imposed by experts who claim to know what is good for other people.

The institutions she favours can still be identified. Hazledine reports that *At the Crossroads* proposes "renationalisation of core infrastructure and public services, without compensation where massive profit taking, asset stripping and degeneration has occurred", repudiation of Third World debt, and "democratisation of political life by resurrecting an activist and popularly accountable state ... and public forums for participatory debate and informed decision making at local, regional, national and international levels" ([Hazledine](https://nzbooks.org.nz/2001/non-fiction/the-missing-alternative-tim-hazledine/), **Scholarly Secondary**, quoting the book). The title of the book's third essay, "A requiem for TINA — Taking the high road", and the publisher's summary that it "rejects market-led approaches to nation-building, and argues for strategies that put people at the centre" give the direction of that proposal ([BWB book page](https://www.bwb.co.nz/books/at-the-crossroads), **Primary Official**).

Her theoretical frame for an alternative is Karl Polanyi's double movement: an unfettered market generates resistance because of the poverty and inequality it creates. Rankin notes that *Reclaiming the Future* is structured around Polanyi's 1944 account of the self-regulating market and the backlash against it ([Rankin](http://keithrankin.co.nz/1999924revKelseyEP.html), **Scholarly Secondary**), and Kelsey herself invokes Polanyi's *The Great Transformation* in the 2022 lecture and cites it in the introduction to *The FIRE Economy* (**Primary Theoretical**). She makes Tiriti-based constitutional transformation a condition of any acceptable alternative: in the same lecture she says academic activism in Aotearoa "must be informed by Te Tiriti" and warns that incremental reform can subvert transformation. She closes with Gramsci's interregnum, "The old is dying, but the new is yet to be born", and insists that what follows depends on organised resistance rather than on the automatic collapse of the old order (**Primary Theoretical**).

Keith Rankin reported in 1996 that her book outlined solutions including a universal basic income ([BIEN conference report](http://www.keithrankin.co.nz/krnknbien.html), **Scholarly Secondary**). That is a reviewer's description of the book, not a passage opened directly from Kelsey's text, so it should be attributed to Rankin rather than stated as her own formulation.

## Recommended reading

- *The New Zealand Experiment: A World Model for Structural Adjustment?* (1995; second edition 1997) — the core account of the reforms, their four claimed deficits and the technique of "embedding" them.
- *Rolling Back the State: Privatisation of Power in Aotearoa/New Zealand* (1993) — the earlier, detailed study of privatisation and the shift of power in the state; Easton calls it an excellent account of law and state over ten years.
- "The closure of critique" (1996) — the clearest statement of how she thinks dissent was silenced and the reforms consolidated.
- *Reclaiming the Future: New Zealand and the Global Economy* (1999) — connects domestic restructuring to trade rules, sovereignty and the Polanyi framework.
- *The FIRE Economy: New Zealand's Reckoning* (2015) — her later account of financialisation and the barriers to a post-neoliberal transformation.

## Intellectual influences

Her own lecture is the evidence for most of these connections. Antonio Gramsci supplies the distinction between traditional and organic intellectuals, which she uses to ask where a scholar aligned with groups she does not belong to stands. William Twining supplied the phrase "licensed subversive". Karl Polanyi's *The Great Transformation* supplies the double movement and the image of the interregnum. Paulo Freire's *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* was taught in workshops run by Māori liberation theologians Joy and Rua Rakena and Rob Cooper; she says it shaped a generation of Māori radicals. Ranginui Walker supplied the term "academic activism"; Moana Jackson supplied the warning against incrementalism that can subvert transformation. Her Cambridge teachers Colin Sumner, Alison Young and the reading of Stuart Hall, Carol Smart, Alan Hunt and Michel Foucault shaped her critical legal method, and Geoffrey Palmer encouraged her into postgraduate study. Bill Pearson's 1952 essay *Fretful Sleepers* supplies the frame for her 1996 argument about the silencing of critique (all from the [2022 lecture](https://e-tangata.co.nz/reflections/jane-kelsey-truth-to-power-the-critical-legal-academic-as-licensed-subversive), **Primary Theoretical**; the 1996 lecture for Pearson, **Primary Theoretical**). In *Reclaiming the Future* the organising influence is Polanyi, as reported by Keith Rankin ([Rankin](http://keithrankin.co.nz/1999924revKelseyEP.html), **Scholarly Secondary**).

## Influence and legacy

The institutional record of her legacy is unusually concrete. The University of Auckland established the Te Whainga i te Tika Award for Social Justice, worth $2,500 annually, on her retirement, and named it after the 1987 report she helped research and write. The university describes her as having mentored thousands of students and as one of the country's best-known critical legal academics ([University of Auckland news](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/news/2022/09/02/in-pursuit-of-social-justice-.html), **Primary Official**). The law school's valedictory event page lists the four areas of her academic activism — decolonisation and Te Tiriti, the political economy of public policy, globalisation, and universities as public institutions ([event page](https://www.auckland.ac.nz/en/law/events/emeritus-professor-kelsey-valedictory-lecture.html), **Primary Official**).

Her scholarly reach is visible in the way other writers use her terms. Curtis says she brought the "New Zealand experiment" to international attention and calls her, with Jesson, the leading critics of neoliberalism in New Zealand ([Curtis](https://socialspacejournal.eu/11%20numer/Bruce%20Curtis%20-%20Class,%20neoliberalism%20and%20New%20Zealand.pdf), **Scholarly Secondary**). The term appears as a standard label in later work by economists, including Cardow and Wilson's 2013 study of privatisation returns, which opens by saying the New Zealand economy was subject to "what has become known as the 'New Zealand experiment' (Kelsey, 1995)" ([study PDF](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/256778), **Scholarly Secondary**). A thesis on economic change in 1980s Taranaki describes *The New Zealand Experiment* as "particularly well known to university lecturers and students alike" ([Massey University thesis](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/81b6e3fc-5dd3-4f00-9c90-2074002f6ff3/content), **Scholarly Secondary**). OpenAlex reported 209 citations for *Rolling Back the State* at the time of the search, a catalogue count rather than a reading of the citing works (**Primary Official** endpoint).

Her policy influence is harder to measure and is claimed mainly in her own account. She says she travelled for years to the World Bank, Asian Development Bank, Canada, Brazil, Mongolia, Latvia, Japan and Iceland, mostly pro bono, and that the alliances built around the TPPA contributed to the collapse of some negotiations and to the New Zealand government's rejection of investor-state dispute settlement in future treaties. Those are her claims, reported in her valedictory lecture, not independently verified here ([valedictory lecture](https://e-tangata.co.nz/reflections/jane-kelsey-truth-to-power-the-critical-legal-academic-as-licensed-subversive), **Primary Theoretical**).

## Criticisms and controversies

The most detailed scholarly criticism of her economics comes from Tim Hazledine, Professor of Economics at the University of Auckland, reviewing *At the Crossroads*. He agrees with her critique of the Third Way, saying "Jane Kelsey is very good on the Third Way (that is, I quite agree with her)", but rejects her use of statistics: she repeats, "without sources (there are no sources given in this book)", the claim that the top 200 transnational corporations account for more than a quarter of world economic activity; he calls that "Rubbish! Wrong by a factor of five". He finds her proposals "cautious and moderate, perhaps to a fault", questions renationalisation without compensation and objects to participatory decision-making on the grounds that "the economy happens in markets, not meeting halls". His central objection is that Kelsey and other critics have not supplied "a coherent economic challenge" ([Hazledine](https://nzbooks.org.nz/2001/non-fiction/the-missing-alternative-tim-hazledine/), **Scholarly Secondary**). This is a review essay in a book magazine rather than a peer-reviewed article, but it is a substantive critique by a named economist and should be reported as such.

Brian Easton, a sympathetic economist, makes two methodological objections. He says Kelsey is "overly dependent upon newspaper sources rather than primary sources" even though she kept detailed records and the book contains a wealth of detail. He also reports that the alternatives chapter "received the most criticism in the reviews of the first edition, and has not been changed in the second", and argues that if the state is to pursue social objectives, an alternative must give the state a constructive role rather than rely only on voluntary collective action. He defends her against the charge of wanting to prescribe from above, and argues that what critics need is a theory of the reforms that incorporates the reformers' account rather than simply rejecting it ([Easton](https://www.eastonbh.ac.nz/1997/07/ithe_new_zealand_experiment_a_model_for_world_structural_adjustmenti_rev/), **Scholarly Secondary**).

Bruce Curtis offers a criticism from within the radical tradition. He credits Kelsey with internationalising the concept of the New Zealand experiment and calls her work rich in narrative, but says her focus on the FIRE economy "continues to eschew the contradictory relationships of class and class fractions as an analytical key". In his judgment she "comes closest to a class-based account" but leaves "the critical analytical deficit ... most keen" because her writing remains policy-centred rather than grounded in class and neo-colonial analysis ([Curtis](https://socialspacejournal.eu/11%20numer/Bruce%20Curtis%20-%20Class,%20neoliberalism%20and%20New%20Zealand.pdf), **Scholarly Secondary**).

Naoise McDonagh's review of *The FIRE Economy* is broadly supportive — he calls it an important critique and "excellent analysis" — but identifies "one minor weakness": its treatment of the Public Finance Amendment Act 2004 and derivatives trading is "one-sided" because it does not note that derivatives are also used to hedge against price movements ([McDonagh](https://www.crimejusticejournal.com/article/view/885), **Scholarly Secondary**). Keith Rankin, generally favourable, says Kelsey "overuses" the word "agenda" and is "somewhat ambivalent about democracy", while praising the book as "an excellent contribution to the globalisation debate" ([Rankin](http://keithrankin.co.nz/1999924revKelseyEP.html), **Scholarly Secondary**). Maria Humphries writes as an avowed admirer but presses the ethical question of whether, as individuals, "we" would accept benefits gained at the expense of the poor in other countries ([Humphries](https://www.msd.govt.nz/documents/about-msd-and-our-work/publications-resources/journals-and-magazines/social-policy-journal/spj16/16-pages203-208.pdf), **Scholarly Secondary**).

Kelsey's own lecture describes controversy outside the scholarly journals: her policy research and advocacy was "pilloried by the National Business Review", and the columnist Fran O'Sullivan urged the Vice-Chancellor to find other academics to counter her influence during the TPPA campaign. These are her accounts of attacks on her, not independently verified reports of what those critics said ([valedictory lecture](https://e-tangata.co.nz/reflections/jane-kelsey-truth-to-power-the-critical-legal-academic-as-licensed-subversive), **Primary Theoretical**). Hazledine reports that Chris Trotter attacked her in *The Independent* for failing to offer an alternative and then attacked the proposals she did offer, linking them to Lenin and Bolshevism; this is Hazledine's account of Trotter's review, which was not opened directly ([Hazledine](https://nzbooks.org.nz/2001/non-fiction/the-missing-alternative-tim-hazledine/), **Scholarly Secondary**).

## Related thinkers

- **Keith Rankin** — New Zealand political economist and former University of Auckland colleague. He was not a co-author of *The New Zealand Experiment*, but reviewed *Reclaiming the Future* in 1999 and discussed her solutions, including basic income, at the 1996 BIEN conference ([Rankin review](http://keithrankin.co.nz/1999924revKelseyEP.html); [BIEN report](http://www.keithrankin.co.nz/krnknbien.html), **Scholarly Secondary**).
- **Brian Easton** — New Zealand economist and fellow critic of the reforms. His 1997 review supports her account of the democratic deficit and the consolidation phase while criticising her sources and her treatment of alternatives ([Easton](https://www.eastonbh.ac.nz/1997/07/ithe_new_zealand_experiment_a_model_for_world_structural_adjustmenti_rev/), **Scholarly Secondary**).
- **Tim Hazledine** — Professor of Economics at Auckland; agrees with her on the Third Way but rejects her statistics and lacks confidence in her alternative programme ([Hazledine](https://nzbooks.org.nz/2001/non-fiction/the-missing-alternative-tim-hazledine/), **Scholarly Secondary**).
- **Bruce Curtis** — sociologist who shares her critique but argues that it needs a richer class and neo-colonial analysis ([Curtis](https://socialspacejournal.eu/11%20numer/Bruce%20Curtis%20-%20Class,%20neoliberalism%20and%20New%20Zealand.pdf), **Scholarly Secondary**).
- **Bruce Jesson** — New Zealand journalist and critic of the reforms, paired with Kelsey by Curtis as the country's leading critics of neoliberalism (same source).
- **Moana Jackson, Ranginui Walker and Annette Sykes** — Māori thinkers and activists whose analysis and strategies she draws on in her Tiriti work; Sykes led the TPPA Waitangi Tribunal claim, and Jackson supplied both constitutional analysis and a warning about incrementalism ([valedictory lecture](https://e-tangata.co.nz/reflections/jane-kelsey-truth-to-power-the-critical-legal-academic-as-licensed-subversive), **Primary Theoretical**).
- **Karl Polanyi and Antonio Gramsci** — the principal theoretical influences she names for the double movement and for law, hegemony and intellectual role (same source).
- **Marilyn Waring** — New Zealand scholar and public figure whose jacket endorsement calls *The New Zealand Experiment* "cogent and persuasive" and says "This is the truth we have been waiting for" ([BWB book page](https://www.bwb.co.nz/books/the-new-zealand-experiment), **Primary Official** publisher copy). This is a publisher's endorsement, not an independent scholarly review read here.
