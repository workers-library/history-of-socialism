## Biography

David Harvey's own curriculum vitae gives the basic record. It lists a B.A. with first-class honours in geography from St John's College, Cambridge, in 1957, and an M.A. and Ph.D. in geography from the same college in 1962. It gives his permanent positions in sequence: lecturer in geography at the University of Bristol from 1961 to 1969; associate professor at Johns Hopkins from 1969 to 1973; professor there from 1973 to 1989; Halford Mackinder Professor of Geography at Oxford from 1987 to 1993; professor at Johns Hopkins again from 1993 to 2001; and Distinguished Professor at the City University of New York Graduate Center from 2001 onward. [Primary Official: David Harvey, *Curriculum Vitae*, hosted at davidharvey.org, opened as PDF]

Harvey's author site describes him as Distinguished Professor of Anthropology and Geography at the CUNY Graduate Center, Director of Research at the Center for Place, Culture and Politics, and a teacher of Marx's *Capital* for more than fifty years. A 2021 post on that site, announcing a televised interview, adds that he was a Miliband Fellow at the London School of Economics and Halford Mackinder Professor at Oxford. [Primary Official: davidharvey.org, About and author pages; Primary Theoretical: davidharvey.org post of 24 September 2021]

The CV records honours that help date his standing in the discipline: the Gill Memorial of the Royal Geographical Society in 1973, the Anders Retzius Gold Medal in 1989, the Patron's Medal of the Royal Geographical Society and the Vautrin Lud International Prize in 1995, election as a corresponding fellow of the British Academy in 1998, election to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in 2007, and honorary doctorates from Buenos Aires and Roskilde in 1997, Uppsala in 2000, Ohio State in 2004 and Lund in 2008. [Primary Official: Harvey CV]

His exact birth date is **not verified** from any primary source opened here. A CUNY faculty page and a CUNY news release appeared in search results but both returned access-denied responses when fetched; no biographical detail has been taken from those pages. A date of 1961 for the doctorate appears in tertiary search results, but the author's own CV says 1962, and that is the figure used here. [Access limitation recorded below]

## Intellectual tradition

Harvey works in Marxist geography and what he calls a historical-geographical materialism: an attempt to rebuild Marx's theory of accumulation with space, place and the production of the built environment treated as central rather than incidental. In his own account of the spatial fix he says the idea "initially came out of attempts to reconstruct Marx's theory of the geography of capitalist accumulation". [Primary Theoretical: Harvey, "Globalization and the 'Spatial Fix'", *geographische revue* 2/2001, PDF, p. 25] Bob Jessop, writing in 2004, describes Harvey's deeper project as "to reconstruct theory with space (and the 'relation to nature') clearly integrated within it as foundational elements", and notes that Harvey sought to overcome the privilege that conventional dialectics give to time over space. [Scholarly Secondary: Jessop, "Spatial Fixes, Temporal Fixes, and Spatio-Temporal Fixes", Lancaster on-line paper, revised 27 June 2004]

He is a Marxist, but his relation to the tradition is argumentative rather than devotional. The 2004 *Antipode* symposium on *The Limits to Capital* included Jessop's "On the Limits of *The Limits to Capital*" and Erica Schoenberger's "The Spatial Fix Revisited", and a 2006 *Historical Materialism* symposium produced Robert Brenner's "What Is, and What Is Not, Imperialism?", Sam Ashman and Alex Callinicos's "Capital Accumulation and the State System: Assessing David Harvey's *The New Imperialism*", and Harvey's own "Comment on Commentaries". These are debates among writers working in the same broad tradition. [Primary Official: Crossref metadata for DOIs 10.1111/j.1467-8330.2004.00427.x, 10.1111/j.1467-8330.2004.00422.x, 10.1163/156920606778982464, 10.1163/156920606778982572 and 10.1163/156920606778982563]

In a 2017 interview Harvey rejects a narrow Marxist account of revolutionary agency. Asked about the privilege given to the industrial worker, he answered: "I've always felt that this is too limited a conception of who is the proletariat and who's in the 'vanguard'." He adds that "it's in this regard that I take up Antonio Gramsci's forms of organization", because Gramsci joined factory councils to organisation in neighbourhoods so that the unemployed, temporary workers and people outside traditional industrial employment could be represented. [Primary Theoretical: Vincent Emanuele, "Rebel Cities, Urban Resistance and Capitalism: a Conversation with David Harvey", Verso Notes, 9 February 2017]

## Major contributions

**The spatial fix and the spatio-temporal fix.** Harvey's own clarification is the best evidence. He says he first used the term in an Antipode article called "The Spatial Fix: Hegel, von Thunen and Marx" and that it was later developed in *The Limits to Capital* (1982) and "The Geopolitics of Capitalism" (1985). He defines his usage as "capitalism's insatiable drive to resolve its inner crisis tendencies by geographical expansion and geographical restructuring". He plays on two meanings of "fix": capital must "fix space" in roads, houses, transport networks and other enduring structures "in order to overcome space" through cheaper movement, and it must later destroy that fixed landscape to make room for a new round of accumulation. [Primary Theoretical: Harvey, "Globalization and the 'Spatial Fix'", pp. 24–25] A related journal record confirms the 1981 article as "THE SPATIAL FIX – HEGEL, VON THUNEN, AND MARX", *Antipode* 13(3), 1–12. [Primary Official: Crossref DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8330.1981.tb00312.x]

**Accumulation by dispossession.** In "The 'New' Imperialism: Accumulation by Dispossession" Harvey explains the coinage directly: "Since it seems peculiar to call an ongoing process 'primitive' or 'original' I shall, in what follows, substitute these terms by the concept of 'accumulation by dispossession'." He lists the processes he has in mind, beginning with "the commodification and privatization of land and the forceful expulsion of peasant populations", the conversion of common, collective and state rights into exclusive private rights, the suppression of the commons, the commodification of labour power, colonial and neo-colonial appropriation, the credit system, usury and the national debt. He insists that such practices are not a one-off origin: "Accumulation by dispossession can occur in a variety of ways and there is much that is both contingent and haphazard about its modus operandi. Yet it is omnipresent in no matter what historical period and picks up strongly when crises of overaccumulation occur". [Primary Theoretical: Harvey, *Socialist Register 2004*, PDF, pp. 74–76]

**The "new imperialism".** The same essay argues that the failure to accumulate through expanded reproduction since the 1970s has been matched by a turn to dispossession, and calls this the hallmark of the "new imperialism". He sets this against an earlier epoch: European-centred imperialism from 1884 to 1945, understood through Hannah Arendt, followed by a US-led system after 1945, and then a shift towards "an open imperialism backed by military force" which he reads as a sign of weakening US hegemony rather than renewed strength. [Primary Theoretical: Harvey, *Socialist Register 2004*, pp. 64, 76]

**Urbanisation and the right to the city.** Harvey treats cities as centres of surplus production and absorption. In "The Right to the City" he writes that "cities have arisen through geographical and social concentrations of a surplus product" and that "urbanization has always been, therefore, a class phenomenon". His formulation of the right is deliberately collective: "The right to the city is far more than the individual liberty to access urban resources: it is a right to change ourselves by changing the city." He calls it "a common rather than an individual right" because the transformation requires collective power over urbanisation. [Primary Theoretical: Harvey, "The Right to the City", *New Left Review* 53, September–October 2008, DOI 10.64590/fmh, opening paragraphs] In the version hosted on his own site he notes that "there are various social movements around the world that are now demanding the right to the city as their goal". [Primary Theoretical: Harvey, "The Right to the City", author-hosted PDF, p. 1]

**Other influential concepts.** His books establish time-space compression and flexible accumulation in *The Condition of Postmodernity*, uneven geographical development in *Spaces of Global Capitalism*, monopoly rent and the "art of rent" in *Rebel Cities*, and a catalogue of systemic contradictions in *Seventeen Contradictions and the End of Capitalism*. The titles and dates are recorded from the author's own book list and CV; the concepts are named in the publisher descriptions and in the 2017 interview, where Harvey explains monopoly rent through branding, wine, signature architecture and the marketing of city histories. [Primary Official: davidharvey.org/books and Harvey CV; Primary Theoretical: Verso interview, 9 February 2017]

## Analysis of capitalism

Harvey's account of crisis begins with overaccumulation. In the *Socialist Register* essay he defines it as a condition in which surpluses of labour and surpluses of capital exist side by side: unsold commodities, idle capacity and money capital with no profitable outlet. Unless capital and labour are devalued, these surpluses must be absorbed. He sets out three routes: temporal displacement through long-lived investment in infrastructure, education or research; spatial displacement through new markets, productive capacity, resources and labour elsewhere; or a combination of the two. Fixed capital embedded in the built environment — ports, airports, transport, water, housing, hospitals, schools — is central because it can absorb very large quantities of capital and labour. Credit and "fictitious capital", mediated by financial and state institutions, allow future-oriented projects to be funded now, with the result that failed investments reappear as devalued assets or a fiscal crisis of the state. He illustrates the pattern with the property collapse that opened the 1973 crisis, New York City's near-bankruptcy in 1975, Japan's asset-price collapse in 1990, the Thai and Indonesian property bubbles in 1997, and the speculative strength of property markets after 2001. [Primary Theoretical: Harvey, *Socialist Register 2004*, pp. 64–65]

Dispossession is, in his account, the other half of the story. He charges that Marx and Luxemburg relegated predation, fraud and violence to an "original" stage or to the outside of capitalism, and argues instead that these practices remain inside it. The contemporary list includes Enron's destruction of pensions and livelihoods, hedge-fund attacks on South East Asian currencies that forced asset sales at fire-sale prices, the patenting of genetic material and seeds under TRIPS, "biopiracy", the commodification of nature, the appropriation of culture, the privatisation of water and public assets, and the reversal of welfare and pension rights won through earlier struggles. The state, "with its monopoly of violence and definitions of legality", is central to enforcing these transfers. [Primary Theoretical: Harvey, *Socialist Register 2004*, pp. 74–76]

In the 2017 interview he gives a concrete history of urbanisation as surplus absorption. He describes how post-1945 suburban construction, highways, automobiles and mortgages absorbed surplus capital in the United States while inner-city communities were left behind, and how capital later returned to the city centre, expelling poorer residents to the periphery. He notes that China after 2007 used urbanisation, high-speed rail and infrastructure on the same model, and that empty cities could become a serious problem if the boom failed. He also describes state support for homeownership — mortgage guarantees, the Federal Housing Administration, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the mortgage interest deduction and the GI Bill — as part of the machinery that produced the subprime crisis and a politics of defending property values. [Primary Theoretical: Emanuele/Harvey interview, Verso Notes, 9 February 2017]

On neoliberalism specifically, the material opened here is narrower than the claim. In "The Right to the City" he says that the human-rights ideals then circulating did "not fundamentally challenge hegemonic liberal and neoliberal market logics", in a world where "the rights of private property and the profit rate trump all other notions of rights". In the Verso interview he describes neoliberal urbanism as a "redesign of the city according to these neoliberal principles of self reliance, taking personal responsibility, competition, the fragmentation of the city into gated communities and privileged spaces". These are Harvey's own formulations. The full text of *A Brief History of Neoliberalism* and the Oxford University Press product page were not opened, so no definition is quoted from that book. [Primary Theoretical: NLR 53, 2008; Verso interview, 2017]

## Analysis of socialism

Harvey offers no single institutional blueprint in the sources opened here. His alternative is sketched through struggles over the city, the commons and the form of political organisation. In the 2017 interview he argues that housing, health care and education should be organised around use rather than exchange: "the house also has an exchange-value" which comes to dominate shelter, and "we should reject the exchange-value system, which is caught up in speculative activity, profiteering, and actually disrupts the ways in which we can acquire necessary products and services." [Primary Theoretical: Verso interview, 2017] He links this to the "right to the city" and to the creation of an urban commons, while insisting that the commons must be thought at several scales at once — neighbourhood gardens, bioregions, national water systems and the global atmosphere — and that local experiments alone cannot address climate change. [Primary Theoretical: Verso interview, 2017]

His institutional examples include the Paris Commune of 1871, the Seattle general strike of 1919, factory and neighbourhood organisation in the Gramscian mode, Workers Party participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre, and organising among taxi drivers, delivery workers and domestic workers. He puts these alongside conventional trade-union organising rather than replacing it, and repeatedly warns that urban social movements can be exclusionary and reactionary as well as progressive. The "right to the city" is for him a collective claim on the process of urbanisation, not a consumer entitlement. [Primary Theoretical: Verso interview, 2017; Harvey, "The Right to the City", NLR 53, 2008 and author-hosted PDF]

## Intellectual influences

Marx is the constant: the CV lists a career of teaching and writing on *Capital*, the *Grundrisse* and the companion volumes, and Harvey's essay on the spatial fix says the idea came from reconstructing Marx's geography of accumulation. [Primary Official: Harvey CV; Primary Theoretical: "Globalization and the 'Spatial Fix'", p. 25]

In the same essay he names Hegel's view of imperialism, von Thünen's frontier wage and Marx on colonialism as the sources he worked through for the 1981 article. [Primary Theoretical: "Globalization and the 'Spatial Fix'", p. 25] In the *Socialist Register* essay he opens with Lefebvre's claim that capitalism survives through the production of space, and says that "both Lenin and Luxemburg" treated imperialism as the answer to the riddle of capitalist survival. Later in the same essay he draws on Luxemburg's *The Accumulation of Capital*, Braudel on the role of the state, Arendt on nineteenth-century British imperialism, Hilferding, and Wade and Veneroso on South East Asian asset transfers. [Primary Theoretical: Harvey, *Socialist Register 2004*, pp. 63, 74–76] In "The Right to the City" he explicitly links his argument to a revival of Henri Lefebvre's ideas, and quotes the urban sociologist Robert Park; the author-hosted version also mentions Engels and Simmel as nineteenth-century urban critics. [Primary Theoretical: NLR 53, 2008; author-hosted PDF, pp. 1–2] The 2017 interview supplies Gramsci as an influence on his organisational thinking. [Primary Theoretical: Verso interview, 2017]

## Influence and legacy

Institutional recognition is recorded in the CV: the Vautrin Lud prize in 1995, corresponding fellowship of the British Academy in 1998 and election to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences in 2007. [Primary Official: Harvey CV] Scholarly engagement is visible in the *Antipode* 2004 symposium and the 2006 collection *David Harvey: A Critical Reader*, edited by Noel Castree and Derek Gregory. The publisher's record shows contributions by Trevor Barnes, Alex Callinicos, Marcus Doel, Melissa Wright, Sharon Zukin, Eric Sheppard, Bob Jessop, Nancy Hartsock, Bruce Braun, Nigel Thrift, Cindi Katz and Castree himself, with a final chapter by Harvey called "Space as a Keyword". [Primary Official: Wiley-VCH product page for ISBN 978-0-631-23510-1, 1st edition January 2006, 338 pages]

The "right to the city" formulation has had a life beyond the article: Harvey himself wrote in 2008 that movements around the world were already making it their goal, and the 2017 interview connects the idea to Occupy, the Paris Commune and urban uprisings in Buenos Aires, El Alto and France. [Primary Theoretical: author-hosted "Right to the City" PDF, p. 1; Verso interview, 2017] Verso's descriptions of *Rebel Cities* and *The Story of Capital* make larger claims for his influence — that he "provoked a revolution in his field" and inspired a generation of radical intellectuals — but these are publisher blurbs, not independent evidence, and are treated as such here. [Primary Official: Verso product pages]

Downstream scholarly use of the spatial fix is documented in journal records: Sam Scott's 2013 *Antipode* article applies it to migrant labour in the UK food industry on the basis of 37 employer interviews, and Andrew Herod's 2019 encyclopedia entry notes that labour geographers have adopted and adapted the concept. [Scholarly Secondary: Crossref abstracts for DOIs 10.1111/anti.12023 and 10.1002/9781118568446.eurs0309]

## Criticisms and controversies

Bob Jessop's 2004 paper accepts the importance of Harvey's contribution but states that the approach "can be criticized on three grounds". First, Harvey long treated temporal and spatial fixes as distinct or merely additive rather than interactive, although credit is inherently both spatial and temporal. Second, his account of spatial fixes rests on one contradiction — capital as place-bound stock versus value in motion — and underplays other contradictions with their own spatial and temporal aspects. Third, the analysis is "primarily value-theoretical": the political and extra-economic dimensions of capitalism enter late or in an ad hoc way, and "power" remains "an undefined primitive term" in the discussion of capitalist and territorial logics. Jessop concludes that "there are serious limitations to what is often a banale and tautological emphasis on internal relations". [Scholarly Secondary: Jessop, Lancaster on-line paper, 2004; published version *Antipode* 36(3), 480–496, per Crossref DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8330.2004.00427.x]

Raju Das's 2017 Marxist critique, in *World Review of Political Economy* 8(4), is the most direct attack on accumulation by dispossession that was opened. The abstract calls the theory "very problematic" and argues that the concept "is chaotic in the critical-realist philosophical sense: it includes processes which bear no internal relations, and it separates processes which should not be separated". Das holds that Harvey "inflates the causal significance of the concept far too much" and "mistakenly considers ABD to be the dominant moment of contemporary capitalism"; that he "generally fails to connect ABD of producers to what I will call 'accumulation by exploitation' of proletarians and semi-proletarians"; that his account of imperialism abstracts from exploitation in production controlled by imperialist businesses; and that his political conclusions are reformist and take "a dim view of the role of the working class in the anti-capitalist socialist movement". [Scholarly Secondary: Raju Das, DOI 10.13169/worlrevipoliecon.8.4.0590, abstract opened through Crossref; full text not opened]

Jim Glassman's 2006 review in *Progress in Human Geography* 30(5), 608–625, says Harvey's adaptation of primitive accumulation "raises a variety of theoretical and practical issues" and reviews its political implications in the Global North. [Scholarly Secondary: Crossref abstract and metadata, DOI 10.1177/0309132506070172; journal full text not opened] A further cluster of criticism concerns *The New Imperialism* specifically: Brenner's "What Is, and What Is Not, Imperialism?" and Ashman and Callinicos's "Capital Accumulation and the State System: Assessing David Harvey's *The New Imperialism*", both in *Historical Materialism* 14(4) in 2006, with Harvey replying in "Comment on Commentaries" in the same issue. Their titles and publication details are verified from Crossref; the arguments themselves are **not summarised here** because the article texts were not opened. [Primary Official: Crossref records for the three DOIs]

No personal or legal controversy is asserted in this dossier. The controversies recorded are theoretical and political disputes within Marxist scholarship, and the dossier does not treat a disagreement as a refutation.

## Related thinkers

Karl Marx and, in his own account, Hegel and von Thünen shape the spatial-fix argument; Lenin, Rosa Luxemburg, Braudel and Hannah Arendt shape the account of imperialism and dispossession; Henri Lefebvre supplies the "right to the city" that Harvey reworks; Robert Park, Engels and Simmel appear in his account of the urban condition; Antonio Gramsci shapes his organisational argument. [Primary Theoretical: Harvey's opened essays and Verso interview]

Contemporary interlocutors and critics include Bob Jessop, Erica Schoenberger, Raju Das, Jim Glassman, Robert Brenner, Sam Ashman, Alex Callinicos, Noel Castree, Derek Gregory, Trevor Barnes, Nigel Thrift, Cindi Katz, Sharon Zukin, Nancy Hartsock and Bruce Braun. [Scholarly Secondary: Jessop paper, Crossref records, Wiley-VCH contents page] The related profile most worth cross-referencing is Henri Lefebvre if that author is later added; several of Harvey's central claims are explicit extensions of Lefebvre's.
