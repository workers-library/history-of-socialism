## Biography

Domenico Losurdo was born on 14 November 1941 and died on 28 June 2018. Verso's author page gives those exact dates and describes him as Professor of Philosophy at the University of Urbino. The translators of the English edition of his Stalin book add that he was born in Sannicandro di Bari and that a tumour took his life in 2018 (Primary Official: Verso author page; Primary Theoretical: Iskra translation foreword). Angelo d'Orsi, writing in *La Repubblica* and reprinted by Verso, gives the same birthplace and year and says he died of a brain tumour after a short illness; David Broder's obituary in *Jacobin* also reports a brain tumour (Primary Official: Verso obituary; Scholarly Secondary: Jacobin obituary). Broder calls him seventy-seven, which does not sit with the 1941–2018 dates; the dates on the Verso page are the safer record.

His university training and teaching career are partly documented. An obituary by Guido Liguori, published by Verso, says he studied under Pasquale Salvucci at Urbino and then taught the history of philosophy there for many years (Primary Official: Verso obituary by Liguori). Springer's author note for *Class Struggle* calls him Emeritus Professor at Urbino, and Verso's author page confirms the professorship (Primary Official). The University of Urbino keeps a personal page listing him as *Professore Emerito* and an institutional CV; I located both in search results, but the CV downloaded as a fifteen-page scan with no text layer and the personal page was not opened directly, so I could not independently verify from them the often-repeated details that he graduated in 1963, wrote a thesis on Johann Karl Rodbertus, or led the Internationale Gesellschaft Hegel-Marx from 1988. Those details remain unconfirmed in this dossier.

His political development is described consistently in three obituaries. Liguori records that he was first a militant and leader in the Italian Communist Party (PCI), then in Rifondazione Comunista (PRC), then in the PdCI and the effort to refound a PCI after 2016, and that he was president of the Associazione Marx XXI (Primary Official: Verso obituary by Liguori). Broder, writing in *New Left Review* in 2017, gives a fuller sequence: as a young militant in the 1960s Losurdo sided with the Chinese position in the Sino-Soviet split and welcomed the Cultural Revolution; in the 1980s he wrote for the PCI daily *l'Unità* and joined the party; after the PCI changed its name in 1991 he went with those who formed Rifondazione Comunista; from 2016 he joined the attempt to recreate a PCI (Scholarly Secondary: Broder, *New Left Review* 107). d'Orsi confirms that Losurdo was an "unrepentant communist" who joined the small party that became the PdCI and helped launch a new Italian Communist Party, and says the two men disagreed significantly about Stalin (Primary Official: Verso obituary by d'Orsi). The claim that he left the PCI specifically over the Sino-Soviet split also appears in a 2025 *People's World* review, but that is a political newspaper's retrospective claim, not an institutional record, and I treat Broder's account as the firmer of the two (Scholarly Secondary: Broder; opinion piece: *People's World*, not used as evidence for the fact).

## Intellectual tradition

Losurdo wrote as a Marxist philosopher and historian of ideas who refused to separate philosophy from the political conflicts in which it was produced. d'Orsi describes him as a convinced Marxist and a historical materialist who looked behind ideas for their social basis and ideological context, and as someone whose standing as a scholar rested on the history of philosophy (Primary Official: Verso obituary by d'Orsi). Liguori places his early work in an Hegelian-Marxist line running from Kant and Hegel through Marx, Lukács and Gramsci (Primary Official: Verso obituary by Liguori). In the 2013 interview reprinted by Verso, Losurdo described himself as "a communist – and not only that, but also as a philosopher and historian" fighting dominant ideology (Primary Theoretical: Verso republication of the *l'Humanité* interview).

He wrote from within the communist movement but against several currents of the left. The title and argument of *Western Marxism* set "Eastern" and "Western" Marxism against one another, and he used the distinction as a political and historical judgment rather than a merely geographical one (Primary Theoretical: *Western Marxism*, described by Monthly Review Press; Scholarly Secondary: Ponce de León and Rockhill introduction, reported in their 2026 special-issue introduction). He rejected the "withering away of the state" as a messianic inheritance and argued that Marxism had more to learn from liberalism about limiting power than liberals had learned from Marxism (Primary Theoretical: 2012 Platypus interview transcript). He also rejected the Cold War category of "totalitarianism" when it placed Stalin and Hitler on the same moral plane. In the *l'Humanité* interview he said the term derived from "total mobilisation" and "total war", and compared it to a "huge historical comparison" that collapses unlike cases (Primary Theoretical: Verso republication). Critics from several directions therefore describe him as a Marxist-Leninist or a pro-Stalin writer, while his translators insisted that his Stalin book was "not a rehabilitation" but an attempt to contextualise and end demonisation (Primary Theoretical: Iskra foreword by Hakamäki and Engel-Di Mauro).

## Major contributions

Losurdo's best-known contribution is the method and vocabulary of *Liberalism: A Counter-History*. He called the book a "counter history" not because it abandoned historical evidence but because it set out to write the history of the liberal movement and liberal society "in their concrete reality" rather than "liberal thought in its abstract purity" (Primary Theoretical: *Liberalism*, "A Short Methodological Introduction"). He borrowed Tocqueville's complaint that histories of the old regime describe its "surface glitter" while saying little about those who could neither make themselves heard nor seen, and applied it to liberalism itself. The book's central concept is the "community of the free": a group whose members enjoyed rights and whose freedom was defined against populations kept outside it. The counterpart is the "sacred" and "profane" space of liberal society, and the "exclusion clauses" that marked slaves, colonial peoples, servants, wage workers and women as outside the promised universality (Primary Theoretical: *Liberalism*, chapters 1, 4, 7 and conclusion).

The term he made most influential was "Herrenvolk democracy", a democracy of the master race. In chapter 4 of *Liberalism* he writes that students of the United States and South Africa should "speak of a 'Herrenvolk democracy' that is, a democracy which applied exclusively to the 'master race'", and that equality inside the white community was "primarily a clear line of demarcation from the excluded" (Primary Theoretical: *Liberalism*, printed p. 107). He used the same category in the 2013 interview to describe Israel's treatment of Palestinians and the claim that the West may wage war without United Nations authorisation (Primary Theoretical: Verso republication). A second concept is the "tangle" or dialectic of "emancipation and dis-emancipation": rights won by one group could be tied to the further subjection of another, and rights once won could be withdrawn. In the conclusion he gives the examples of the French Revolution's male suffrage, abolished two years later, and the losses of Black Americans after the 1877 compromise (Primary Theoretical: *Liberalism*, printed pp. 341–342).

His second major contribution is a widening of the concept of class struggle. Springer's description of *Class Struggle: A Political and Philosophical History* states his claim that class struggle is not merely the poor against the rich but "a general theory of social conflict" in which conflicts are intertwined and each situation has its own characteristics (Primary Official: Springer book record). The publisher's chapter list confirms that he treated struggles for recognition, the family, the "South-East passage", Lenin in 1919, the period after the revolution and the "end of history" as parts of one argument. In the 2013 interview he named three fronts: peoples shaking off the colonial yoke, subaltern classes fighting capitalist exploitation, and women refusing the "domestic slavery" of the patriarchal family (Primary Theoretical: Verso republication). Scholarly admirers describe this as a genus with several species, including national liberation and women's emancipation (Scholarly Secondary: Ponce de León and Rockhill, 2026 introduction).

His third contribution is the East-West account of Marxism and the comparative method used in the Stalin book. In its introduction he asks whether the vilification of Stalin should be explained by "the initiative of a single personality or a narrow ruling class", and argues instead for an "all-round comparative approach" that keeps in view the history of Russia, the Western countries in what he calls the "Second Thirty Years' War", and comparable institutions elsewhere, including liberal states (Primary Theoretical: *Stalin*, introduction, printed pp. 9–11). This produces the claim that Stalin's gulag cannot be understood by comparing it only with Nazi camps; it must also be compared with tsarist Siberia, British penal transportation and the plantation (Primary Theoretical: *Stalin*, table of contents and chapter 4).

## Analysis of capitalism

Losurdo did not write a conventional account of the labour process or of value. His analysis of capitalism is historical and institutional: he asks which social relations a given regime of freedom requires, and who is excluded from them. The clearest statement is in the conclusion of *Liberalism*. He grants that liberal thought "vigorously insisted on the need for competition between individuals in the market, in order to develop social wealth and the productive forces", and calls this "a further, major historical merit". He immediately adds that the liberal market was for centuries "a site of exclusion, de humanization and even terror": enslaved people were commodities rather than buyers and sellers, white indentured servants were bought and sold, workers' coalitions were repressed, and essential aspects of human dignity were commodified (Primary Theoretical: *Liberalism*, printed pp. 343–344). The threat of being sold away from family members, he writes, imposed obedience more effectively than the lash.

For Losurdo, chattel slavery and colonialism are not deviations from capitalism's liberal history but part of its formation. Chapter 2 of *Liberalism* is titled "Liberalism and Racial Slavery: A Unique Twin Birth"; in chapter 1 he quotes John C. Calhoun calling slavery "a positive good" while presenting himself as a defender of liberty and minority rights against absolutism (Primary Theoretical: *Liberalism*, printed pp. 1, 35). The English text traces the wealth of the British Empire to the slave trade and the *asiento*, and notes that people who profited from slavery sat in Parliament (Primary Theoretical: *Liberalism*, printed pp. 106–107). In the final chapter he cites other historians on the deportation of Indigenous Americans, the slave trade as "the largest involuntary movement of human beings in all history", lynching, eugenics and racial segregation, and asks whether the twentieth-century catastrophe can still be described as an invasion of a previously peaceful liberal world (Primary Theoretical: *Liberalism*, printed pp. 336–340). This is an argument by juxtaposition and comparison; it depends heavily on the work of historians such as Eric Williams, C. Vann Woodward, George Frederickson and Mike Davis, whom he cites by name.

His later work adds an international political economy. Sympathetic editors write that, following Lenin, he located the roots of Western left Eurocentrism and national chauvinism in the political economy of imperialism, visible in the racial and exclusionary structures of "Herrenvolk imperial liberalism", and that he treated the labour aristocracy as a material basis for reformism in the imperial core (Scholarly Secondary: Ponce de León and Rockhill, 2026 introduction). That is a summary by editors of his work, not a verbatim statement from one of his books. Losurdo's own words in the 2013 interview are more direct: China's struggle to end the Western monopoly on high technology and refuse a place in "inferior segments of the international labour market" must itself count as class struggle (Primary Theoretical: Verso republication). Capitalism, in this account, is held together by coerced divisions inside the working people of the world as much as by the wage relation alone.

## Analysis of socialism

Losurdo wrote no institutional blueprint for a future society. His preferences have to be recovered from scattered arguments in interviews, the late books on class struggle and Western Marxism, and his historical studies of Stalin and China. The most consistent preference is for strong public power. In the 2012 Platypus interview he called the Marxist idea that the state would simply disappear "totally misguided" and said that he did not believe society could exist without regulation and laws. He distinguished a naive "withering away of the state" from a "withering away of the state in terms of today's political form", and credited Gramsci with the claim that civil society is itself a form of domination (Primary Theoretical: Red Sails transcript of the 2012 interview). In the 2013 interview he repeated that Gramsci's warning against the "myth of the withering-away of the state" helped overcome the messianism that he believed damaged the building of a postcapitalist society (Primary Theoretical: Verso republication).

He defended markets and competition as instruments rather than as ends. In 2012 he said that "we cannot develop a post-capitalist society, at least for a long time to come, without some form of competition", and that Gramsci's phrase "determinate market" was preferable to an abstract market (Primary Theoretical: Red Sails transcript). In 2013 he argued that Deng Xiaoping's turn to incentives was continuous with Mao's goals: moral exhortation could not sustain development over a long period, and access to Western technology required opening to the world market (Primary Theoretical: Verso republication). He treated the Chinese claim to have lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty as an achievement while acknowledging "stark contradictions" (Primary Theoretical: Verso republication). His article "Has China Turned to Capitalism?", published in *International Critical Thought* 7(1) in 2017, reaches a similar conclusion through a comparison with Soviet Russia: the New Economic Policy, Stalinist collectivisation and late Soviet welfare each had distinct logic, and China's post-Mao market socialism is defended as a success (Primary Theoretical: abstract on the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences site).

His defence of historical communist projects rests on comparison and on the argument that judgement must use coeval standards. The translators of the Stalin book write that he sought to contextualise the material conditions of the Soviet leadership and to compare its institutions with those of other states, not to deny its "mistakes and terrible outcomes" (Primary Theoretical: Iskra foreword). In the book itself he shows how respected non-communist figures, from Isaac Deutscher and Benedetto Croce to Thomas Mann and Harold Laski, judged Stalin and the USSR favourably in the 1940s and early 1950s, and he treats Khrushchev's 1956 speech as a political act to be historicised rather than as the final disclosure of truth (Primary Theoretical: *Stalin*, introduction, printed pp. 2–11). The positive institutional vision behind this is one of parties and states capable of defending national independence, developing productive forces, limiting power by law, maintaining a public sphere in the economy, and participating in a democratic international order that refuses the "right" of powerful states to wage war (Primary Theoretical: Verso republication of the 2013 interview).

## Selected lectures and interviews

The only interview I read in full transcript form is the 2012 conversation with Ross Wolfe and Pam Nogales for the Platypus Affiliated Society, conducted around the English translation of *Liberalism: A Counter-History*. The transcript is hosted at Red Sails, which states that the original was on platypus1917.org and that a video recording exists. In it Losurdo explains the emancipation/de-emancipation dialectic, defends his comparison of Locke and Bodin, rejects the "withering away of the state", and describes liberalism's legacy as the limitation of power and competition (Primary Theoretical: Red Sails transcript, 2012; video endpoint listed but not opened). A hostile reply to this interview by Henry Flynt appeared in *Platypus Review* 49 in September 2012 (Scholarly Secondary/opinion).

A 2013 interview for *l'Humanité* was republished by Verso on 16 July 2018 under the title "Liberalism, the most dogged enemy of freedom". It covers Herrenvolk democracy, totalitarianism, class struggle, Gramsci and China (Primary Theoretical: Verso republication; the original *humanite.fr* link was listed but not opened). Verso's introduction also mentions a talk Losurdo gave at the 2007 Fête de l'Humanité at the invitation of the Fondation Gabriel-Péri; this is a reported event, not material I examined (Primary Official: Verso republication). Monthly Review Press states that the English edition of *Western Marxism* includes "the unprecedented publication of a major lecture" on Western Marxism, but gives no date or title on the page I opened (Primary Official: Monthly Review Press page).

## Intellectual influences

Losurdo's own statements name Marx, Engels, Lenin, Mao and Gramsci as sources for his widened theory of class struggle, and he repeatedly appeals to Marx's authority while arguing that Marx himself was one-sided about the bourgeois revolutions (Primary Theoretical: Verso 2013 interview; Red Sails 2012 transcript). Gramsci is especially important: he took from him the claim that civil society can be a form of domination, the idea of a "determinate market", and a non-messianic communism understood as an "integral humanism" completing the unity of the human race (Primary Theoretical: 2012 and 2013 interviews; Scholarly Secondary: Ponce de León and Rockhill credit the same Gramsci line). Liguori lists Kant, Hegel, Marx, Lukács and Gramsci as the objects of his earliest studies (Primary Official: Verso obituary by Liguori). In the 2013 interview Losurdo also names Rousseau and Hegel as thinkers who understood the link between economics and politics, and treats Tocqueville's method in *The Ancien Régime* as a model for his own "counter history" (Primary Theoretical: Verso 2013 interview; *Liberalism*, methodological introduction).

Hegel is a continuing presence rather than a simple influence. Liguori calls Losurdo one of the great contemporary authorities on Hegel, and the 2012 interview shows him using Hegelian categories of recognition while criticising idealist and messianic readings of Marx (Primary Official: Verso obituary; Primary Theoretical: Red Sails transcript). The Chinese revolution is an empirical and political influence: Broder records his youthful Maoist commitment, and the 2013 interview treats Mao and Deng as continuous (Scholarly Secondary: Broder, *New Left Review*; Primary Theoretical: Verso 2013 interview).

## Influence and legacy

Losurdo published a large body of work in Italian, German, French and Spanish, but much of the English-language reception came after his death. Verso's author page lists only a few English titles; Monthly Review Press describes him as the author of over fifty books. Broder counted some thirty-five books in Italian in 2017 and called him one of the most prolific Italian thinkers of his generation (Scholarly Secondary: Broder). Posthumous English editions include *Liberalism* (Verso, 2011 and 2014), *War and Revolution* (Verso, 2015 and 2020), *Non-Violence* (2017), *Class Struggle* (2016 and 2018), *Stalin* (Iskra, 2023), *Democracy or Bonapartism* (Verso, 2024), *Western Marxism* (Monthly Review Press, 2024) and *The Communist Question* (Iskra, 2026). These are publisher records opened for this dossier.

His institutional legacy is visible in Italy and internationally. The journal *Materialismo Storico*, published at Urbino, devoted its volume 10, issue 1 (2021) to "Domenico Losurdo, three years after", including an account of the first international prize named for him (Primary Official: Urbino journal issue page). *International Critical Thought* published a memorial by Stefano G. Azzarà of Urbino in volume 9, issue 2 (2019) and a special issue on Losurdo in 2026; the 2026 introduction by Jennifer Ponce de León and Gabriel Rockhill argues for the continuing relevance of his analysis of imperialism and emancipation (Scholarly Secondary: Taylor & Francis record of the 2019 article, seen as a search result only; Substack text of the 2026 introduction, opened). Roland Boer reported on a seminar series in China on Losurdo's books on liberalism and Hegel, attended by members of the Communist Party of China and teachers of Marxism (Scholarly Secondary: *Materialismo Storico* 10(1), 2021). Ross Wolfe's 2025 essay, whatever its polemical tone, is evidence that Losurdo's work had acquired a significant English-language readership and a organised body of promoters and critics (Scholarly Secondary/opinion: *New International*).

## Criticisms and controversies

The scholarly criticism of *Liberalism* is substantive and varied. Andrew Nurse, reviewing the book in *Labour / Le Travail* 69 in 2012, accepted that the story of liberal exclusions was important but argued that it had been told many times before, that the text was repetitive, that its footnotes did not always separate a secondary author's words from those of the thinker under discussion, and that gender was barely treated. He also criticised the use of the word "redskins" and concluded that Losurdo never supplied a workable definition of liberalism, so that Burke, Gobineau, Calhoun and supporters of Mussolini could all be called liberal (Scholarly Secondary: Project MUSE summary of Nurse, pp. 243–244). Henry Flynt's 2012 reply went further, accusing Losurdo of taking a Benjamin Franklin remark out of context, of citing a Tocqueville passage about Haiti that he could not find, and of ignoring the historical record of actually existing communism while treating republicanism as uniquely blameworthy (Scholarly Secondary/opinion: *Platypus Review* 49). Chris Byron's 2013 review was broadly sympathetic but noted that the condemnation could seem "helter-skelter" (Scholarly Secondary: Marx & Philosophy Review of Books). Joe Cleffie, writing from a Trotskyist standpoint in *International Socialist Review*, praised the historical material but objected to Losurdo's political conclusions about Marxism (Scholarly Secondary/opinion: ISR). An Italian newspaper review recorded by Laterza had already called him an "apologeta di Stalin" in 2011; I read only the headline and not the review (endpoint not opened).

The Stalin book and the later political works drew sharper criticism from historians and from the left. Guido Liguori, who admired much of the work, called Losurdo's argument about a pro-Stalin "Fifth Column" in the 1930s USSR "at least rather dubious" and rejected the comparison between the gulag and the Jewish Holocaust (Primary Official: Verso obituary by Liguori, itself a critical assessment). David Broder's review of *Il marxismo occidentale* in *New Left Review* argued that the East-West contrast was "Manichean" and that "Eastern Marxism becomes an undifferentiated bloc" in which contradictions were flattened out; he also questioned the claim that nation came before class for Lenin (Scholarly Secondary: Broder, NLR 107). Douglas Kellner, writing in *Logos* in 2026 and republished by the International Marxist-Humanist journal, called *Western Marxism* a "screed" and "apologetics for Stalinism", and argued that Losurdo ignored the anti-imperialist record of Bloch, Marcuse, the Frankfurt School and Korsch (Scholarly Secondary/opinion: Kellner). Ross Wolfe's 2025 essay "Against Losurdo" made the same charge in stronger language, describing an "entry of Stalinism into philosophy" and a "school of falsification", and documented what he takes as non sequiturs, selective quotation and misreading of Perry Anderson, whom Losurdo is said to have treated as a celebrant of Western Marxism (Scholarly Secondary/opinion: *New International*).

Two of the most useful sustained critiques focus on the structure of the argument rather than its tone. Joshua Nicholas Pineda, in *Spectre* in 2025, argues that Losurdo's category of Western Marxism has no coherent object once it includes Arendt, Foucault and Agamben; that the only principle uniting them is a supposed rejection of anticolonial struggle; that state-led industrialisation is wrongly identified with socialist revolution; and that the widening of class struggle into a general struggle for recognition drains the concept of the specific relation of exploitation it had denoted (Scholarly Secondary: Pineda). Stéphane Doucet, writing in *People's World* in 2025, criticised the same binary from a Marxist-Leninist position, arguing that Losurdo elevated "Third World versus West" above capital versus labour and nationalism above imperialism (Scholarly Secondary/opinion: *People's World*). Carlos Garrido's dissertation at Southern Illinois University is reported in a search result as arguing that Losurdo's critique leaves class and social function underdeveloped; I did not open the dissertation and do not rely on it here.

The book also drew favourable scholarly readings. Vernon Shaw's 2025 review in the *Marx & Philosophy Review of Books* defended the East-West distinction as a real geopolitical and economic division, while conceding that it was "imprecise in some ways" (Scholarly Secondary: Shaw). Zeyad el Nabolsy's 2024 review essay, seen only as a search excerpt, treats the Della Volpe/Togliatti debate and the critique of Bloch as serious contributions (endpoint blocked; not evidence here). Ponce de León and Rockhill's 2026 introduction is an explicit defence of Losurdo's framework (Scholarly Secondary).

## Related thinkers

The nearest published profile in this collection is Michael Parenti: both writers have argued that Western left intellectuals misunderstood actually existing socialism. I opened no Parenti source for this dossier, so this is a thematic overlap, not a sourced comparison. Rockhill and others connect Losurdo to Parenti's idea of "siege socialism", but the phrase is theirs, not Losurdo's (Scholarly Secondary: Ponce de León and Rockhill; Spectre review). Antonio Gramsci is the thinker Losurdo most often claimed as a guide, particularly on civil society, the state, hegemony and "integral humanism" (Primary Theoretical: interviews). Perry Anderson's *Considerations on Western Marxism* is the foil for the 2017 book, and critics on both sides note that Losurdo inverted Anderson's evaluation while depending on his framework (Scholarly Secondary: Broder, Pineda, Wolfe). Hannah Arendt, Ernst Nolte and François Furet are recurring targets: Losurdo treats their comparisons of Nazism and communism as historically revisionist (Primary Official: Verso book descriptions; Primary Theoretical: *Stalin*, chapter 5). Locke, Burke, Constant, Bentham, Sieyès, Calhoun and Tocqueville are the liberal writers through whom he develops the exclusion thesis (Primary Theoretical: *Liberalism*, table of contents and text). Frantz Fanon, Mao, Hồ Chí Minh, Lenin and Sun Yat-sen appear as the anticolonial counterweight in *Western Marxism* (Scholarly Secondary: Shaw review of the book; Broder review). Vivek Chibber is not discussed by Losurdo in the material I opened, but his work is part of the later debate about whether capitalism could have thrived without colonialism; I make no claim of influence.
