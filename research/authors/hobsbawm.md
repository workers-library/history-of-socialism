# Eric Hobsbawm (1917–2012) — research dossier

Scope: primary-source research for the Authors and Intellectual Traditions collection. Titles and dates of the eight works named in the brief were checked against the University of Edinburgh's Eric Hobsbawm Bibliography (an AHRC-funded project built from his papers), publisher records, library catalogues and scholarly obituaries. Claims about what Hobsbawm argued are taken from his own books, lectures and interviews wherever possible; where a quotation survives only inside a later study, that is stated at the point of use. No quotation, page number or URL in this dossier has been supplied from memory or from an unopened tertiary source.

## Biography

Eric John Ernest Hobsbawm was born in Alexandria on 9 June 1917, the son of Leopold Percy Hobsbaum, a British postal official whose family name had been mangled by an immigration officer, and Nelly Grün, from a Viennese Jewish family. The family moved to Vienna after the First World War. His father died of a heart attack in 1929 and his mother of tuberculosis in 1931; the orphaned boy and his sister moved to Berlin, where he entered a Gymnasium and joined a communist school students' organisation in 1932. He took part in the last Communist demonstration in Berlin in January 1933. His uncle moved the family to Britain shortly afterwards. Hobsbawm was a British citizen who came to England for financial reasons at the same time as the Nazis took power, and he later rejected the description of himself as a refugee from Nazism ([British Academy obituary](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Høgsbjerg 2018](http://isj.org.uk/eric-hobsbawms-histories/)).

He won a scholarship to King's College, Cambridge, and went up in October 1936, joining the student branch of the Communist Party of Great Britain (CPGB) on arrival. He edited *Granta*, was elected to the Cambridge Apostles, and in 1940 co-wrote a pamphlet with Raymond Williams defending the Soviet position in the war against Finland. After war service in the Royal Engineers and the Army Educational Corps he returned to Cambridge for doctoral work and was appointed a lecturer at Birkbeck College, London, in 1947. He stayed at Birkbeck until retiring in 1982, then taught one semester a year at the New School for Social Research in New York until 1997. He was elected a Fellow of the British Academy in 1978. He died in London in 2012, aged 95 ([Edinburgh bibliography, biography](https://hobsbawm.shca.ed.ac.uk/exist/apps/hobsbawm-app/biography); [British Academy obituary](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Weidenfeld & Nicolson author page](https://www.weidenfeldandnicolson.co.uk/contributor/eric-hobsbawm/)).

The CPGB fact needs careful handling. The Edinburgh project says he identified with communism as a teenager in Berlin but joined the CPGB formally in 1936 at Cambridge and remained a member until the party dissolved in 1991. Richard Evans's British Academy memoir gives the same sequence, adding that after Khrushchev's secret speech and the Soviet invasion of Hungary in 1956 Hobsbawm signed a protest letter which the *Daily Worker* refused and which appeared in the *New Statesman* on 18 November 1956; he then ceased all active work inside the party but refused to resign. He explained the refusal later as loyalty "to a great cause and to all those who had sacrificed their lives for it", and said he wanted to succeed as a known Communist despite the career cost. Evans records that the party hierarchy treated him as unreliable, that MI5 monitored him, and that the Foreign Office tried to block a UNESCO lecture in 1970. In later life he shifted towards the Italian Communist Party and Eurocommunism and then towards social democracy while remaining, in his own phrase, at the "tail-end of the first generation of communists" ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Kocka 2013](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/eric-j-hobsbawm-19172012/608B6BD01721D05F0623CD4CA32567D4); [Souvlis 2018](https://praticasdahistoria.pt/article/download/22448/16554/87110)).

## Intellectual tradition

Hobsbawm worked inside Marxist historiography but was never its official theorist. He was a central member of the Communist Party Historians' Group (founded as the Marxist Historians' Group in 1938, reconstituted in 1946) and one of the founders of *Past and Present* in 1952. The group's debates on the transition from feudalism to capitalism, and Maurice Dobb's *Studies in the Development of Capitalism* (1946), shaped his early work. He also drew on the French *Annales* school, introduced to him before the war by Munia Postan. At the 2008 Vienna ceremony granting him honorary citizenship he placed himself in "a worldwide generation of historians who revolutionized the writing of history" through links between history and the social sciences, whether under the banner of economic history, the *Annales*, Marxism or Max Weber; the quotation is preserved in Kocka's obituary ([Kocka 2013](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/eric-j-hobsbawm-19172012/608B6BD01721D05F0623CD4CA32567D4); [Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf)).

He accepted the label "Marxist historian" but rejected the idea of a distinct school. Evans reports that at a Birkbeck conference for his eightieth birthday he said there was no such thing as "Hobsbawmism" and no "Hobsbawmians". His Marxism was unorthodox: he defended Marx against the caricature of economic determinism, insisted that "what historians investigate is real" against postmodern relativism, and described history as a social science in the *Annales* sense. The Popular Front of the 1930s was, by his own admission in *Interesting Times*, the formation that continued to determine his strategic thinking; George Souvlis argues that it shaped not only his politics but the way he wrote history ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Souvlis 2018](https://praticasdahistoria.pt/article/download/22448/16554/87110)).

## Major contributions

His most durable contribution was a scheme of periodisation. The three volumes on the "long nineteenth century" ran from the dual revolution — industrial in Britain, political in France — to 1914; the fourth added the "short twentieth century" from 1914 to the collapse of the USSR. The idea of a long nineteenth century existed before him, but Anette Schlimm concludes that he was instrumental in popularising it, and Richard Evans notes that textbooks followed it ([Schlimm 2019](https://www.ghil.ac.uk/fileadmin/redaktion/dokumente/bulletin/GHIL_Bulletin_41.2/GHIL%20Bulletin%2041%20%282019%29%2C2%20-%20095%20-%20The%20Guerrilla%20Historian.pdf); [Evans 2018](https://www.ghil.ac.uk/fileadmin/redaktion/dokumente/bulletin/GHIL_Bulletin_40.1/GHIL%20Bulletin%2040%20%282018%29%2C1%20-%20007%20-%20Writing%20the%20History%20of%20Nineteenth-Century%20Europe.pdf)).

His second contribution was to widen what counted as the subject of history. *Labouring Men* (1964) collected studies of machine-breakers, tramping artisans, the labour aristocracy and the standard-of-living debate, treating workers' own practices rather than only unions and leaders. *Primitive Rebels* (1959) introduced "social banditry" and studied millenarian peasants, anarchists and pre-industrial city mobs: forms of politics, in his words, of people who had not acquired "the modern vocabulary, syntax, grammar, institutions and means of action of politics" ([Books & Ideas interview](https://booksandideas.net/Where-have-the-rebels-gone); [Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf)).

Third, *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), co-edited with Terence Ranger, gave historians and anthropologists a portable concept: practices presented as ancient but often instituted recently, such as the royal Christmas broadcast of 1932 or the invented ceremonies of nation-states. Hobsbawm's own introduction says the term covers traditions "actually invented, constructed and formally instituted" and those emerging rapidly within a short, dateable period ([Cambridge University Press, Introduction: Inventing Traditions](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/invention-of-tradition/introduction-inventing-traditions/05B9EDFC0304BE3F5D704BB66B286710)). *Nations and Nationalism since 1780* (1990) applied a related argument to nationalism: nations are made by states and elites rather than the reverse ([IRS review of Nations and Nationalism](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/7183DB004F129A4DBD63934A084B2400/S002085900011096Xa.pdf/ej_hobsbawm_nations_and_nationalism_since_1780_programme_myth_reality_cambridge_university_press_cambridge_etc_1990_viii_191_pp_1495.pdf)).

His 1978 Marx Memorial Lecture, published as "The Forward March of Labour Halted?", argued that the size, power and class consciousness of the British labour movement had peaked around thirty years earlier and were being eroded by occupational and ethnic division, sectional wage militancy and the decline of traditional working-class communities. He insisted this was not inevitable: "marxists are not economic and social determinists", and the task was to "analyse it realistically and concretely" and to formulate "not only what we would want to do, but what can be done" ([1978 lecture text](https://rtraba.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/78_09_hobsbawm.pdf)).

## Analysis of capitalism

Hobsbawm's stated organising principle for the nineteenth-century trilogy was "the triumph and transformation of capitalism in the historically specific forms of bourgeois society in its liberal version" (quoted by Kocka from *The Age of Empire*, pp. 8–9). *The Age of Revolution* treated the industrial and political revolutions as one "dual revolution" whose consequences reached into war, diplomacy, agriculture, government, science, religion and the arts. *The Age of Capital* covered the years in which bourgeois society stabilised; he illustrated the 1850s boom with figures such as the doubling of British cotton exports and the rise of Prussian joint-stock capital from 45 to 114.5 million taler. *The Age of Empire* shifted from an economic account of imperialism towards prestige and great-power strategy, while insisting that the nineteenth century had created "a single global economy" reaching into the most remote corners of the world ([Schlimm 2019](https://www.ghil.ac.uk/fileadmin/redaktion/dokumente/bulletin/GHIL_Bulletin_41.2/GHIL%20Bulletin%2041%20%282019%29%2C2%20-%20095%20-%20The%20Guerrilla%20Historian.pdf); [Høgsbjerg 2018](http://isj.org.uk/eric-hobsbawms-histories/); [Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf)).

He did not write a closed economic model. Schlimm notes that he "refrained from identifying unambiguous causalities", that structural change and contingent events were intertwined in his narrative, and that contemporaries' hopes and fears were treated as a historical factor: "both expected it, not through some historic automatism, but through effort and struggle" (quoted from *The Age of Empire*, p. 339). His account of *Age of Extremes* organised the short century as a triptych — catastrophe, golden age, landslide — and stressed that the "Golden Age" of growth and social transformation probably changed human society more profoundly than any comparable period ([Age of Extremes preface, scanned edition](https://ia801506.us.archive.org/27/items/112200_202307/The%20age%20of%20extremes%20%20a%20history%20of%20the%20world%2C%201914-1991.pdf)).

Scholars have pressed on weak points in this analysis. Evans judged his treatment of industrialisation in *The Age of Revolution* unambiguously negative, close to slavery, and inattentive to the emancipatory effects of the decline of servile tenure. Schlimm argues that he largely equated capitalism with industrialism, which makes the concept undifferentiated, and that his "world history" retained a clear north-west European centre from which change radiated outward. Evans calls the model of Europe's relations with the rest of the world one of stimulus by the former and response by the latter ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Schlimm 2019](https://www.ghil.ac.uk/fileadmin/redaktion/dokumente/bulletin/GHIL_Bulletin_41.2/GHIL%20Bulletin%2041%20%282019%29%2C2%20-%20095%20-%20The%20Guerrilla%20Historian.pdf)).

## Analysis of socialism

Hobsbawm wrote no blueprint of a socialist society, and the dossier should say so plainly. What he left instead is a political judgement about which institutions and alignments could defend ordinary people against capitalism, together with a history of why the movements that claimed socialism succeeded or failed. In the 2012 IPPR interview he called politics "the only effective mechanism for social redistribution", "the necessary support, the 'employer of last resort' for de-industrialised labour, the guarantor of retirement pension, the necessary foundation of mass education" and "the last bastion against the free market". He defended the state and the public sector against neo-liberalism, and judged New Labour to have continued "Thatcherism in trousers" after 1997 ([IPPR interview](https://www.ippr.org/articles/the-forward-march-eric-hobsbawm-in-conversation-with-jonathan-rutherford)).

His relation to actually existing socialism was more complicated than advocacy. Evans records that after 1956 he stopped being active in the CPGB but stayed because the global Communist movement remained, in his view, "humankind's best hope for the future"; Souvlis, using *Interesting Times*, shows that he continued to value the USSR as a non-capitalist state and an opponent of imperialism in the Global South even after it ceased to be a normative model. In the 1980s he backed the Popular-Front-style politics of the Italian Communist Party and the "realistic Marxism" of *Marxism Today*, including the campaign against the Labour left; he later conceded that his optimism about the PCI had been misplaced and distanced himself from Blairism ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Souvlis 2018](https://praticasdahistoria.pt/article/download/22448/16554/87110); [IPPR interview](https://www.ippr.org/articles/the-forward-march-eric-hobsbawm-in-conversation-with-jonathan-rutherford)).

*Age of Extremes* treated "real socialism" as one of the century's major experiments and recorded its collapse without treating that collapse as simple progress. Kocka notes that he perceived 1991 "mainly as a historical loss, and not as a step forward on the way to more democracy and freedom", and that his account of the twentieth century ends gloomily. Critics from several directions found this compromise telling: Evans reports Stanley Hoffmann's view that by the late 1960s Hobsbawm had become a political and cultural conservative; Høgsbjerg argues that his "soft-Stalinist Eurocommunism" limited his contribution to Marxist theory; and Schlimm notes he was strongly criticised for a "defense of Stalinism" after the Soviet Union fell ([Kocka 2013](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/eric-j-hobsbawm-19172012/608B6BD01721D05F0623CD4CA32567D4); [Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Høgsbjerg 2018](http://isj.org.uk/eric-hobsbawms-histories/); [Schlimm 2019](https://www.ghil.ac.uk/fileadmin/redaktion/dokumente/bulletin/GHIL_Bulletin_41.2/GHIL%20Bulletin%2041%20%282019%29%2C2%20-%20095%20-%20The%20Guerrilla%20Historian.pdf)).

## Intellectual influences

His own interviews and memoir point to a small set of clear influences. In the 2010 *Books & Ideas* interview he said he was inspired "not so much by Marx but more by people like Georges Lefebvre, and in a distinct way Gramsci (and by his interest in the subaltern classes)", and described the generation of historians who sought to marry historical scholarship with the social sciences. In the same interview he defended his organisational view of politics by saying that "one of the reasons why I was communist was the enormous force of communist parties as an organization which was capable of pulling together these forces and turning them into historically active forces" ([Books & Ideas](https://booksandideas.net/Where-have-the-rebels-gone)).

The 1983 "Marx and History" lecture puts Marx at the centre: "Marx remains the essential base of any adequate study of history... his only real rival as a theoretical influence on historians" being Max Weber, with whose work he contrasted Marx's capacity to ask questions about "the entire process of human social evolution" and its "extraordinarily uneven and combined" course (quoted in [Høgsbjerg 2018](http://isj.org.uk/eric-hobsbawms-histories/)). Evans adds Maurice Dobb, the *Annales* school, the Cambridge economic historians of the 1930s, Italian Marxists and Gramsci in the 1950s; Kocka records that in 2008 Hobsbawm grouped himself with economic history, the *Annales*, Marxism and Weber as so many routes away from Rankean "past politics" ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Kocka 2013](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/eric-j-hobsbawm-19172012/608B6BD01721D05F0623CD4CA32567D4)). George Weidenfeld commissioned *The Age of Revolution* as part of a projected *History of Civilization*; Max Gluckman invited the Manchester lectures behind *Primitive Rebels*; Piero Sraffa supplied contacts for the Italian research ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf)).

## Influence and legacy

The long nineteenth century became standard terminology: Schlimm found it in professorships, examination subjects and university courses, and Evans notes that textbooks adopted the scheme. The "dual revolution" shaped Hans-Ulrich Wehler's *Deutsche Gesellschaftsgeschichte*. *The Invention of Tradition* was adopted across history and anthropology almost immediately, and *Nations and Nationalism since 1780* became one of the standard works alongside Gellner, Anderson, Smith and Breuilly ([Schlimm 2019](https://www.ghil.ac.uk/fileadmin/redaktion/dokumente/bulletin/GHIL_Bulletin_41.2/GHIL%20Bulletin%2041%20%282019%29%2C2%20-%20095%20-%20The%20Guerrilla%20Historian.pdf); [Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf)).

His institutional legacy lies in *Past and Present*, the Communist Party Historians' Group and the broadening of social history into what he called the history of society. His books sold in millions and were translated into dozens of languages; *Age of Extremes* went into more than fifty. Yet he founded no school. Evans records that supervision did not produce a coherent body of pupils, and that both Festschriften were eclectic. He quoted Tony Judt's phrase "Hobsbawm generation" while insisting there were no Hobsbawmians. Samuel and Stedman Jones remarked that ideas recognisably Marxist in inspiration had lost their sectarian qualities and become "almost the 'common sense' of these subjects" ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Kocka 2013](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/eric-j-hobsbawm-19172012/608B6BD01721D05F0623CD4CA32567D4)).

His political legacy is contested. Høgsbjerg and other writers on the left credit *Marxism Today* with helping to shift the Labour Party, while arguing that this shift helped prepare New Labour in a form Hobsbawm himself rejected. Brotherstone's *Critique* essay argues that his refusal to break with Stalinism shaped his history and that his celebrity obscured that problem ([Høgsbjerg 2018](http://isj.org.uk/eric-hobsbawms-histories/); [Brotherstone 2013](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03017605.2013.805003)).

## Criticisms and controversies

**Periodisation.** Evans calls the long nineteenth century "in terms of political history at least, a broken-backed century": its first half is dominated by the legacy of the French Revolution, its second by new institutions and the fitful advance of democracy. Schlimm finds that Hobsbawm never explicitly defined or problematised the term, and that the triad of revolution, capital and empire did not by itself make 125 years into one era; she reconstructs the underlying Marxist grand narrative from the structure of the books rather than from any methodological statement by the author. She notes Charles S. Maier's *American Historical Review* article of 2000, which rejects the vogue for "long" and "short" centuries as forcing evidence into "the Procrustean bed of secular structures" and names Hobsbawm. Lawrence Freedman's review in *Reviews in History* said of the short century's subdivisions that "Neither the periodization nor the labelling are particularly felicitous", and Garry Wills argued in the *New York Review of Books* that "Naming these periods as if they were single things is a dubious exercise" ([Evans 2018](https://www.ghil.ac.uk/fileadmin/redaktion/dokumente/bulletin/GHIL_Bulletin_40.1/GHIL%20Bulletin%2040%20%282018%29%2C1%20-%20007%20-%20Writing%20the%20History%20of%20Nineteenth-Century%20Europe.pdf); [Schlimm 2019](https://www.ghil.ac.uk/fileadmin/redaktion/dokumente/bulletin/GHIL_Bulletin_41.2/GHIL%20Bulletin%2041%20%282019%29%2C2%20-%20095%20-%20The%20Guerrilla%20Historian.pdf); [Freedman 1997](https://reviews.history.ac.uk/review/28); [Wills 1999](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1999/07/15/a-readers-guide-to-the-century/)).

**Eurocentrism, gender and the concept of capitalism.** Ravi Arvind Palat's review of *The Age of Extremes* called the project "deeply flawed by its very pronounced Eurocentrism"; Edward Said, in Evans's summary, objected to its Eurocentrism, its treatment of politicised religion and its deep pessimism. Schlimm documents the absence of women as a cross-cutting category; Hobsbawm himself noted in 1997 that his 1971 essay contained "no reference at all to women's history", and the third volume's chapter on the "new woman" concluded that for the great majority of the world's women "there was as yet no change whatever". Schlimm also argues that his equation of capitalism with industrialism leaves his central concept under-differentiated, and that his work is largely absent from current research on capitalism ([Palat 1997](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/179261/summary); [Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Schlimm 2019](https://www.ghil.ac.uk/fileadmin/redaktion/dokumente/bulletin/GHIL_Bulletin_41.2/GHIL%20Bulletin%2041%20%282019%29%2C2%20-%20095%20-%20The%20Guerrilla%20Historian.pdf)).

**Reductionism and the "from below" question.** Evans judges the class model of *The Age of Revolution* reductive, especially its treatment of 1830 and the radical politics of the 1830s and 1840s, its neglect of serfdom and its characterisation of the Paris Commune as a workers' revolution. Høgsbjerg, following Logie Barrow and Carlin and Birchall, argues that Hobsbawm gave structure more attention than agency, was suspicious of working-class self-activity and treated "the primitive" and "the modern" as an evolutionary hierarchy with disciplined Communist parties at the top. The "Forward March" thesis was attacked in *International Socialism* for equating the fate of the working class with Labour's electoral vote, for economism and for neglecting women and black workers ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Høgsbjerg 2018](http://isj.org.uk/eric-hobsbawms-histories/); [Carlin and Birchall 1983](https://www.marxists.info/history/etol/writers/birchall/1983/xx/hobsbawm.htm); [Callinicos 1985](https://www.marxists.org/history/etol/writers/callinicos/1985/xx/marxtoday.html)).

**The Communist Party and Stalinism.** This is the sharpest dispute. Tony Judt, quoted by Evans, wrote that "others left the Party in despair because it had meant so much to them; Hobsbawm was able to remain because, in his daily life at least, it meant so little"; he also accused Hobsbawm's prose of retreating into "hooded, wooden language" on politically sensitive subjects and of never engaging "the moral as well as the political heritage of Stalin and his works". Evans replies that moral denunciation is not the historian's purpose. Brotherstone's abstract says Hobsbawm's reputation "overrode criticisms of his persistent refusal to radically break with Stalinism", and that his politics influenced his history. Kocka judges that the political creed sometimes constrained his analysis but more often supplied the energy for it. Schlimm records the "defense of Stalinism" charge. Donald Sassoon's *New Left Review* notice records the opposite side of the case: Hobsbawm admitted errors and the belated realization of Stalin's crimes, but never renounced being a Communist ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf); [Brotherstone 2013](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03017605.2013.805003); [Kocka 2013](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/eric-j-hobsbawm-19172012/608B6BD01721D05F0623CD4CA32567D4); [Schlimm 2019](https://www.ghil.ac.uk/fileadmin/redaktion/dokumente/bulletin/GHIL_Bulletin_41.2/GHIL%20Bulletin%2041%20%282019%29%2C2%20-%20095%20-%20The%20Guerrilla%20Historian.pdf); [Sassoon 2012](http://newleftreview.org/issues/ii77/articles/donald-sassoon-eric-hobsbawm-1917-2012)).

**Method and evidence in *Age of Extremes*.** Hobsbawm disarmed some criticism in advance. In the preface he wrote that his knowledge of twentieth-century historiography was "casual and patchy even by the standards of historical erudition in the field of contemporary history", that he had done no more than "dip into the literature of particularly thorny and controverted questions", and that the book therefore "rests on curiously uneven foundations". He described himself as a "participant observer" and listed the daily press, UN and World Bank publications as central sources. This candour should be reported alongside the charge, made by Walter McDougall and noted by Evans, that the book was "ignorant, impressionistic, and prejudiced" ([Age of Extremes preface](https://ia801506.us.archive.org/27/items/112200_202307/The%20age%20of%20extremes%20%20a%20history%20of%20the%20world%2C%201914-1991.pdf); [Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf)).

## Related thinkers

- **Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels** — the methodological base; Hobsbawm edited Marx's *Pre-Capitalist Economic Formations* in 1964 and wrote introductions to *Condition of the Working Class in England* (1969) and the *Communist Manifesto* (1998). He defended Marx's base-and-superstructure formulation against "vulgar Marxism" ([Høgsbjerg 2018](http://isj.org.uk/eric-hobsbawms-histories/); [Edinburgh bibliography](https://hobsbawm.shca.ed.ac.uk/bibl/introduction-karl-marx-pre-capitalist)).
- **Maurice Dobb** — CPGB economist whose transition debate shaped the Historians' Group ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf)).
- **Antonio Gramsci, Georges Lefebvre, Fernand Braudel and the *Annales*** — named influences, especially for subaltern classes and total history ([Books & Ideas](https://booksandideas.net/Where-have-the-rebels-gone); [Høgsbjerg 2018](http://isj.org.uk/eric-hobsbawms-histories/)).
- **E. P. Thompson, Christopher Hill, Rodney Hilton, George Rudé, Raphael Samuel, John Saville** — fellow members of the Historians' Group; Thompson and Hilton co-signed the 1956 protest letter, Rudé co-authored *Captain Swing* ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf)).
- **Terence Ranger** — co-editor of *The Invention of Tradition*; **Hugh Trevor-Roper, David Cannadine, Prys Morgan and Bernard S. Cohen** — contributors ([CUP contents](https://www.cambridge.org/gb/universitypress/subjects/history/regional-and-world-history-general-interest/invention-tradition-2?format=PB&isbn=9781107604674)).
- **Ernest Gellner, Benedict Anderson, Anthony D. Smith** — the other major theorists of nationalism against whose work *Nations and Nationalism since 1780* was read ([Evans 2015](https://thebritishacademy.ac.uk/sites/default/files/10%20Hobsbawn%201820.pdf)).
- **Tony Judt, Perry Anderson, Jürgen Kocka, Richard J. Evans, Anette Schlimm** — critics and interpreters whose assessments are used above; **Alex Callinicos, Norah Carlin, Ian Birchall, Christian Høgsbjerg and Terry Brotherstone** — critics from within or adjacent to the Marxist tradition ([sources listed below](#sources-cited)).
