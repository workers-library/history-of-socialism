It reports arguments as claims made by Chomsky or by his critics. It does not convert them into established history. ## 1. Biography

Noam Chomsky was born on 7 December 1928 in Philadelphia. The biographical note that Chomsky's own website reproduces from MIT gives the date and city, and the note it reproduces from the University of Arizona gives the same. Both records describe a career divided between linguistics and political writing, but they differ in emphasis: the MIT note lists his linguistic publications and named lectures, while the Arizona note calls him founder of modern linguistics and one of the most cited scholars in modern history ([chomsky.info 2002](https://chomsky.info/2002____/); [University of Arizona, 2017](https://chomsky.info/noam-chomsky-ua/)).

He studied at the University of Pennsylvania, receiving his BA and PhD there; the PhD was awarded in 1955. His dissertation was titled "Transformational Analysis", and its main theoretical claims appeared in the 1957 monograph *Syntactic Structures*. From 1951 to 1955 he was a Junior Fellow of the Harvard Society of Fellows. He joined MIT in 1955, became a full professor in 1961, held the Ferrari P. Ward Professorship from 1966 to 1976, and was appointed Institute Professor in 1976. He spent 1958–59 at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton and delivered the John Locke Lectures at Oxford in 1969, the Bertrand Russell Memorial Lecture at Cambridge in 1970, the Nehru Memorial Lecture in New Delhi in 1972 and the Huizinga Lecture in Leiden in 1977 ([chomsky.info 2002](https://chomsky.info/2002____/)). The MIT Linguistics page now lists him as Institute Professor and Professor of Linguistics emeritus ([MIT Linguistics](https://linguistics.mit.edu/user/chomsky/)). The Arizona record says he joined that university in autumn 2017 after his long MIT career ([University of Arizona, 2017](https://chomsky.info/noam-chomsky-ua/)).

Chomsky's own account of his political formation is richer than these institutional summaries. In an interview excerpted from *The Chomsky Reader* he describes an experimental, Deweyite school attended until about the age of twelve, where there was no ranking of pupils and "the sense was that everyone was doing something important". High school was the opposite: he discovered he was a good student, disliked the competition and regimentation, and remembered almost nothing of the classes. At the University of Pennsylvania he planned to leave after two years to pursue political interests. The philosophy course of C. West Churchman, Arabic classes connected partly to his political interests and partly to his father's work in Hebrew, and the antifascist exile Giorgio Levi Della Vida mattered to him. Through these interests he met the linguist Zellig Harris, whose broad political and intellectual circle drew him into graduate work in linguistics and philosophy. Nelson Goodman recommended him for the Society of Fellows ([Chomsky, "Personal influences"](https://chomsky.info/reader01/)).

The same interview gives the political background. Chomsky describes his teenage politics as "anarchist or left-wing (anti-Leninist) Marxist", combined with a socialist, binationalist view of Palestine and support for the kibbutzim and cooperative labour institutions of the pre-state Jewish settlement. He was "strongly anti-Bolshevik" and could find no organised group that shared his views. An uncle ran a newsstand at Seventy-second Street and Broadway that served as a meeting place for radical debate in the late 1930s. His first article, written at about ten, was a school-newspaper editorial on the fall of Barcelona. He recalls the Depression, police strikebreaking, the rise of nazism in a hostile neighbourhood, and his isolation on the day of the Hiroshima bombing ([Chomsky, "Personal influences"](https://chomsky.info/reader01/)). He spent a short period on a kibbutz in 1953; in the same interview he says both "a few months" and "about six weeks", so the length is not settled by that source. He admired what he called a functioning libertarian community but was repelled by ideological conformity, by the defence of the Slánský trials, and by the exclusion of Arabs under land law ([Chomsky, "Personal influences"](https://chomsky.info/reader01/)).

The 1967 essay "The Responsibility of Intellectuals", first published in the *New York Review of Books* on 23 February 1967, brought him a wider political audience. The essay begins from Dwight Macdonald's question about collective guilt and turns it toward American intellectuals: "Intellectuals are in a position to expose the lies of governments, to analyze actions according to their causes and motives and often hidden intentions." Its central sentence is blunt: "It is the responsibility of intellectuals to speak the truth and to expose lies." The essay examines the Vietnam war, the Bay of Pigs, and the willingness of academics to treat criticism of American policy as irresponsible while accepting official accounts without evidence ([Chomsky 1967](https://chomsky.info/19670223/)). A New Press edition later paired it with a retrospective essay written on the tenth anniversary of 9/11 ([The New Press description](https://chomsky.info/books/)).

## 2. Intellectual tradition

Chomsky's political position is best described as libertarian socialism in the anarchist and anarcho-syndicalist tradition, with a strong affinity to council communism and to the anti-Bolshevik left Marxism of Anton Pannekoek and Paul Mattick. Anarcho-syndicalism here means the view that workers' organisations should own and run production directly, rather than captured state power doing it on their behalf. In "Notes on Anarchism", written as an introduction to Daniel Guérin's *Anarchism: From Theory to Practice* in 1970, he states that "a consistent anarchist must be a socialist, but a socialist of a particular sort": such a person opposes private ownership of the means of production and wage labour, but also opposes "the organization of production by government". He quotes Pannekoek's "Five Theses on the Class Struggle" to make the point that the working class cannot reach liberation by "a new directing and governing class substituting itself for the bourgeoisie" ([Chomsky 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/)).

In a 1970 talk published as "Government in the Future", Chomsky set out four positions: classical liberalism, libertarian socialism, state socialism and state capitalism. He identified his own view at the start: "the libertarian socialist concepts, and by that I mean a range of thinking that extends from left-wing Marxism through to anarchism", were "fundamentally correct" and the proper extension of classical liberalism into industrial society. State socialism, which he identified with "what has become of Bolshevism", and state capitalism, which he identified with the modern welfare state, were in his view "regressive and highly inadequate social theories" ([Chomsky 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

This places him at a distance from writers who treat the Soviet Union or other communist states as imperfect but genuine socialist projects. In "The Soviet Union Versus Socialism", published in *Our Generation* in 1986, he argues that the claim that the society built by Lenin, Trotsky, Stalin and their successors had any meaningful relation to socialism is a "fantasy" shared by "the world's two great propaganda systems"; the actual relation, he writes, "is the relation of contradiction". He defines socialism as "the liberation of working people from exploitation" and "mastery over production by the producers". He condemns the Bolshevik destruction of factory committees and soviets, and calls the structures created under Lenin and Trotsky "proto-fascist structures converted by Stalin into one of the horrors of the modern age" ([Chomsky 1986](https://chomsky.info/1986____/)). This is Chomsky's polemical claim; it is not a finding established by this dossier.

He also rejected labels applied to him from other directions. In the *Chomsky Reader* interview he says he never joined a political organisation, because every group he knew on the left was Leninist, "either Stalinist or Trotskyite", and he saw little difference between them. He was drawn emotionally to Hashomer Hatzair but remained unaffiliated ([Chomsky, "Personal influences"](https://chomsky.info/reader01/)). In the 1971 debate with Michel Foucault he identified the concept of human nature as central to his own thinking and defended the place of innate structure in explanations of language and knowledge; Foucault challenged precisely that move ([Chomsky–Foucault debate](https://chomsky.info/1971xxxx/)). Cohen and Rogers, writing sympathetically in *New Left Review* in 1991, report that Chomsky denies originality for his social views and calls himself "a merely 'derivative fellow traveller' in the anarchist and libertarian socialist traditions" ([Cohen and Rogers 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky)).

## 3. Major contributions

The contribution most relevant to this book is the propaganda model developed with Edward S. Herman in *Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media*. The model starts from the claim that in "a world of concentrated wealth and major conflicts of class interest", the media's integrating function "requires systematic propaganda". It then identifies five filters through which news passes: concentrated ownership and profit orientation; advertising as the main income source; reliance on government, business and approved "experts" for information; "flak" as a means of disciplining the media; and anticommunism as "a national religion and control mechanism". The authors argue that these filters fix "the premises of discourse and interpretation" and explain why dissident material is marginalised without any formal censorship ([Herman and Chomsky, chapter 1, full text](https://files.libcom.org/files/2022-04/manufacturing_consent.pdf)). In a 1997 interview Chomsky explains the method: study the institution's internal structure, study its setting in relation to other systems of power, and look for internal records in which insiders explain themselves to one another. He adds a "second-order prediction": that the first-order finding will itself be excluded from respectable discussion ([Chomsky 1997](https://chomsky.info/199710__/)).

The second contribution is his account of the responsibility of intellectuals. The 1967 essay argues that privileged people with access to information have a duty to expose government lies, and that the "cult of the experts" serves power by declaring moral and political questions too complex for ordinary citizens ([Chomsky 1967](https://chomsky.info/19670223/)). In an *Understanding Power* excerpt he applies the same standard to his own intellectual community, recounting how Norman Finkelstein's demonstration that Joan Peters's *From Time Immemorial* was fraudulent was met with silence in the United States and with reviews in Britain that used his work without acknowledgement. Chomsky presents this as an example of how ideological control works, while noting that Finkelstein later published books with independent presses ([Chomsky, "The Fate of an Honest Intellectual"](https://chomsky.info/power01/)).

Third is his account of state power and foreign policy. *Hegemony or Survival: America's Quest for Global Dominance* argues that the United States has pursued a "grand imperial strategy" for more than half a century, and describes a drive toward "full spectrum dominance" that in his view endangers human survival ([Macmillan publisher record](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781429900218/hegemonyorsurvival/); [Chomsky's book list](https://chomsky.info/books/)). The 1970 "Government in the Future" talk supplies the domestic counterpart: a claim that "capitalism and democracy are ultimately quite incompatible", because the central institutions of the economy are placed outside democratic control and private power shapes what remains within it ([Chomsky 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

Fourth is a theory of social change built on ordinary capacities. Against the view that people are easily manipulated, Chomsky argues in *The Chomsky Reader* interview that people apply considerable intelligence to sport but are discouraged from applying it to public affairs, because they cannot see any way to affect decisions. He calls the capacity to see through official accounts "Cartesian common sense": it needs "normal skepticism and willingness to apply one's analytical skills", not specialist training. He concedes a criticism from friends that much of his work addresses particular atrocities rather than "revolutionary strategy", and admits the force of the point while defending the practical value of concrete cases ([Chomsky, "What the World is Really Like"](https://chomsky.info/reader02/)).

## 4. Analysis of capitalism

Chomsky's account of capitalism centres on the claim that modern capitalism has never been a free market left to itself. In "Government in the Future" he argues that Humboldt's classical liberalism, written before industrial capitalism, was in essence "profoundly, though prematurely, anticapitalist", because its premise that people develop through free, self-directed activity also condemns wage labour and alienated work. Humboldt did not foresee the corporate economy; once that economy existed, the same liberal assumptions led logically to opposition to both state and private domination ([Chomsky 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)). Chomsky quotes Marx's account of alienated labour and of the worker "mutilated into a fragment of a human being" to show what he takes to be a convergence between early Marx and libertarian thought.

In the same talk he separates political and economic power and argues that the range of decisions open to democratic control "excludes in law in principle the central institutions in any advanced industrial society, i.e. the entire commercial, industrial and financial system". Within the narrow democratic sphere, private power exerts influence through control of the media and political organisations and by supplying personnel to government. He quotes Richard Barnet's finding that most of the top 400 decision makers in the postwar national security system came from a small cluster of city blocks, and he cites a Federal Trade Commission report on the concentration of manufacturing assets. His conclusion is that "the democratic system at best functions within a narrow range in a capitalist democracy", and that "capitalism and democracy are ultimately quite incompatible" ([Chomsky 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

He also treats militarised state spending as a form of support for private capital. In the 1970 talk he quotes business historian Alfred Chandler on the economic effects of the Second World War and the persistence of central control, and concludes that government acts as "a coordinator of last resort" when private managers cannot sustain demand. He calls the cold war a propaganda device that served "the long time imperial enterprise" and helped the managers of both great power blocs control their own populations ([Chomsky 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)). Alison Edgley, writing in 2009, summarises this as the claim that the state supplies the legislative conditions and coercive authority for capitalism, is itself a major economic actor, and provides "welfare for the rich" through subsidy and guaranteed markets ([Edgley 2009](https://www.westminsterpapers.org/article/id/128/download/pdf/)).

Class appears in Chomsky's work less as a category of income than as a relation of control. He treats wage labour as "wage slavery" when the worker sells labour power because of need rather than choosing work freely, and treats hierarchy inside the firm as a political question: "industrial feudalism" is his phrase for autocratic control of production. In "Notes on Anarchism" he insists that the reduction of people to "an appurtenance of the machine" could in principle be reduced by technology, "but not under the conditions of autocratic control of production by those who make man an instrument to serve their ends" ([Chomsky 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/)). In the 1997 media interview he describes corporations as "basically tyrannies, hierarchic, controled from above", and argues that universities and media reproduce the same hierarchy through dependence on outside funders and through socialisation that selects for obedience ([Chomsky 1997](https://chomsky.info/199710__/)).

## 5. Analysis of socialism

Chomsky has written no blueprint for a fully specified socialist economy, and he explicitly distrusts fixed social theory. Cohen and Rogers observe that he "does not have a theory of society or justice, in the sense of a clearly elaborated and defended set of fundamental principles", and that he treats social and ethical thought as relying on "guesses, hopes, expectations" until a theory of human nature exists ([Cohen and Rogers 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky)). Within that limit, his preference is clear. In "Government in the Future" he defines the libertarian socialist position as the elimination of state power in favour of "the democratic organization of the industrial society with direct popular control over all institutions by those who participate in as well as those who are directly affected by the workings of these institutions". He imagines "workers' councils, consumer councils, commune assemblies, regional federations" with representatives who are "directly answerable to and return directly to" the group they represent ([Chomsky 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

He argues that "one might argue, or at least I would argue, that council communism in this sense is the natural form of revolutionary socialism in an industrial society", because democracy is "largely a sham" whenever production is controlled by "owners, managers, technocrats, a vanguard party, a state bureaucracy". He rejects two standard objections. Against the claim that free cooperation is contrary to human nature, he cites Rousseau, Kant, Humboldt and Luxemburg for the view that people learn freedom only by exercising it. Against the claim that democratic control is inefficient, he answers that the same technology which brings information to managers can bring it to the whole workforce, and that "the very concept of efficiency is dripping with ideology" ([Chomsky 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)).

"Notes on Anarchism" adds that the workers' organisations must create "not only the ideas, but also the facts of the future itself" before a revolution, quoting Bakunin. He praises the Spanish Revolution of 1936 as a case where years of patient organisation in unions and assemblies prepared workers to collectivise industry and agriculture. He warns that "spontaneous revolutionary action" is a misleading phrase if it suggests that no preparation occurred ([Chomsky 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/)). The essay also states the refusal of state socialism in the strongest terms: "control of production by a state bureaucracy, no matter how benevolent its intentions, also does not create the conditions under which labor, manual and intellectual, can become the highest want in life" ([Chomsky 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/)).

The 1986 essay on the Soviet Union applies this standard to actually existing communist states. Chomsky argues that socialism means workers mastering production, and that the Bolshevik leadership destroyed the factory committees and soviets that might have realised it. He ends by calling for "a way to save the socialist ideal from its enemies in both of the world's major centres of power" ([Chomsky 1986](https://chomsky.info/1986____/)). This is the sharpest difference between him and writers such as Michael Parenti or Domenico Losurdo, who treat historical communist projects with more sympathy; their positions were not researched in this dossier and no claim about their arguments is made here. In practice Chomsky has supported limited use of state authority. George Fish, a socialist reviewer, notes that *Chomsky on Anarchism* records "limited, pragmatic support for state authority in order to protect the people's rights and gains" and that Chomsky says he votes, especially in local elections ([Fish 2010](https://newpol.org/review/chomsky-anarchism-and-socialism/)). That observation comes from a critic and should be read as his interpretation of the collection.

## 8. Intellectual influences

Chomsky's own interviews provide the strongest evidence for influence. He names Zellig Harris as the person who drew him from prospective dropout into graduate linguistics and philosophy, and mentions C. West Churchman, Giorgio Levi Della Vida, Nelson Goodman, Morton White and the mathematician Nathan Fine as teachers who made his unconventional education productive. His uncle's radical newsstand and the Jewish working-class culture of New York, with its mixture of Freud, Marx, literature and chamber music, were in his words "the most influential intellectual culture during my early teens" ([Chomsky, "Personal influences"](https://chomsky.info/reader01/)). In the 1967 essay he explicitly credits Dwight Macdonald's articles on the responsibility of intellectuals, read as an undergraduate, with framing his own question about war guilt and intellectual duty ([Chomsky 1967](https://chomsky.info/19670223/)).

In political theory the line of influence runs through Bakunin, Proudhon, Rudolf Rocker, Daniel Guérin, Karl Polanyi and the early Marx, with Pannekoek, Mattick, Luxemburg and the young Trotsky as anti-Leninist Marxists he cites approvingly. "Notes on Anarchism" quotes Rocker, Bakunin, Guérin, Pannekoek, William Paul and Rosa Luxemburg, and treats Guérin's project of rehabilitating anarchism as a contribution that can "enrich Marxism" ([Chomsky 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/)). "Government in the Future" adds Wilhelm von Humboldt, Rousseau, Kant and Polanyi; Chomsky treats Humboldt's *The Limits of State Action* as a text whose premises lead, once industrial capitalism is understood, to libertarian socialism ([Chomsky 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)). In the media work he credits Walter Lippmann with the phrase "manufacture of consent" and Edward Bernays with the public-relations technique of engineering consent; these are influences he describes in order to reject them ([Chomsky 1997](https://chomsky.info/199710__/)).

The linguistic work matters here only because of what Chomsky and his interpreters make of it politically. In the 1971 debate he argues that the gap between the "small and rather degenerate" data given to a child and the rich knowledge the child acquires supports the postulate of innate "schematism", which he calls a constituent of human nature ([Chomsky–Foucault debate](https://chomsky.info/1971xxxx/)). Cohen and Rogers show how this conception connects to his politics: the human capacity for "normal creativity" underwrites both his moral claim that people need autonomy and his expectation that authoritarian systems must repress it ([Cohen and Rogers 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky)). Chris Knight, a Marxist critic, makes the same connection in order to attack it; Chomsky rejects Knight's account in a detailed reply ([Knight 2002/2010](http://radicalanthropologygroup.org/wp-content/uploads/pub_chomsky_politics_science.pdf); [Chomsky 2019](https://chomsky.info/20190905-2/)). These are disputes about method, not settled facts about the mind.

## 9. Influence and legacy

Chomsky's political influence is hard to measure because it is strongest in movements rather than in institutions. The documentary *Manufacturing Consent: Noam Chomsky and the Media* is one documented channel: Chomsky reports that it was shown on national television in many countries outside the United States, generated a large volume of reviews, and prompted letters from viewers who had organised screenings in their own communities. He also records the limits of that influence: he thought the film personalised collective work and turned viewers into would-be followers, and he says the same problem appears in public speaking and book signing ([Chomsky, "An Exchange on Manufacturing Consent"](https://chomsky.info/power02/)). The New Press describes *Understanding Power* as a collection of previously unpublished talks in which he links imperial foreign policy to declining domestic living standards and "establishes a theory of social change" ([The New Press](https://thenewpress.org/books/understanding-power/)).

Academic reception is a different story. Edgley notes that Chomsky's political work remains marginal within the social sciences, that academics often dismiss it as "polemical" without demonstrating that it is, and that his work is "not even read to be rejected" in some fields ([Edgley 2009](https://www.westminsterpapers.org/article/id/128/download/pdf/)). Mullen's peer-reviewed study of the propaganda model's reception reaches a similar conclusion from a different angle: using counts of journal articles and textbooks, it argues that the model has been systematically marginalised within media and communication studies, as Herman and Chomsky had predicted ([Mullen 2010](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/twenty-years-on-the-second-order-prediction-of-the-herman-chomsky/)). Mullen and Klaehn's companion article situates the propaganda model in the Marxist-radical tradition of media studies and sets out its three hypotheses and five operative principles ([Mullen and Klaehn 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-9020.2010.00275.x)). These studies support a claim about reception; they do not by themselves prove that the propaganda model is correct.

Cohen and Rogers's 1991 survey is evidence of the same duality. They treat Chomsky's social thought as a coherent and optimistic perspective worth reconstructing, while stressing that its central claims rest on a contested view of human nature and that Chomsky himself offers no systematic social theory ([Cohen and Rogers 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky)). For the purposes of this book, his legacy is best described as a set of questions and methods: who benefits from a given policy, whose sources count as evidence, and how institutions teach people to think about their own interests.

## 10. Criticisms and controversies

Criticism from within the Marxist tradition is substantial and takes several forms. Chris Knight's essay "Noam Chomsky: Politics or Science?", written in 2002, published in *What Next? Marxist discussion journal* in 2003 and revised in 2010, argues that Chomsky's linguistics and his anarchist politics had to be reconciled, and that he achieved this by presenting a formal, asocial theory of language as a liberating science. Knight points to the military funding acknowledged in the preface to *Syntactic Structures* — the Army Signal Corps, Air Force research command, Navy research, the National Science Foundation and Eastman Kodak — and argues that a "cognitive revolution" stripped of meaning and social content suited the needs of the computer age and its sponsors. He further claims that Chomsky's turn to "normal creativity" and an innate language capacity was designed to make his linguistics harmless to his political conscience ([Knight 2002/2010](http://radicalanthropologygroup.org/wp-content/uploads/pub_chomsky_politics_science.pdf)). Chomsky's 2019 reply calls this a "web of deceit and misinformation", points out that the formal work began in the 1940s and 1950s before MIT, cites interviews with students at MITRE who said their work was syntax rather than command and control, and argues that military funding of universities in that period was general support for higher education under a defence label ([Chomsky 2019](https://chomsky.info/20190905-2/)). Both texts are polemical. The dossier records the dispute and does not adjudicate it.

A second Marxist objection concerns his treatment of the Russian Revolution. George Fish, reviewing *Chomsky on Anarchism* for the socialist journal *New Politics* in 2010, accepts that Chomsky's critique of Bolshevism has "elements of merit and political insight" but calls it "simplistic" where it treats Lenin and Trotsky as laying the foundations of Stalinism and traces some failures to Marx and Engels themselves. Fish also objects that Chomsky's account of intellectuals "deterministically reduces them to careerists, commissars, or apologists", and asks how Chomsky himself could emerge from such a class. At the same time Fish finds common ground, including Chomsky's defence of democracy against anarchist dismissals of it, his qualified support for state authority, and his critique of incomprehensible left-wing jargon ([Fish 2010](https://newpol.org/review/chomsky-anarchism-and-socialism/)). This is a review in a partisan journal, not a peer-reviewed study, and is tagged accordingly.

A third line of criticism is theoretical rather than Marxist. In the 1971 debate Foucault argues that "human nature" is not a scientific concept of the kind Chomsky claims but an "epistemological indicator" that marks off a domain of inquiry. On creativity, Foucault says his own concern is with collective rules and transformations of knowledge rather than the originating subject. He explicitly asks whether the regularities that make knowledge possible might be located "somewhere else, even outside the human mind, in social forms, in the relations of production, in the class struggles", and says he finds it difficult to accept Chomsky's placement of those regularities in human nature ([Chomsky–Foucault debate](https://chomsky.info/1971xxxx/)). Cohen and Rogers, though broadly sympathetic, make a related point: Chomsky's four key claims about moral nature, autonomy, social stability and hope are "neither concrete nor precise", and his political conclusions depend on a view of human nature that is "speculative and imprecise" ([Cohen and Rogers 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky)). Edgley likewise calls his account of human nature "essentialist (yet unverifiable, so open to amendment)", while judging that it gives his social analysis a distinctive and coherent foundation ([Edgley 2009](https://www.westminsterpapers.org/article/id/128/download/pdf/)).

Chomsky's work on Cambodia and the Khmer Rouge is a further area of dispute. Chomsky's own statements can be checked: in a 1983 interview excerpt he says that it was doubted by no one that the Khmer Rouge committed atrocities, while adding that Western propaganda exploited those crimes to justify Western violence and produced "a record of fabrication and deceit" that he and Herman documented. He describes the resulting outrage as a defence of "the right to lie in the service of the state" ([Chomsky, "What the World is Really Like"](https://chomsky.info/reader02/)). Critics of his position were found in the search results, but the pages returned were tertiary or aggregator pages and were not opened as evidence. The specific charge that he minimised Khmer Rouge crimes, and the scholarly responses to it, could not be verified from a primary or academic source in this research pass and should not be asserted here.

Finally, the propaganda model has drawn criticism for overstating the power of corporate and state elites. The abstract of Mullen and Klaehn's 2010 review records that reviewers of *Manufacturing Consent* "charged that the PM overstated the power of" elites, and Mullen's study measures the model's marginalisation in the field. The full text of those articles was not opened; the Wiley page returned a 403 and the CiteSeerX link returned HTML rather than a PDF. The criticism is real enough to record, but its exact wording and the evidence offered for it were not verified ([Mullen and Klaehn 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-9020.2010.00275.x); [Mullen 2010](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/twenty-years-on-the-second-order-prediction-of-the-herman-chomsky/)).

## 11. Related thinkers

Edward S. Herman is the indispensable partner for this profile. *Manufacturing Consent* is jointly authored, and Chomsky has said that Herman's name was placed first at his insistence because Herman did most of the book ([Chomsky, "An Exchange on Manufacturing Consent"](https://chomsky.info/power02/)). Herman also co-authored *Counter-Revolutionary Violence*, *After the Cataclysm* and *The Washington Connection and Third World Fascism*, all listed in the author's chronology ([Chomsky's book list](https://chomsky.info/books/)).

Michel Foucault is a related thinker through disagreement: both addressed power and knowledge, but Foucault rejected the appeal to a fixed human nature and located the conditions of knowledge in historical practices and social relations ([Chomsky–Foucault debate](https://chomsky.info/1971xxxx/)). Bakunin, Rocker, Guérin, Pannekoek, Mattick, Luxemburg and Polanyi are positive influences on the socialist side ([Chomsky 1970](https://chomsky.info/1970____/); [Chomsky 1970a](https://chomsky.info/government-in-the-future/)). Cohen and Rogers and Alison Edgley are scholarly interpreters whose work summarises and tests his social thought ([Cohen and Rogers 1991](https://newleftreview.org/issues/i187/articles/joshua-cohen-joel-rogers-knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky); [Edgley 2009](https://www.westminsterpapers.org/article/id/128/download/pdf/)). Chris Knight and George Fish are critics writing from Marxist or socialist positions ([Knight 2002/2010](http://radicalanthropologygroup.org/wp-content/uploads/pub_chomsky_politics_science.pdf); [Fish 2010](https://newpol.org/review/chomsky-anarchism-and-socialism/)). The connection to Michael Parenti and Domenico Losurdo is one of political contrast in the framing of this collection; their works were not opened here, so no claim about the content of their positions is made in this dossier.

### 1. The propaganda model: the full text of Mullen and Klaehn (2010)

The full 15-page article was opened by downloading the PDF and extracting its text from a non-publisher mirror that reproduces the journal pagination, DOI and author affiliations ([full text, mirror](https://aklatangbayan.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/mullen-klaehn-sociology-compass-propaganda-model.pdf)). Its identity as the *Sociology Compass* article was checked against the bibliographic records of two university repositories ([Strathclyde Strathprints](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/40906/); [Northumbria Research Portal](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/the-herman-chomsky-propaganda-model-a-critical-approach-to-analys/)). The publisher's full-text endpoint was not opened; it still returns HTTP 403. The mirror is not the publisher's file, and that limit is stated rather than hidden.

The earlier dossier recorded only the abstract's summary that reviewers "charged that the PM overstated the power of" elites. The opened text gives the fuller picture, and it is a review of the reception written by two scholars who support the model. In their account of a "first wave of criticism" in the late 1980s and 1990s, reviewers of *Manufacturing Consent* charged that the propaganda model overstated the power of the "propaganda system" and downplayed popular opposition to elite preferences (LeFeber 1988); presented a "conspiratorial" view of the media (Lemann 1989; Entman 1990a, 1990b; Nelson 1990); was a blunt instrument for analysis (Schudson 1989); was "political" (Salmon 1989); was deterministic, functionalist and simplistic (Schlesinger 1989; Golding and Murdock 1991; Eldridge 1993); and neglected journalistic professionalism (Goodwin 1994; Hallin 1994). Mullen and Klaehn add their own assessment that most of these writers did not engage with the model on its own terms and avoided the evidence Herman and Chomsky had assembled. That assessment is the reviewers' judgement, not an independent finding of this dossier, and the original reviews just named were not opened here.

For the "second wave", also reported rather than independently checked, the opened text records John Corner's six objections in *European Journal of Communication* in 2003: doubt that a model devised for the United States could apply to countries with very different media systems and political structures; doubt about its theoretical novelty for European research; the complaint that the five filters were "assumed to function without much, if any, need for further specification or qualification" and produced a "totalizing and finalizing view" of media performance; a question about whether filtering produces media messages or only modifies what is already produced; a charge that proponents ignore the European critical-Marxist research tradition on media, state and market; and questions about liberal principles, the awareness of media workers, and whether "brainwashing under freedom" and "thought control" make the model a theory of effects rather than of behaviour. Kurt and Gladys Lang, in *Political Communication* in 2004, are reported as objecting that Herman and Chomsky made a political point with their data, gave no information about sampling and coding, built a general model of "the media" on anecdotal evidence, assumed that information existed and had been screened out, underrated adversarial relations between sources and journalists and the protective effect of professional norms, ignored the fact that Chomsky can place a "deviant point of view" in the public domain through the very media he criticises, and misquoted Walter Lippmann. Oliver Boyd-Barrett is reported as accepting the basic premises but complaining that the model offered no way to weigh the relative power of the filters in different settings, that some filters lacked precision, and that it marginalised intentionality; he proposed a sixth filter for the buying out of journalists or media by intelligence agencies and other bodies.

Against these charges, the article's own evidence concerns reception. Of 3,053 articles sampled from ten European and North American media and communication journals over 1988–2007, only 79 (2.6 per cent) attended to the model, and most of those merely cited *Manufacturing Consent* in a bibliography. Of 48 textbooks used on British undergraduate and postgraduate courses and surveyed over 1990–2007, only 11 (22.9 per cent) engaged with the model, four extensively; 43.8 per cent ignored it and 33.3 per cent listed the book only in a bibliography. These figures support a claim about marginalisation in the field. They do not establish that the propaganda model is correct, nor do they settle any of the objections listed above.

### 2. Cohen and Rogers (1991): the complete article

The complete article, pages 5–27, was opened by downloading the PDF and extracting its text from the University of Wisconsin–Madison High Road Strategy Center ([full text](https://highroad.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2056/2020/04/1991-Knowledge-Morality-and-Hope-The-Social-Thought-of-Noam-Chomsky.pdf); [landing page](https://highroad.wisc.edu/publications/knowledge-morality-and-hope-the-social-thought-of-noam-chomsky/)). The subscription block that stopped the first research pass no longer limits what is recorded here.

The passages already quoted in section 10 are confirmed in the full text. Two further qualifications belong with them. Cohen and Rogers write: "In sum, we agree with Chomsky's claims about our moral nature and 'instinct for freedom', but are less persuaded by the suggestions about how these features of human nature might be understood on the model of the theory of language." They therefore say they will confine later references to Chomsky's "conception of human nature" to the points on which they agree. This is a precise, sympathetic disagreement about method: they accept the moral claim but not the analogy with his linguistics. Later, discussing whether societies hostile to freedom can change, they note that their account of his view of social order, "even admitting the amendments to that view we have suggested", may suggest that "he sees dominant groups as in some sense invincible", which makes his insistence that the struggle is "never hopeless" harder to ground. Their own conclusion remains favourable: they call his optimism "highly plausible" and his insistence on reason and moral hope "his signal contribution to social thought". The full article therefore strengthens, rather than overturns, the earlier description of them as broadly sympathetic critics.

### 3. Cambodia and the Khmer Rouge: the dispute can now be stated

Three kinds of evidence were opened and are kept separate.

**Chomsky's own position (primary).** The full text of "Distortions at Fourth Hand", co-authored with Edward Herman, was opened ([text](https://chomsky.info/19770625/)). It says that "executions have numbered at most in the thousands" in analyses by specialists the authors cite, calls the Barron and Paul book a "third-rate propaganda tract", finds Ponchaud's book "serious and worth reading" but says it "plays fast and loose with quotes and with numbers", and concludes: "We do not pretend to know where the truth lies amidst these sharply conflicting assessments." The page heading gives the date as 6 June 1977, while Chomsky's own article index gives 25 June 1977; this discrepancy is unresolved, so only the year 1977 is asserted.

The clearest statement of Chomsky's later position is in a letter he wrote on 3 February 1991 to Jamie Metzl, then a doctoral candidate in modern history at Oxford researching the Western response to the Cambodian genocide ([full correspondence, with scans](https://jamiemetzl.com/noam-chomsky-and-the-cambodian-genocide/)). Chomsky writes that "the term 'genocide' is much too vague to permit a clear answer"; that at the time of the 1977 review "the facts were extremely sparse and uncertain" and he "expressed no firm conclusion"; that "I did not deny atrocities in Cambodia. I questioned some of the claims, and I insisted on skepticism about sources"; and that "with the benefit of hindsight, I would certainly emphasize more strongly that atrocities were taking place on a massive scale", while continuing to stress the need for scepticism about sources and the double standard in Western coverage. He denies that the 1977 review caused inaction, calling that claim "absurd". These are Chomsky's statements about his own conduct. They are evidence of his position, not independent verification of it.

**The reported charge.** Bruce Sharp's long, footnoted essay "Averaging Wrong Answers: Noam Chomsky and the Cambodia Controversy" argues that the underlying theme of the 1977 review and of *After the Cataclysm* was that "Khmer Rouge Cambodia was not nearly as bad as the regime's detractors claimed", and that Chomsky's work on the subject is "marred by omissions, dubious statistics, and, in some cases, outright misrepresentations" ([essay](https://www.mekong.net/cambodia/chomsky.htm)). Sharp also objects that Chomsky and Herman praised Hildebrand and Porter's book as "a carefully documented study" while dismissing books based on refugee testimony, and reports that in one chapter 33 of 50 citations in Hildebrand and Porter derive from Khmer Rouge sources and six from the Chinese news agency Hsinhua. Sharp is careful to say that whether Chomsky "supported" the Khmer Rouge is "not as clear as either his critics or his defenders would like to pretend", and he distinguishes the charge of enthusiastic advocacy from Chomsky's disclaimers. The essay is self-published on a specialist Cambodia archive and is not peer reviewed; the site's About page returned HTTP 403, so no institutional affiliation is claimed for Sharp. Ernest Sternberg's article "The origin of globalized anti-Zionism: A conjuncture of hatreds since the Cold War", *Israel Affairs* 21(4), 2015, 585–601, describes the essay as "a detailed, reliable, and well-documented summary" and calls Sophal Ear's thesis (below) "still an excellent source" ([journal record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13537121.2014.984419?journalCode=fisa20); [institutional record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/the-origin-of-globalized-anti-zionism-a-conjuncture-of-hatreds-si/)); that is one scholar's assessment in an argument about a different subject, not verification of every claim. Sharp's case is recorded here as a reported charge.

Sophal Ear's 1995 political science honours thesis at the University of California, Berkeley, archived by the University of the West of England, places "Chomsky and Herman 1977" in a "Khmer Rouge Canon" of works that supported the Khmer Rouge explicitly or implicitly, and judges that "most of what they do is to rehash the Hildebrand and Porter line in a more palatable design" ([thesis, Part 1](https://phdn.org/archives/www.ess.uwe.ac.uk/genocide/cambear1.htm)). This is an undergraduate honours thesis, not a peer-reviewed study; it is evidence of an academic criticism, not a finding of this dossier.

Metzl's third letter of 26 February 1991 puts the charge in narrower terms. He says that although Chomsky's facts may have been right and his reservations justified, "the overall effect of the debate in which you were involved was to create a certain level of doubt that somewhat inhibited decisive action on this issue", and he cites Amnesty International's then director-general Martin Ennals on the effect of contradictory reports. Metzl also concedes that Chomsky wrote only one or two pieces on the subject. This is an interpretation by a historian of the Western response, not a demonstrated causal finding, and Chomsky disputes it in the same correspondence.

**A scholarly finding.** Ben Kiernan, the Cambodia historian whose later books include *The Pol Pot Regime*, published a 1979 article in the *Bulletin of Concerned Asian Scholars* explaining why he had "changed my mind" about Kampuchea ([full text](https://gsp.yale.edu/sites/default/files/bkbcaoct.1979vietnam_kampuchea.pdf)). He wrote that he was "late in realizing the extent of the tragedy in Kampuchea after 1975 and Pol Pot's responsibility for it", that he had been "wrong about an important aspect of Kampuchean communism: the brutal authoritarian trend within the revolutionary movement after 1973 was not simply a grassroots reaction", and that "the evidence also points clearly to a systematic use of violence against the population by that chauvinist section of the revolutionary movement that was led by Pol Pot". He added that he had been "too willing to believe that information from the most important sources about post-1975 Kampuchea, the refugees and the government radio, could be reconciled", and that concentration on "the correction of specific lies or distortions (fake atrocity photographs, fake interview, etc.)" did "not by itself establish the truth about the actual situation in Kampuchea". Sharp reports that Chomsky and Herman drew on Kiernan's earlier, more qualified work; that linkage is Sharp's account, not something verified independently here.

In a later peer-reviewed article, "Myth, nationalism and genocide", *Journal of Genocide Research* 3(2), 2001, 187–206, Kiernan concluded that "Massive Khmer Rouge brutality in the repression of Cambodian 'treason' resulted in genocide", that "Over one and a half million Cambodians perished in 'Democratic Kampuchea' (DK) under Khmer Rouge rule in 1975–1979", and that "Absolute control of the CPK was maintained by a supra-Party clique of half a dozen leaders (the 'Center,' led by 'Brother Number 1,' Pol Pot) by means of massive, rolling purges of the Party" ([full text](https://gsp.yale.edu/sites/default/files/myth_nationalism_and_genocide.pdf)). Publisher and institutional records confirm Kiernan's position and the book's details ([Yale University Press](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300144345/the-pol-pot-regime/); [Yale History department](https://history.yale.edu/people/ben-kiernan); [Macmillan Center publication list](https://macmillan.yale.edu/gsp/ben-kiernan-publications-cambodia-1978-2017)).

**What can be concluded.** The dispute is documented and can be reported. A specialist historian publicly revised the qualified interpretation that had been available in 1976 and found, on evidence he had gathered, that the Khmer Rouge regime exercised central control and committed genocide on a very large scale. Several critics, most fully Sharp, argue that Chomsky and Herman gave too much weight to doubts about atrocity reports and to sources sympathetic to the Khmer Rouge, and that they did not adequately revise their position when better evidence emerged. Chomsky's own stated position, in the 1991 letter, is that he called for caution about sources rather than denying atrocities, that he had expressed no firm conclusion in 1977, and that with hindsight he would emphasise more strongly that atrocities took place on a massive scale. The dossier does not adopt the stronger charge that Chomsky supported the Khmer Rouge or denied the genocide; that is a critic's characterisation which he rejects, and Sharp himself treats it as unsettled. Nor does it adopt Metzl's causal claim about inhibited action, which Metzl offered as an interpretation and Chomsky denied.
