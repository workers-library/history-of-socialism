## 2. Biography

Goldman's own writings supply the outline of her early life, though they are retrospective and select what serves the argument of the book they appear in. Hippolyte Havel's biographical sketch prefacing *Anarchism and Other Essays* states that she "was born of Jewish parentage on the 27th day of June, 1869, in the Russian province of Kovno" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-anarchism-and-other-essays)). Havel describes a childhood divided between Kurland, Königsberg and St Petersburg, and says that the struggle over the 1881 assassination of Alexander II drew her into revolutionary student circles. He records that she became a factory worker at seventeen and emigrated with her sister Helena, and that life in the United States quickly destroyed her expectation of a free republic: "Instead of one Tsar, she found scores of them; the Cossack was replaced by the policeman with the heavy club."

Her autobiography opens with a precise date: "It was the 15th of August 1889, the day of my arrival in New York City. I was twenty years old" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-living-my-life)). She describes the Haymarket executions of November 1887 as the event that turned her towards anarchism: after hearing Johanna Greie speak in Rochester, "A great ideal, a burning faith, a determination to dedicate myself to the memory of our martyred comrades, to make their cause my own." In New York she met Alexander Berkman on her first day, heard Johann Most speak, and began lecturing in German and Yiddish. The 1892 attempt on Henry Clay Frick's life at Homestead, carried out by Berkman, drew her into a defence of "propaganda by the deed" that she later abandoned. Havel records her trial in October 1893 and a year's imprisonment on Blackwell's Island; the 1895 European trip included midwifery training in Vienna; the first major American lecture tour followed in 1897 ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-anarchism-and-other-essays)). The Cambridge-based *American National Biography* entry by Alice Wexler gives the same outline, including the brief marriage to Jacob Kersner in Rochester and the claim that Goldman's complicity in the Frick attempt was not acknowledged until *Living My Life* appeared in 1931, but the article itself returned 403 when fetched; its text was available only in search output and is not used here as directly opened evidence.

The later chronology can be checked against her own works. She was arrested in the birth-control case in 1916 and, in an essay written for *Mother Earth* in April of that year, noted that she was "to be tried at Special Sessions April 5th" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1916/social-aspects.html)). A *Mother Earth* advertisement for June 1916 lectures described her as "Just out of the New York Workhouse where she spent 15 days for advocating BIRTH CONTROL" ([Primary Official record of the periodical, text checked in search output](https://archive.org/stream/mother-earth/Mother%20Earth%20v11n04%20%281916-06%29%20%28c2c%20Harvard%20DSR%29_djvu.txt)). In 1917 she and Berkman were arrested for opposing conscription and served two years; in her Russia book she writes that when the New York courts "upheld the fraudulent methods by which I was disfranchised and my American citizenship of thirty-two years denied me, I waived my right of appeal in order that I might return to Russia" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-my-disillusionment-in-russia)). She records the deportation precisely: "On the night of December 21, 1919, together with two hundred and forty-eight other political prisoners, I was deported from America." She left Soviet Russia in December 1921. Her autobiography was written in Saint-Tropez and dated by her "January 1931"; she says Berkman suggested the title ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-living-my-life)). She acted as English-language spokesperson for the Spanish CNT-FAI in 1936–39 and died in Toronto on 14 May 1940. That terminal date and place are given in tertiary reference works — the *Canadian Encyclopedia* and the Jewish Women's Archive entry by Candace Falk — and could not be confirmed from a primary document opened here; it is flagged in section 15.

## 3. Intellectual tradition

Goldman was an anarchist who spent her career arguing with socialists, with other anarchists and, after 1917, with Bolsheviks. She never accepted the Marxist claim that a workers' state was a route to emancipation. In 1940 she set out the terms starkly: "My answer is neither the one nor the other. I am against dictatorship and Fascism as I am opposed to parliamentary regimes and so-called political democracy" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1940/individual.htm)). This placed her outside both liberal democracy and the Leninist party model, and also outside the "rugged individualism" she distinguished from her own position, which she called "the social and economic laissez faire: the exploitation of the masses by the classes."

Her relation to feminism was equally specific. She opposed women's suffrage not because she opposed women's equality but because she thought the ballot a substitute for social change. In "The Tragedy of Woman's Emancipation" she wrote: "The right to vote, or equal civil rights, may be good demands, but true emancipation begins neither at the polls nor in courts. It begins in woman's soul" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-anarchism-and-other-essays)). She also rejected the label of a solely "free love" agitator by redefining the phrase in her own terms rather than disowning it: "Free love? As if love is anything but free!" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-anarchism-and-other-essays)).

Her disputes inside anarchism were real. Voltairine de Cleyre, an individualist anarchist and an early defender of Goldman against the authorities, declined to repeat Goldman's 1893 Union Square advice that hungry people should "take bread". De Cleyre wrote: "Miss Goldman is a communist; I am an individualist. She wishes to destroy the right of property, I wish to assert it" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/subject/anarchism/voltairine-de-cleyre/1893/in-defense-of-emma-goldman.html)). Goldman later acknowledged that de Cleyre "had often been censured by Voltairine for my 'waste' of effort to read the American intelligentzia rather than to consecrate all my efforts to the workers" ([Primary Theoretical](https://pzacad.pitzer.edu/anarchist_archives/goldman/egtribtovdc/Pages/13.html)). In Russia after 1920 she found herself criticised by anarchists for too readily trusting the Bolsheviks, and by Bolsheviks and their supporters for undermining the revolution; she records both in her prefaces.

## 4. Major contributions

Her first durable contribution was a definition of anarchism that joined individual liberty to a material critique of property. "ANARCHISM: The philosophy of a new social order based on liberty unrestricted by man-made law; the theory that all forms of government rest on violence, and are therefore wrong and harmful, as well as unnecessary" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-anarchism-and-other-essays)). She then gave it a social content: "Anarchism stands for a social order based on the free grouping of individuals for the purpose of producing real social wealth; an order that will guarantee to every human being free access to the earth and full enjoyment of the necessities of life, according to individual desires, tastes, and inclinations." Her emphasis on the individual was not a defence of isolation. In the same essay she argued that individual and social instincts were "closely related and truly harmonious, if only placed in proper environment", and that "Anarchism is the only philosophy which brings to man the consciousness of himself."

The second was her critique of marriage and the family, developed in "Marriage and Love" and "The Tragedy of Woman's Emancipation". Her claim was that marriage was a legal and economic arrangement rather than a form of love. "Marriage and love have nothing in common; they are as far apart as the poles; are, in fact, antagonistic to each other." More precisely, "Marriage is primarily an economic arrangement, an insurance pact" in which the woman pays "with her name, her privacy, her self-respect, her very life." She argued that the institution made "a parasite of woman, an absolute dependent" and that its claim to protect the child was "the sham, the hypocrisy of it!" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-anarchism-and-other-essays)).

The third was her argument for birth control as part of a broader politics of emancipation. In "The Social Aspects of Birth Control" she wrote that woman "ought to be in a position to decide how many children she should bring into the world, whether they should be brought into the world by the man she loves and because she wants the child, or should be born in hatred and loathing." She defended the right to information against the Comstock laws: "it is not the Birth Control Movement, but the law, which will have to go" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1916/social-aspects.html)). In "Marriage and Love" she framed the same demand as "fewer and better children, begotten and reared in love and through free choice."

The fourth was her critique of Bolshevik centralism after 1921. In the Afterword to *My Disillusionment in Russia* she generalised from Russia to revolution itself: "It would be an error to assume that the failure of the Revolution was due entirely to the character of the Bolsheviki. Fundamentally, it was the result of the principles and methods of Bolshevism... It is not so much the Bolsheviki who killed the Russian Revolution as the Bolshevik idea. It was Marxism, however modified; in short, fanatical governmentalism" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1920s/disillusionment/afterword.htm)).

The fifth was her use of drama and literature as political education. In *The Social Significance of the Modern Drama* she argued that "It is the dynamite which undermines superstition, shakes the social pillars, and prepares men and women for the reconstruction" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-the-social-significance-of-the-modern-drama)).

## 5. Analysis of capitalism

Goldman treated capitalism as a system of property and coercion rather than merely a market. In *Anarchism and Other Essays* she adopted Proudhon's formulation — "Property is robbery," she wrote, "but without risk and danger to the robber" — and argued that monopoly had "robbed him of his birthright, and has turned him loose a pauper and an outcast." The state existed to defend that arrangement: "there is hardly a modern thinker who does not agree that government, organized authority, or the State, is necessary only to maintain or protect property and monopoly. It has proven efficient in that function only." She also quoted Shaw's description of the state as "a huge machine for robbing and slave-driving of the poor by brute force" and added that "The State is the economic master of its servants" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-anarchism-and-other-essays)). These are her arguments and her chosen authorities, not independent findings of this dossier.

She thought wage labour damaged the person as well as the pocket. She described the worker turned "into a mere particle of a machine, with less will and decision than his master of steel and iron", robbed "not merely of the products of his labor, but of the power of free initiative, of originality." Work under capitalism was compulsion; "Anarchism aims to strip labor of its deadening, dulling aspect, of its gloom and compulsion. It aims to make work an instrument of joy, of strength, of color, of real harmony." In her 1916 birth-control essay she joined this to militarism and demography: capitalism, she wrote, had "an insatiable appetite" and needed "a large race" both for the labour market and for the trenches ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1916/social-aspects.html)).

Marriage and the family appeared in her analysis as institutions that reproduced the labour force and disciplined women. "The defenders of authority dread the advent of a free motherhood, lest it will rob them of their prey. Who would fight wars? Who would create wealth? Who would make the policeman, the jailer, if woman were to refuse the indiscriminate breeding of children?" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-anarchism-and-other-essays)). The phrase she is reported to have used about the state as "the instrument of the capitalist" could not be found verbatim in the primary texts opened here; the closest verified formulations are given above and the gap is recorded in section 15.

## 6. Analysis of socialism

Goldman wrote no detailed blueprint of a finished socialist society, and in 1940 she said explicitly what she wanted instead: "Such an ideal I see in Anarchism. To be sure, not in the popular misrepresentations of Anarchism spread by the worshippers of the State and authority. I mean the philosophy of a new social order based on the released energies of the individual and the free association of liberated individuals" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1940/individual.htm)). She defined liberty positively: "Real freedom, true liberty is positive: it is freedom to something; it is the liberty to be, to do; in short, the liberty of actual and active opportunity."

Before 1917 her target was mainly Marxist determinism and parliamentary socialism. After 1917 her target widened to the existing socialist state. In the Afterword she argued that the Russian Revolution had begun through the direct action of peasants and workers rather than through any Marxian timetable: "The Russian peasants began to expropriate the landlords and the workers took possession of the factories without taking cognizance of Marxian dicta." The Bolsheviks then "shear them gradually of their powers and finally to subordinate them to their own needs" — the Soviets, trade unions and co-operatives alike ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1920s/disillusionment/afterword.htm)).

Her preferred institutions were voluntary and industrial. "The industrial power of the masses, expressed through their libertarian associations--Anarcho-syndicalism--is alone able to organize successfully the economic life and carry on production." Co-operatives would handle distribution and exchange between town and country, and cultural bodies would keep "independent scope and freedom of expression." She was explicit that syndicalism alone was not enough: "syndicalism (or industrialism) alone is not, as its exponents claim, sufficient unto itself. It is only when the libertarian spirit permeates the economic organizations of the workers that the manifold creative energies of the people can manifest themselves." The ethical test was the identity of means and ends: "No revolution can ever succeed as a factor of liberation unless the MEANS used to further it be identical in spirit and tendency with the PURPOSES to be achieved" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1920s/disillusionment/afterword.htm)).

## 8. Recommended reading

- ***Anarchism and Other Essays* (1910)** — the clearest statement of her definition of anarchism, her critique of property and the state, and her arguments on marriage and women's emancipation.
- ***My Disillusionment in Russia* (1923) together with *My Further Disillusionment in Russia* (1924)** — the two volumes contain the eyewitness case against Bolshevik centralism, Kronstadt and the Cheka, and her replies to contemporary critics.
- ***Living My Life* (1931)** — her own account of how a Russian Jewish immigrant became "Red Emma"; indispensable but openly self-justifying and selective.
- ***The Social Significance of the Modern Drama* (1914)** — shows how she used Ibsen, Shaw and the modern stage as a vehicle for radical education rather than treating art as decoration.
- **"The Individual, Society and the State" (1940)** — her last systematic statement, written after both the Russian and Spanish experiences, of why she rejected fascism, parliamentary democracy and proletarian dictatorship alike.

## 9. Selected lectures and interviews

No sound or film recording of Goldman's own voice was located and verified during this research, so none is claimed. The University of California, Berkeley project "Light and Shadows: Emma Goldman 1910–1916" documents her lecture itinerary, topics and related archival documents, including contemporary newspaper reports ([Primary Official](https://metadata.berkeley.edu/emma/)). Among the dated entries are a lecture on "Woman Suffrage" in Chicago on 17 January 1910, a debate in San Francisco on 28 April 1910 on the resolution "that collective regulation, and not free love, is a guarantee for a healthy race", and lectures on "Marriage and Love", "The Drama" and "Francisco Ferrer and the Modern School" across several cities. The same site records that the January 1910 issue of *Mother Earth* was held up by the Postmaster after Anthony Comstock's complaint about her essay "The White Slave Traffic", and released at the end of the month.

The Emma Goldman Papers microfilm index lists numerous printed interviews and radio scripts, including a 23 September 1936 Barcelona radio address and a 30 September 1936 radio talk titled "Whom the Gods Wish to Destroy, They First Strike Mad" ([index checked in search output](https://archive.org/stream/goldmanwritings9101emma_5/goldmanwritings9101emma_5_djvu.txt)). Later audiovisual material exists but is not Goldman speaking: the Talking History archive hosts a reading from her 1910 suffrage essay and a 1991 Pacifica documentary with interviews about her; Pacifica Radio Archives holds a 1975 WBAI documentary, *Emma Goldman: 1869–1940* ([checked in search output](https://www.talkinghistory.org/arch2006july-december.html); [Pacifica record](https://pacificaradioarchives.org/recording/bc2149/)). These are noted as access leads, not as verified Goldman recordings.

## 10. Intellectual influences

The influences can be documented from her own citations rather than inferred. In chapter 1 of *Anarchism and Other Essays* she quotes or invokes Proudhon, Bakunin, Thoreau, Emerson, Shaw and Ouida, and approves Kropotkin's attack on law and punishment ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-anarchism-and-other-essays)). Havel's biographical sketch adds that she came to regard American anarchism as finding its highest expression "theoretically by Josiah Warren, Stephen Pearl Andrews, Lysander Spooner; philosophically by Emerson, Thoreau, and Walt Whitman" — a claim by Havel about her development, not a statement by Goldman herself. In her preface she named Johann Most as "the first great Anarchist speaker" she heard, and said his spoken word could never be erased from the mind. Alexander Berkman was a constant presence in her account of her own work; she says he spent eighteen months revising *Living My Life* with her and suggested its title.

In 1940 she singled out Kropotkin's *Mutual Aid* as the corrective to a one-sided Darwinian struggle for existence, writing that Kropotkin showed "that in the animal kingdom, as well as in human society, co-operation - as opposed to internecine strife and struggle - has worked for the survival and evolution of the species" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1940/individual.htm)). In the same essay she cited Nietzsche's phrase that the state is a cold monster. In "The Social Aspects of Birth Control" she took Malthus as the starting point while rejecting his premise when it became an excuse for poverty: the cause was that "the earth is monopolized in the hands of the few to the exclusion of the many" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1916/social-aspects.html)). Marx was an object of argument throughout, never a source: she called the Marxian theory "a cold, mechanistic, enslaving formula" in her Russia preface.

## 11. Influence and legacy

Her influence ran through print, lecture tours and the example of her life rather than through an organisation she built. The *International Socialist Review* concedes that "a generation after her death, she was forgotten" and that interest revived in the 1960s with the New Left and the resurgence of radical history; it records that Howard Zinn wrote a play about her, Martin Duberman a screenplay, E. L. Doctorow made her a central character in *Ragtime*, and Maureen Stapleton won an Oscar portraying her in *Reds* ([Scholarly Secondary, political journal](https://isreview.org/issues/34/emmagoldman/)). These are the critic's examples, reported as his claims, not an independent verification of each work.

In anarchism her reputation rests principally on the Russian books. The International Communist Current article, writing from a hostile left-communist position, nevertheless calls her "the Rosa Luxemburg of the anarchists" in the anarchist and anarcho-syndicalist milieu and credits her observations on the fusion of the Bolsheviks with the state apparatus as "highly pertinent" in 1922 ([Scholarly Secondary, political journal](https://en.internationalism.org/content/16588/belated-answer-revolutionary-anarchist-emma-goldman-and-russian-revolution)). The same article credits her with taking a firm internationalist stand in 1915 against Kropotkin and the "Manifesto of the Sixteen" on the First World War. Her role in the Spanish Civil War is documented in the Goldman Papers microfilm index, which lists her Barcelona radio addresses and her appointment as CNT-FAI's English-language representative; the *American National Biography* excerpt describes her as appointed CNT-FAI agent in London.

Her legacy remains contested. The *International Socialist Review* argues that later radicals selected her as "the archetypical rebel" while ignoring what it calls her elitist politics, her opposition to suffrage and her birth-control arguments. The International Communist Current argues the opposite case for her Russia analysis: that she underplayed the isolation of the Russian Revolution and the defeat of the world proletariat. Both judgements are interpretations by politically committed writers and should be reported as such.

## 12. Criticisms and controversies

**Contemporary anarchist criticism.** De Cleyre's 1893 lecture defended Goldman's right to speak while refusing her advice to "take bread": "I, as an anarchist, have no right to advise another to do anything involving a risk to himself." She identified a substantive theoretical divide: "Miss Goldman is a communist; I am an individualist" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/subject/anarchism/voltairine-de-cleyre/1893/in-defense-of-emma-goldman.html)). Goldman's own tribute records a second complaint, that she spent her effort "to read the American intelligentzia rather than to consecrate all my efforts to the workers" ([Primary Theoretical](https://pzacad.pitzer.edu/anarchist_archives/goldman/egtribtovdc/Pages/13.html)). The International Communist Current article notes that her anti-state position was not simply emotional: "Goldman's position is not exclusively emotional, as some anarchists constantly reproached her for at the time, but is based on historic experience" ([Scholarly Secondary, political journal](https://en.internationalism.org/content/16588/belated-answer-revolutionary-anarchist-emma-goldman-and-russian-revolution)). That sentence is the critic's report of contemporary anarchist objections, not a quotation from those anarchists opened here.

**The 1920s debate with the Bolsheviks.** Goldman's own account gives both sides. She records that she defended the Bolsheviks in the United States from November 1917 to February 1918, and that in Russia she met Lenin, Zinoviev, Radek and others. Of Lenin she wrote: "A shrewd Asiatic, this Lenin. He knows how to play on the weak sides of men by flattery, rewards, medals", and after he proposed that her "Russian Friends of American Liberty" scheme be placed under the Third International, she refused: "I could not consistently align myself with the Third International" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-my-disillusionment-in-russia)). She reports Lenin's assurance that "Anarchists of ideas [ideyni] are not in our prisons" and her discovery that anarchists were in fact imprisoned. Her final position was that "Kronstadt broke the last thread that held me to the Bolsheviki. The wanton slaughter they had instigated spoke more eloquently against them than aught else" ([Primary Theoretical](https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/emma-goldman-my-further-disillusionment-in-russia)). In the Afterword she generalised: the state is "institutional and static; revolution is fluent, dynamic", and "the State idea killed the Russian Revolution" ([Primary Theoretical](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/goldman/works/1920s/disillusionment/afterword.htm)). The MIA editor adds a footnote correcting her chronology of Lenin's "All power to the Soviets" slogan, evidence that her polemic was not treated as infallible even by its hosts.

**Marxist objections.** Lance Selfa's *International Socialist Review* essay is the most detailed Marxist critique opened here. It argues that she ignored "her own elitist politics", quoting her from "Minorities Versus Majorities" that "Always, at every period, the few were the banner bearers of a great idea, of liberating effort. Not so the mass, the leaden weight of which does not let it move." It accuses her of using socialist abuses "to attack socialism in general", of treating the Russian civil war and foreign intervention as an excuse rather than a context, and of calling her experience "disillusionment" when "she wasn't a supporter of socialism". It contrasts her with Victor Serge, who argued that defence of the revolution against counter-revolution was a condition for anything else, and cites Paul Avrich's judgement that "the historian can sympathize with the rebels and still concede that the Bolsheviks were justified in subduing them" ([Scholarly Secondary, political journal](https://isreview.org/issues/34/emmagoldman/)). Selfa also notes that she published denunciations of the Bolsheviks in Pulitzer's *New York World* against comrades' advice.

The International Communist Current critic accepts much of her factual account but rejects her conclusion: she "speaks little of its political isolation as due to the fact that the world proletariat had not been able to take power in other countries", her description of the revolution as possibly "doomed at its birth" shows "a fatalistic approach", and her attribution of the defeat to "the whole Socialist conception of revolution itself" goes beyond what her evidence establishes ([Scholarly Secondary, political journal](https://en.internationalism.org/content/16588/belated-answer-revolutionary-anarchist-emma-goldman-and-russian-revolution)).

**Myth and selective memory.** Selfa reports that "she never said the quote that is most attributed to her--'If I can't dance, I don't want to be part of your revolution'--although she no doubt would have agreed with the sentiment", and attributes its origin to a 1973 anti-war rally ([Scholarly Secondary](https://isreview.org/issues/34/emmagoldman/)). The phrase was not found in any primary text opened here and should not be printed as hers.

**Internal contradiction on means and ends.** In her Russia preface she insists that violence in combat is one thing and "to make a principle of terrorism, to institutionalize it" is another, while her early career included support for Berkman's attack on Frick. Her biographer Alice Wexler is reported in the *American National Biography* excerpt as saying that Goldman relinquished support for individual violent protest after Homestead but retained admiration for those willing to sacrifice their lives for an ideal. The tension between the early and later positions belongs in the profile and should not be smoothed over.

## 13. Related thinkers

**Alexander Berkman.** Lifelong companion and political collaborator, whose 1892 attack on Frick shaped her early reputation and whose suicide in 1936 preceded her Spanish work. She credited him with suggesting the title of her autobiography.

**Voltairine de Cleyre.** Individualist anarchist who defended Goldman's right to speak in 1893 while rejecting both her economics and her advice to the hungry; later criticised *Mother Earth* for addressing the intelligentsia.

**Johann Most.** Her first mentor on the American anarchist platform and the target of her break over tactics in 1891; in her preface she recalled the effect of his oratory.

**Peter Kropotkin.** A source of the mutual-aid argument and a figure she visited in Russia; she records his death and funeral in *My Further Disillusionment in Russia*. The International Communist Current critic notes that she took a different wartime position from Kropotkin's in 1915.

**Mikhail Bakunin, Pierre-Joseph Proudhon and the American individualists.** Invoked in her definition of anarchism and in her account of the intellectual sources of American anarchism.

**Vladimir Lenin, Leon Trotsky and Grigory Zinoviev.** The Bolshevik leaders she met, defended, then broke with; her descriptions of them are primary testimony and are also polemic.

**Margaret Sanger.** The birth-control organiser with whom she worked and from whom she separated over strategy. Her own view was that birth control could not be separated from the wider struggle; the JWA entry by Candace Falk makes the same point ([tertiary reference, checked in search output](https://jwa.org/womenofvalor/goldman/womens-rights/birth-control)).

**Victor Serge.** An anarchist who joined the Russian Revolution and defended it against counter-revolution; used by Selfa as the counter-example to Goldman's position.

**Rosa Luxemburg.** Not a direct correspondent in the sources opened here, but a parallel case of an internal socialist critic of Bolshevik centralism; the International Communist Current comparison of Goldman to Luxemburg is its own judgement.

## 15. Facts not verified

1. **The exact phrase "the instrument of the capitalist".** It was not found in any primary text opened here. The closest verified formulations are "government, organized authority, or the State, is necessary only to maintain or protect property and monopoly" and "The State is the economic master of its servants" (*Anarchism and Other Essays*). A libertarianism.org editor's note uses the phrase about Goldman, but that is the editor's wording, not hers. The profile must not put the phrase in quotation marks as hers.
2. **"If I can't dance, I don't want to be part of your revolution."** Not found in the primary texts opened here. Selfa reports that she never said it and attributes it to a 1973 rally. Do not attribute it to Goldman.
3. **Date of death.** 14 May 1940 in Toronto appears in tertiary reference works only (Jewish Women's Archive, Canadian Encyclopedia, Embryo Project); no primary or official record was opened that confirms it. The American National Biography entry could not be fetched directly.
4. **The title of the 1918 pro-Bolshevik pamphlet.** *My Disillusionment in Russia* quotes from "The Truth About the Bolsheviki"; the *International Socialist Review* calls it "In Defense of the Bolsheviks". No copy of the pamphlet was opened; the title is not single-valued here.
5. **The date of de Cleyre's lecture.** MIA heads the text "Delivered in New York, Dec. 16. 1894" but its own source note says Paul Avrich dates it 16 December 1893. Not resolved.
6. **Edition dates for *Living My Life*.** The Anarchist Library and Internet Archive give 1931; the MIA index gives "1931 (Volume 1), 1934 (Volume 2)". Not resolved.
7. **The preface date of *My Further Disillusionment in Russia*.** The 1924 text is dated Berlin, June 1924; the revised combined edition in the MIA/libcom text is dated August 1925. Both are reported; no single date is claimed.
8. **No page numbers** are supplied for any quotation. The opened HTML texts did not consistently preserve edition page numbers, and no scanned edition was read page by page. No claim in this dossier relies on an unverified page number.
9. **Audiovisual material.** No recording of Goldman's voice was opened or verified. The radio scripts in the microfilm index are texts, not recordings, and the audio pages found are later productions.
10. **Physical archival access.** None. The Emma Goldman Papers are held in microfilm and in multiple archives; the finding aids and Berkeley lecture database were consulted as electronic records only. No document was examined in an archive.
11. **Scholarly studies not read at source.** Alice Wexler's two-volume biography, Candace Falk's *Love, Anarchy and Emma Goldman*, Paul Avrich's work on Kronstadt, and the 2018 *Diacronie* article were encountered only through citations, excerpts or a blocked endpoint. Their positions are reported where necessary from the opened political-journal essays and are labelled as such.
