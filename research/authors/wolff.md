# Richard D. Wolff — research dossier

Status: `research-stage`. This dossier follows `research/AUTHOR-PROFILES-STANDARD.md` and `research/MASTER-STANDARD.md`. It reports what the opened sources say. It does not turn Wolff's claims into established fact.

## Biography

Richard David Wolff was born in Youngstown, Ohio, on 1 April 1942. The UMass Amherst finding aid for his papers says his parents, Max and Lieselotte Wolff, had emigrated from Germany during the Second World War and later lived near New York; his mother wrote a manuscript about her escape from Germany. Wolff took a BA in history at Harvard in 1963, an MA in economics at Stanford in 1964, an MA in economics at Yale in 1966, an MA in history at Yale in 1967, and a PhD in economics at Yale in 1969. His dissertation was titled "Economic Aspects of British Colonialism in Kenya, 1895–1930". His own website lists the Harvard, Stanford and Yale history and doctorate degrees but omits the 1966 Yale economics MA, a small discrepancy between the two records ([UMass finding aid](http://findingaids.library.umass.edu/ead/mufs210); [Wolff, About](https://www.rdwolff.com/about)).

He taught at Yale while a graduate student from 1967 to 1969, then at City College of the City University of New York from 1969 to 1973. UMass Amherst hired him in 1973 as part of a "radical package" of economists that included Samuel Bowles, Herbert Gintis, Richard Edwards and Stephen A. Resnick. He became a full professor in 1981, won a UMass Distinguished Teaching Award in 1981, and taught there until retiring in 2008, when he was named professor emeritus. He has been a visiting professor in the Graduate Program in International Affairs at The New School in New York. In 1979–80 he spent a sabbatical in Paris studying with the Marxist philosopher Louis Althusser. He was a founding member of the New Haven Green Party and ran unsuccessfully for mayor of New Haven in 1985. He lives in New York with his wife, the psychotherapist Harriet Fraad; they have two adult children ([UMass finding aid](http://findingaids.library.umass.edu/ead/mufs210); [Wolff, About](https://www.rdwolff.com/about); [Democracy at Work, The Progressive interview](https://www.democracyatwork.info/the_progressive_interview)).

In 2012 he co-founded the nonprofit Democracy at Work, which produces *Economic Update*, a bi-monthly *Global Capitalism* lecture, the *Ask Prof Wolff* series and books. Wolff says he donates his work on several books so that sales revenue goes to the organisation ([Wolff, About](https://www.rdwolff.com/about); [Democracy at Work, Understanding Marxism](https://www.democracyatwork.info/understanding_marxism)).

## Intellectual tradition

Wolff works in Marxian political economy, but his version of it differs from both Soviet-style orthodoxy and from Marxists who give the economy a determining role. His own summary says that the approach he developed with Stephen Resnick "retains and systematically elaborates the Marxist notion of class as surplus labor" while it "rejects the economic determinism typical of most schools of economics and usually associated with Marxism as well". Two ideas define it: class, defined as the production, appropriation and distribution of surplus labour, is used as an "entry point" for social analysis; and "overdetermination" is used as the logic of a non-determinist analysis ([Wolff, About](https://www.rdwolff.com/about)).

That position places him in the "Amherst School" of heterodox economics, alongside Resnick and their students. A 2021 scholarly survey describes the school as an "open-system framework" built on "overdetermination" and "a novel conception of class as a process of performing, appropriating, and distributing surplus labour". It says the group developed a "post-Althusserian critique of economic determinism" and rejected the base-and-superstructure model in favour of a "decentered totality" ([McIntyre, Burczak and Garnett, introduction to *Knowledge, Class, and Economics*](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781351798082_A31470389/preview-9781351798082_A31470389.pdf)).

Wolff also rejects the common equation of socialism with state ownership. In an interview with Young Democratic Socialists he said that the "popular" definition of socialism as government enterprise and planning "has got to be stopped", and that he did not want a debate about "more or less government" ([Democracy at Work, Young Democratic Socialists interview](https://www.democracyatwork.info/young_democratic_socialists_interview_with_professor_richard_wolff)). He does not report rejecting the label "Marxist"; his own site describes him as a Marxian economist and his books use the term ([Wolff, About](https://www.rdwolff.com/about)).

## Major contributions

The central contribution is the class analysis he built with Resnick. In a FAQ on his own site Wolff defines class through the surplus: workers produce an output larger than what they receive back for their own consumption, and exploitation exists when "the people who receive and distribute the surplus are different from those who produce it". Socialism is "the negation of exploitation" ([Wolff, FAQ: Marxism and socialism](https://www.rdwolff.com/faq_marxism_socialism)). The scholarly survey of the Amherst School says Resnick and Wolff read all three volumes of *Capital* as a theory of exploitation, distinguished the "fundamental class process" of producing surplus from the "subsumed class process" of distributing it, and treated this focus on class processes as Marxism's distinctive feature ([McIntyre, Burczak and Garnett, introduction](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781351798082_A31470389/preview-9781351798082_A31470389.pdf)).

The second contribution is the idea of the worker self-directed enterprise, usually abbreviated WSDE. Haymarket's table of contents for *Democracy at Work* lists chapters on "The Solution: Worker Self-Directed Enterprises (WSDEs)", on how they would function inside a capitalist economy, and on them as "the key to a new socialism for the 21st century" ([Haymarket, *Democracy at Work*](https://www.haymarketbooks.org/books/483-democracy-at-work)). Wolff describes a WSDE as an enterprise in which the workers who produce the output also act collectively as the board of directors, deciding what, how and where to produce and what to do with the surplus. Ownership by the workers is welcome but not the defining point; the transfer of decision-making power is ([Democracy Now! transcript, 25 March 2013](https://www.democracynow.org/2013/3/25/capitalism_in_crisis_richard_wolff_urges); [Democracy at Work, Young Democratic Socialists interview](https://www.democracyatwork.info/young_democratic_socialists_interview_with_professor_richard_wolff)).

A third contribution is pedagogical. With Resnick he wrote comparative textbooks that set neoclassical, Keynesian and Marxian economics side by side. The publisher says *Contending Economic Theories* identifies each theory's "starting point, its goals and foci, and its internal logic", then connects the differences to policy questions such as the role of government and the class structure of production ([Penguin Random House / MIT Press, *Contending Economic Theories*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/655797/contending-economic-theories-by-richard-d-wolff-and-stephen-a-resnick/)).

## Analysis of capitalism

Wolff's definition of exploitation is precise and, in his telling, independent of the labour theory of value. In his FAQ he writes that in a capitalist industrial corporation the value of the workers' output exceeds the cost of physical inputs plus wages, and that "the difference is the surplus value appropriated by the corporation's board of directors". He contrasts this with the mainstream definition, in which "there is no such thing as a surplus" ([Wolff, FAQ](https://www.rdwolff.com/faq_marxism_socialism)). In the same answer he lists slavery, feudalism and capitalism as exploitative arrangements because in each the surplus is received by people other than its producers.

He locates power in the governance of the enterprise. In a 2014 interview he described "the capitalist workplace" as "one of the most profoundly undemocratic institutions on the face of the Earth", with major shareholders choosing a board of roughly fifteen to twenty directors who decide what to produce, how, where and what to do with the profit, while the great majority of workers "must live with the results of all of the decisions that are made by a tiny minority" ([Democracy at Work, The Progressive interview](https://www.democracyatwork.info/the_progressive_interview)). On *Democracy Now!* in 2013 he made the same point with examples: a board that closes a factory in Cincinnati and moves production to Shanghai, adopts a polluting technology, or distributes large pay packages and dividends, is making decisions from which the workers are excluded ([Democracy Now! transcript](https://www.democracynow.org/2013/3/25/capitalism_in_crisis_richard_wolff_urges)).

His account of crisis combines underconsumption, debt and the long stagnation of real wages. Haymarket's description of *Capitalism Hits the Fan* says he argues that "deep economic structures—the relationship of wages to profits, of workers to boards of directors, and of debts to income—account for the crisis", and that the post-1970s halt in the rise of real wages set in motion the events that broke the world economy ([Wolff, Books](https://www.rdwolff.com/books)). In a 2013 interview he attacked austerity because it cuts government purchasing and lowers workers' purchasing power, and said the 2008 crisis showed that banks considered too big to fail had become larger rather than smaller ([Democracy Now! transcript](https://www.democracynow.org/2013/3/25/capitalism_in_crisis_richard_wolff_urges)). He also argues that criticism of capitalism was largely taboo in the United States for half a century, which he says allowed the system to indulge its "darkest anti-social tendencies" ([Wolff, FAQ](https://www.rdwolff.com/faq_marxism_socialism)). These are Wolff's interpretations; the sources opened here do not test them against economic data.

## Analysis of socialism

Wolff's socialism is defined by the organisation of production rather than by who owns it or whether markets exist. His FAQ says socialism means that "the workers whose labor generates a surplus ... are also identically the collective of persons who receive and distribute that surplus". Markets, state planning and other distribution mechanisms can coexist with socialist production because "markets have co-existed with every other kind of organization of production" ([Wolff, FAQ](https://www.rdwolff.com/faq_marxism_socialism)). In the Young Democratic Socialists interview he put it more simply: "Socialism means the democratic control of the enterprise by all of the workers in it." He illustrated this with a hypothetical 400-person factory voting against its own closure, workers rejecting a profitable but polluting technology, and workers funding childcare from the enterprise's surplus ([Democracy at Work, Young Democratic Socialists interview](https://www.democracyatwork.info/young_democratic_socialists_interview_with_professor_richard_wolff)).

He explicitly warns that this is not a complete blueprint. In the same interview he said it would be "childish and simple-minded" to claim that worker co-operatives alone would make everything else fall into line, and said he still supported collective control through government and regulation of markets. His emphasis is strategic: move the decision-making "who" and "why" from shareholders and boards to the people who work, while seeking government support for the growth of such enterprises ([Democracy at Work, Young Democratic Socialists interview](https://www.democracyatwork.info/young_democratic_socialists_interview_with_professor_richard_wolff)). He uses Mondragon in Spain's Basque Country and the Arizmendi bakeries in California as existing examples, and points to Italy's Marcora Law as a state policy that lets unemployed workers pool their benefits to start a co-operative ([Democracy Now! transcript](https://www.democracynow.org/2013/3/25/capitalism_in_crisis_richard_wolff_urges)). *Understanding Socialism*, published by Democracy at Work in November 2019, examines Russia and China and describes its aim as "a socialism built on democracy in the workplace" ([Democracy at Work, Understanding Socialism](https://www.democracyatwork.info/understanding_socialism)).

## Intellectual influences

Karl Marx is the starting point. Wolff's FAQ cites *Capital* and points readers to *Knowledge and Class* for the extended discussion, and the 2021 survey says Resnick and Wolff used a "symptomatic reading" of all three volumes of *Capital* ([Wolff, FAQ](https://www.rdwolff.com/faq_marxism_socialism); [McIntyre, Burczak and Garnett, introduction](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781351798082_A31470389/preview-9781351798082_A31470389.pdf)).

Louis Althusser is the decisive modern influence. The UMass finding aid records Wolff's 1979–80 sabbatical in Paris studying with him. A peer-reviewed tribute paper says Althusser's concept of overdetermination was "pivotal in shaping the new Marxian theory they produced", and that Resnick and Wolff were the first in North America to build a Marxian theoretical system from Althusserian concepts. The same paper records that Wolff and Resnick reached Althusser through Barry Hindess and Paul Hirst's *Pre-Capitalist Modes of Production*, and that they identified a dissenting tradition running through the Frankfurt School, Gramsci, Lukács and Mao ([Bhattacharya and Seda-Irizarry, tribute paper](https://www.jjay.cuny.edu/sites/default/files/2024-09/Ian__Stephen_Resnick_Tribute_revised_0.pdf)). The survey adds that Quine and Rorty helped them sharpen their break with established Marxist epistemology ([McIntyre, Burczak and Garnett, introduction](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781351798082_A31470389/preview-9781351798082_A31470389.pdf)).

Paul Sweezy and Harry Magdoff of the *Monthly Review* school were another influence: Resnick and Wolff dedicated their 1985 *Rethinking Marxism* volume to them, and the tribute paper says the pair were "steeped in the Marxian political economy of the Monthly Review school" before moving beyond it ([UMass books page](https://www.umass.edu/resnick-wolff/books.html); [Bhattacharya and Seda-Irizarry, tribute paper](https://www.jjay.cuny.edu/sites/default/files/2024-09/Ian__Stephen_Resnick_Tribute_revised_0.pdf)). Stephen Resnick was the closest intellectual partner until his death in 2013 ([UMass finding aid](http://findingaids.library.umass.edu/ead/mufs210)).

## Influence and legacy

Within academic Marxism, the Amherst School's class analysis has had a substantial institutional afterlife. The 2021 survey says the theory inspired more than seventy doctoral dissertations and that the journal *Rethinking Marxism*, founded in 1988, carried the approach through three decades. It documents a conference and retreat network and a body of work extending into anthropology, feminist theory, geography and postcolonial studies ([McIntyre, Burczak and Garnett, introduction](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781351798082_A31470389/preview-9781351798082_A31470389.pdf)). The UMass finding aid records Wolff as a founding member of the Association for Economic and Social Analysis and an editorial board member of the journal ([UMass finding aid](http://findingaids.library.umass.edu/ead/mufs210)).

Outside academia, Wolff's reach comes from mass media rather than journal citations. Haymarket's page for *Democracy at Work* quotes *The New York Times Magazine* calling him "probably America's most prominent Marxist economist"; the quote is a publisher's endorsement, not an independent assessment. *Economic Update* is carried on more than 120 stations, and Democracy at Work distributes books, lectures and short videos ([Haymarket, *Democracy at Work*](https://www.haymarketbooks.org/books/483-democracy-at-work); [Wolff, Economic Update](https://www.rdwolff.com/economicupdate)). His phrase "democracy at work" and the WSDE model have entered wider discussion of employee ownership and economic democracy; the Haymarket table of contents shows the model presented as a practical programme rather than only a theory ([Haymarket, *Democracy at Work*](https://www.haymarketbooks.org/books/483-democracy-at-work)). No opened source provides independent measurements of how many enterprises adopted the model because of his work, so that question remains open.

## Criticisms and controversies

The Resnick–Wolff framework has been contested inside Marxism from the start. The tribute paper by two economists states that the overdeterminist position "remains controversial; it continues to attract as much admiration as criticism", and cites written critiques by Herbert Gintis (*Review of Radical Political Economics*, 1979), Richard Peet (*Antipode*, 1992), Silverman (2013) and H. W. Park (*Rethinking Marxism*, 25(3), 2013, pp. 325–340), alongside Resnick and Wolff's replies. I opened the tribute paper, not those critique articles themselves, so I can confirm that the criticisms exist and where they were published but not reproduce their detailed arguments ([Bhattacharya and Seda-Irizarry, tribute paper](https://www.jjay.cuny.edu/sites/default/files/2024-09/Ian__Stephen_Resnick_Tribute_revised_0.pdf)). The UMass finding aid independently records Wolff's papers containing "Reply to Herb Gintis", "Reply to Richard Peet" and "On Overdetermination: A Response to John Miller" ([UMass finding aid](http://findingaids.library.umass.edu/ead/mufs210)).

A peer-reviewed critique with a recoverable abstract is Jim Glassman's "Rethinking Overdetermination, Structural Power, and Social Change: A Critique of Gibson-Graham, Resnick, and Wolff", *Antipode* 35(4), 2003, pp. 678–698, DOI 10.1046/j.1467-8330.2003.00345.x. The abstract argues that the approaches exemplified by those authors "limit the ability of studies of resistance to articulate the conditions under which political and social struggles might transcend resistance", and offers a reading of Althusser's overdetermination, traced to Lenin and Mao, that is both non-economistic and compatible with the concept of structural power. Glassman is a geographer, not an economist; the objection is to the anti-realist use of overdetermination rather than to Wolff's economics directly ([Crossref record for the *Antipode* article](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1467-8330.2003.00345.x)).

A 2021 collection of thirty essays by scholars of the Amherst School contains several chapters that interrogate the same framework from within, including Bruce Roberts on "value theory and the core of Resnick and Wolff's Marxism", Fred Moseley's "Wolff and Resnick's interpretation of Marx's theory of value and surplus-value: where's the money?", Paul Smith on "the cost of anti-essentialism", and a chapter by Wolff himself, "Marxism and postmodernism: our goal is to learn from one another". I opened the publisher's preview containing the introduction and table of contents, not the individual chapters, so only the existence and titles of these critiques are verified ([publisher preview of *Knowledge, Class, and Economics*](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781351798082_A31470389/preview-9781351798082_A31470389.pdf)).

A substantive economic objection was made by the Marxist economist Michael Roberts in a blog essay of 6 December 2019. Roberts accepts Wolff's exposition of surplus value and his warning that state ownership without workplace democracy can simply replace a capitalist board with state officials, but rejects Wolff's crisis theory as an underconsumption argument, holding that Marx rejected it and that the evidence favours a falling rate of profit. Roberts also argues that worker co-operatives without economic planning leave the wider economy under the law of value and market competition, and compares the proposal to the utopian socialists. This was published as a blog post by an economist; under the project rules it is not admissible as evidence for factual claims, and is recorded here only as a reported criticism. Wolff's own reply, quoted in a comment on that page, is that he never proposed co-operatives as the single sufficient solution but as a corrective to the old left's overemphasis on the macro level ([Michael Roberts blog](https://thenextrecession.wordpress.com/2019/12/06/understanding-socialism/)).

A separate line of criticism concerns whether Wolff's crisis explanation is compatible with Marx's value theory. Fred Moseley's chapter title, noted above, signals this question, and Glassman's objection concerns the epistemology underneath it. No opened source establishes a scholarly consensus on either point.
