## Biography

Michael Parenti was born into an Italian-American working-class family in New York City and raised in East Harlem. After high school he worked for several years, then returned to education, earning a BA from City College of New York, an MA from Brown University and a PhD in political science from Yale University (source 1). The date of birth, 30 September 1933, appears in the *New York Times* search record and in other secondary reporting; the author's own biography gives no birth date, so this profile treats it as reported by obituaries rather than by the author.

He taught at colleges and universities in the United States and abroad. According to Cook (source 8), his academic career was disrupted after a protest against the Kent State shootings in 1970: he was struck by police, left the University of Illinois, moved to the University of Vermont, and was dismissed there after a conviction connected with the protest. He then worked largely as a lecturer and writer. Cook reports that he stood for Congress in Vermont in 1974 on the Liberty Union ticket. Source 1 records fellowships from the Ford Foundation, the Social Science Research Council and others, a visiting fellowship at the Institute for Policy Studies, a 2007 Certificate of Special Congressional Recognition, and a 2014 American Book Award lifetime achievement award.

He died on 24 January 2026 in Amherst, Massachusetts, aged ninety-two (sources 8 and 9). His son Christian Parenti confirmed the death to the *New York Times*, according to the reporting quoted in source 8.

## Intellectual tradition

Parenti wrote as a Marxist political scientist and public intellectual, outside the academic posts from which he had been pushed. Source 1 makes no claim that he held a party membership; this dossier did not verify one and does not assert it. His formation belongs to the Old Left of the 1930s–1960s rather than to the student New Left, in Cook's judgment (source 8) — an interpretation, not a fact about every part of his work.

His principal disputes were with three targets: liberal accounts of US politics that separate economic power from political institutions; "left anti-communism", which he argued applied standards to communist states that it did not apply to capitalist ones; and mainstream accounts of fascism that treated it as an aberration rather than as a response to working-class organizing (sources 5, 6, 11). He shared Chomsky's interest in media and foreign policy but rejected what he called the "pure socialist" position of anarchist and libertarian critics, because it compared an ideal to existing societies under siege (sources 5, 10, 11). This is a substantive disagreement that the author collection should preserve rather than smooth over.

## Major contributions

Five arguments recur across his work and can be checked in his own texts:

1. **Class power as the hidden subject of politics.** In *Democracy for the Few* (1974) he argued that government protects large accumulations of property; the formulation quoted by Cook (source 8) is: "The close relationship between politics and economics is neither neutral nor coincidental. Large governments evolve through history in order to protect large accumulations of property and wealth." The quotation is reproduced by a secondary source; the book itself was not opened in this session.
2. **Corporate media as a business, not a public service.** Also quoted by Cook (source 8) from *Inventing Reality* (1986): "As with any business, the mass media's first obligation is to make money for their owners." The book was not opened in this session.
3. **"Rational fascism".** In the excerpt from *Blackshirts and Reds* (source 5), he argues that fascist regimes in Italy and Germany broke unions and strikes on behalf of large landowners and industrialists, and that literature on fascism neglects "the class policies of fascist Italy and Nazi Germany". He claims business funding for Mussolini's March on Rome and for the Nazi rise, with his own footnotes to Dutt, Salvemini, Neumann and Pool and Pool.
4. **Imperialism and "overexploitation".** In the excerpt from *Against Empire* (source 6), he defines imperialism as "the process whereby the dominant politico-economic interests of one nation expropriate for their own enrichment the land, labor, raw materials, and markets of another people", and argues that countries called "underdeveloped" are "overexploited". These are his definitions, not settled measurements.
5. **"Siege socialism" and the defence of existing communist states.** Sources 10 and 11 report his distinction between an ideal "pure socialism" and the actually existing "siege socialism" of states formed under military and economic attack. The phrase is his; the historical weight of the claim is disputed.

## Analysis of capitalism

In his own excerpt (source 6), capitalism is expansionist because investors must reinvest to make more money; he quotes Marx and Engels on the bourgeoisie chasing "over the whole surface of the globe". He distinguishes older empires, interested in plunder and tribute, from capitalist imperialism, which "systematically accumulates capital through the organized exploitation of labor and the penetration of overseas markets". He argues that capitalism might survive without empire but "shows no inclination to do so" — a claim about tendency rather than logical necessity, and one he states explicitly. He also describes "neoimperialism" or "neocolonialism" as rule through finance rather than formal colonial administration, with a "comprador class" that cooperates in turning its own country into a client state.

In *Blackshirts and Reds* he treats fascism as a "rational" instrument of capital when democratic rights obstruct wage cuts and subsidies to industry (source 5). The claim is explanatory and polemical; the book's factual assertions about funding and voting are footnoted in the excerpt but were not independently checked here.

## Analysis of socialism

Parenti did not publish a blueprint of a future socialist economy in the sources opened. His positive case is defensive and historical: he argues that the communist states achieved rapid industrialisation, reduced economic inequality, prioritised public services and supported national liberation, and that the "free-market" transition after 1989 had disastrous consequences (sources 7, 10, 11). He calls this "siege socialism" and argues that its centralised features cannot be judged against an ideal that never faced the same conditions. He does not deny problems: source 10 quotes him conceding internal difficulties and external military pressure. Critics (source 10) argue that he never explains the collapse of the Soviet system by its own contradictions.

## Intellectual influences

Source 5 shows Parenti citing Gaetano Salvemini, R. Palme Dutt, Franz Neumann, Daniel Guérin and James and Suzanne Pool on fascism. Source 6 quotes Marx and Engels, Frederick Engels and Frances Moore Lappé and Rachel Schurman on underdevelopment and fertility. These are documented citations in his own texts. His relation to Lenin, Mao and other revolutionary leaders is a matter of his political defence of existing socialist states (sources 10, 11), not a verified claim of personal intellectual debt in every case.

## Influence and legacy

Sources 8 and 9 describe his books as widely read on the American left and in college courses. Source 8 judges *Democracy for the Few* (1974) and *Inventing Reality* (1986) to be his most durable works and places him alongside Chomsky and Zinn in reach. Source 11 records renewed interest among younger readers after 2015. These are interpretations by the cited writers; no circulation figures were verified.

## Criticisms and controversies

- **Apologetics charge.** The *New York Times* obituary, as quoted in source 8, says he was "accused of being an apologist for brutal regimes, including in the Soviet Union and Yugoslavia". This is a reported criticism; the dossier did not open the obituary itself.
- **Method and sourcing.** Source 11, a left critique, argues that he omits the "social fascist" line of the German Communist Party before 1933, treats the revolutionary left as too weak to take power in Italy and Germany, and fails to explain the Soviet collapse by internal contradictions. Source 10 makes the same point about his "siege socialism" argument: it compares an ideal with an imperfect reality and does not analyse the system itself.
- **Selective standards.** A reader review (not used as evidence) objected that *Blackshirts and Reds* gives few citations for its factual claims. The excerpt in source 5 does carry footnotes, so the fair statement is that documentation varies across the book and was not checked here.
- **Political distance from other lefts.** Parenti's criticism of "left anti-communism" and of anarchist critics is itself contested; source 11 rejects his account of leadership and individual agency in history as inconsistent with Marxism.

## Related thinkers

- **Noam Chomsky** — shared critique of media and foreign policy; opposed on "pure socialism" and the status of existing socialist states (sources 5, 10, 11).
- **Domenico Losurdo** — similar defence of historical communist projects and critique of liberalism; both write counter-histories of the twentieth century.
- **Edward S. Herman** — overlapping critique of corporate media; Herman's propaganda model is a more formal account of the mechanism Parenti describes.
- **Michael Hudson** and **Paul Sweezy** — overlapping political economy of finance and monopoly; neither was compared directly in the sources opened here.
- **Howard Zinn** — grouped with Parenti as a popular left historian (source 8); their methods differ and this was not examined.
