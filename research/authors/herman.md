## Biography

Edward Samuel Herman was born in Philadelphia into a family of liberal Democrats. The University of Pennsylvania's official record says he studied economics at Penn, taking bachelor's and master's degrees, and then found a mentor in Robert A. Brady, a Berkeley professor who studied the economic systems of fascist societies. He completed a doctorate in economics at Berkeley in 1953, joined Penn as a lecturer in 1958, became an associate professor in 1961 and a professor in 1970, and retired as professor emeritus in 1989. The same record describes him as an economist and media analyst specialising in corporate and regulatory issues and political economy. He died on 11 November 2017, aged 92 ([Penn Almanac, 5 December 2017][s1] `[Primary Official]`). The precise birth date, 7 April 1925, appears in the author biography appended to his articles at ZNetwork and in an Open Library authority record; it was not confirmed from an institutional record opened here, and should be treated as unverified for the published profile ([ZNetwork author bio][s22] `[Primary Official]`, opened as part of the article page; Open Library was seen only as a search-result endpoint).

Herman's working life before the media books was that of a finance academic with an unusual taste for regulatory conflict. In a 2012 interview with Paul Jay he said his doctoral work at Berkeley studied the Bank of America system and the Transamerica Corporation, that he moved from Penn State to Wharton after being recruited for a study of branch banking in Pennsylvania, and that at Wharton he worked on an early Securities and Exchange Commission study of mutual funds and on a Federal Home Loan Bank Board study of savings and loan associations. The conflict-of-interest paper from the second study, he recalled, was the one the industry hated and publicly denounced. A grant from the Twentieth Century Fund let him update Berle and Means's *The Modern Corporation and Private Property*; the result was *Corporate Control, Corporate Power* (1981). He placed the start of his collaboration with Noam Chomsky in the early 1970s antiwar movement, their first joint article being on Saigon, and described the two men's entry into media analysis as a natural extension of their work on corporate power ([The Real News Network interview, 1 July 2012][s9] `[Primary Theoretical]`; [RePEc/Cambridge book record][s14] `[Primary Official]`).

Two details in the Penn obituary need care. It groups *The Washington Connection and Third World Fascism* and *After the Cataclysm* under 2015, although Chomsky and Herman's own records and later publisher biographies place the original two-volume *Political Economy of Human Rights* in 1979; the 2015 dates are best read as later editions, a point that was not independently verified. It also describes *The Myth of the Liberal Media* as being "with Robert W. McChesney", while the Peter Lang publisher page lists Herman alone as author. Neither discrepancy affects the central biography, but neither should be silently repeated ([Penn Almanac][s1]; [Peter Lang publisher page][s23] `[Primary Official]`).

The same obituary records that Herman's wife of 67 years, Mary Woody, died in 2013, that he married Christine Abbott two years later, and that a brother, Harris, survived him. These are family facts from a single institutional notice; they are not corroborated elsewhere in this dossier.

## Intellectual tradition

Herman belonged to the tradition of critical political economy rather than to a party or a school with a formal membership. His own account of the propaganda model traced it to "my economist studies of industrial structure", and in the 2012 interview he described it explicitly as "a structural analysis of the media" that asks who owns the media, where their money comes from, how they obtain information, what negative feedback they face, and what ideology they internalise ([The Real News interview][s9] `[Primary Theoretical]`). Chomsky confirmed this division of labour in 2018: "The basic framework of *MC* was Ed's", he wrote, and insisted on placing Herman's name first despite the usual alphabetic order; the opening investigation of the business structure of the major media was "mainly Ed's" ([Media Theory discussion, 2018][s10] `[Scholarly Secondary]`, reporting Chomsky's first-person account).

The intellectual lineage he acknowledged runs through the study of corporate control. Brady's work on fascist economic systems and Leo Rogin's history of economic thought formed the Berkeley environment; Berle and Means supplied the question of who controls the large corporation; and the American tradition of industrial organisation supplied the method of deriving conduct from market structure ([The Real News interview][s9]; [Penn Almanac][s1]). Later scholars place the propaganda model alongside ideology critique and the Frankfurt School's account of instrumental reason, alongside Robert K. Merton's functional analysis and Warren Breed's newsroom study, and alongside Thomas Ferguson's investment theory of party competition ([Media Theory discussion][s10] `[Scholarly Secondary]`). Tom Mills, one of the participants in that discussion, observed that *Manufacturing Consent* "wasn't that well integrated into existing work" and was "not a Marxist text in the narrower sense", though it overlapped substantially with Marxist media scholarship. That is a useful caution against labelling Herman as a Marxist theorist on the basis of his political conclusions.

He was, however, a combative participant in left politics, and his difference with Chomsky over Iran illustrates the point. In the 2012 interview Herman said Chomsky disliked "fighting on the left" and ad hominem argument, whereas Herman believed it important to criticise liberal and left organisations that he thought had followed Washington's line on Iran while ignoring the coup in Honduras. He also said he had never had a direct exchange with Chomsky on the matter ([The Real News interview][s9]). His intellectual tradition is therefore best described as structural political economy applied to media and foreign policy, combined with an activist refusal to treat the liberal left as outside the field of criticism.

## Major contributions

The propaganda model is the central contribution. In the words of the book's own chapter, the five filters are: "(1) the size, concentrated ownership, owner wealth, and profit orientation of the dominant mass-media firms; (2) advertising as the primary income source of the mass media; (3) the reliance of the media on information provided by government, business, and 'experts' funded and approved by these primary sources and agents of power; (4) 'flak' as a means of disciplining the media; and (5) 'anticommunism' as a national religion and control mechanism." The authors added that the filters "interact with and reinforce one another" and that "the raw material of news must pass through successive filters, leaving only the cleansed residue fit to print" ([Herman and Chomsky, *A Propaganda Model*, excerpted from *Manufacturing Consent*, 1988][s6] `[Primary Theoretical]`). Herman later explained that the phrase "manufacture of consent" came from Lippmann and that the model was "a decentralized and nonconspiratorial market system of control and processing", not a claim of direct orders or a single centre of command ([Herman, *The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective*][s5] `[Primary Theoretical]`). Chomsky noted that the fifth filter was generalised in a later edition; Herman's 2003 interview confirms that market ideology was added alongside anticommunism after the Soviet Union's collapse ([Media Theory discussion][s10]; [David Ross interview, 2003][s21]).

A second contribution is the paired comparison of "worthy" and "unworthy" victims. Herman wrote that he and Chomsky used the terms to describe a dichotomisation "clearly related to political and economic advantage rather than anything like actual worth", illustrated by the heavy coverage of Solidarity's repression in Poland in 1980–81 beside the near-silence on the Turkish military government's attack on unions at the same time ([Herman, *The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective*][s5]). The earliest version of this argument is in *Counter-Revolutionary Violence* (1973), which sorted killings into "benign", "constructive", "nefarious" and "mythical" bloodbaths according to the political interests served by reporting them ([Chomsky and Herman, *Counter-Revolutionary Violence*, full text][s24] `[Primary Theoretical]`).

A third contribution is the analysis of the institutions that supply "terrorism" news. In *The "Terrorism" Industry* Herman and Gerry O'Sullivan described a public and private apparatus of government agencies, think tanks, security firms and experts that manufactures a preferred view of terrorism, with the mass media acting as a "transmission belt" ([Herman, "Terrorism: the struggle against closure", 1996, pp. 77–97][s17] `[Primary Theoretical]`; see also a chapter extract reproduced with Herman's permission at powerbase.info, which was located in a search result but not opened here). Herman's own retrospective on that book says its aim was to expose the institutional roots of bias in mainstream perceptions of terrorism ([UOW PDF][s17]). In *Demonstration Elections* (1984), written with Frank Brodhead, the concept of an election staged chiefly for a domestic American audience was developed through the cases of the Dominican Republic, Vietnam and El Salvador ([East Carolina University catalogue][s15] `[Primary Official]`; [Village Voice Literary Supplement review, 1984][s16] `[Scholarly Secondary]`).

A methodological point should be stated plainly. Herman insisted that the propaganda model explains "media behavior and performance" and not media effects: "We never claimed that the propaganda model explained everything or that it shows media omnipotence and complete effectiveness in manufacturing consent." He pointed to alternative media, grassroots information sources and public scepticism as limits, and to divisions within elites as openings for debate ([Herman, *The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective*][s5]). This qualification is often absent from summaries of his work and should be carried into the profile.

## Analysis of capitalism

For Herman, the decisive feature of capitalism was not simply that owners held property but that ownership, advertising and institutional dependence produced a predictable alignment of news with elite interests. The news media are major corporations selling audiences to advertisers; the advertisers want an affluent audience and a "supporting selling environment"; the media therefore compete for patrons rather than for readers alone ([*A Propaganda Model*][s6]; [David Ross interview][s21]). In *Corporate Control, Corporate Power* the publisher's description says Herman found that managerial control had not changed the corporation's basic objectives of growth and profit, nor enhanced social responsibility, and that government regulation had "done surprisingly little to reduce the autonomy of the corporation" ([RePEc/Cambridge record][s14] `[Primary Official]`; the full book was not opened).

In the 1997 interview with David Peterson, Herman named the transnational corporation, the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank and the World Trade Organization as the institutions of global capital, and said they press for trade and investment rights over local preferences, lower welfare spending, lighter business taxes and weaker unions and environmental constraints ([ZNetwork, "The Global Media", June 1997][s7] `[Primary Theoretical]`). In a 1996 essay he used the term "closure" for the process by which dissent on terrorism is kept out of public forums, and argued that official designations of "terrorist state" followed US diplomatic interests rather than a consistent standard: Nicaragua, Iran, Cuba, North Korea and Libya were named by Reagan, while South Africa, Israel and Iraq were variously exempted as allies or "constructively engaged" (["Terrorism: the struggle against closure"][s17]).

He treated the political system as structurally similar to the media system. In 2003 he called the United States a "corporate pseudo-democracy" and a "plutocracy", and argued, following Thomas Ferguson, that when major party funders agree, the parties do not compete on the issue; the 2000 election and the exclusion of Ralph Nader from the debates served as his examples ([ZNetwork, "Media Democratization", 17 April 2003][s22] `[Primary Theoretical]`). He also drew attention to the non-reporting of worker insecurity, quoting Alan Greenspan's 1986 explanation that inflation was low because workers were "scared to death" and were unable to ask for wage increases ([David Ross interview][s21]). These are Herman's arguments and should be reported as such; this dossier does not re-verify his empirical counts or the causal weight he assigned to each filter.

## Analysis of socialism

Herman wrote no blueprint for a socialist economy and did not present the propaganda model as a theory of transition. His positive institutional preferences concern communication. In the 1997 Peterson interview he quoted the conclusion of *The Global Media*: the ultimate goal of media activists must be "the establishment of a global, nonprofit public sphere to replace, or at least complement, the global commercial media market". He defended public broadcasting where it existed, described the spread of the commercial American model as a weakening of the public sphere, and argued that the internet would not serve democratic ends if left to the market ([ZNetwork, "The Global Media"][s7] `[Primary Theoretical]`). In 2003 he pointed to South Korea's OhmyNews as a small but real precedent for independent online journalism and wrote that "we desperately need our own" media, together with strong grassroots organisations ([ZNetwork, "Media Democratization"][s22]). In the 2012 interview, asked what he would like to see, he answered: "Oh, I would like to see a good democratic socialist movement", and praised George Galloway's victory over Labour and Conservative candidates as evidence of a third force ([The Real News interview][s9]).

His treatment of actually existing socialism was sharply asymmetrical, and this needs to be reported with the same care as his critiques of the United States. In the book's own chapter, state-bureaucratic media are described as openly serving a dominant elite through monopolistic control and censorship, while private media are said to make the same function harder to see ([*A Propaganda Model*][s6]). In *Counter-Revolutionary Violence* he and Chomsky quoted a Rand analyst's favourable account of North Vietnamese agricultural cooperatives and described North Vietnam as genuinely popular, while arguing that the largest post-war bloodbaths were sponsored by the United States and its clients ([*Counter-Revolutionary Violence*][s24]). Those judgments are contested and are treated in the controversies section; they should not be presented as established history in the published profile.

## Intellectual influences

Herman's own interview testimony supplies the reliable influences. He named Robert A. Brady, author of *The Spirit and Structure of German Fascism* and *Business as a System of Power*, and Leo Rogin, a historian of economic thought, as the Berkeley figures who drew him toward radical economics; he named Berle and Means's *The Modern Corporation and Private Property* as the work his Twentieth Century Fund study updated ([The Real News interview][s9]). Inside the media argument he drew on James Curran and Jean Seaton's history of the British working-class press, Ben Bagdikian's *The Media Monopoly*, Mark Fishman's "bureaucratic affinity", Warren Breed's newsroom study and Walter Lippmann's phrase "manufacture of consent" ([*A Propaganda Model*][s6]; [*The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective*][s5]; [Media Theory discussion][s10] for the Breed and Merton connection). Chomsky's role was that of co-author and joint developer rather than a one-way influence: the 2018 discussion records Chomsky saying the framework was Herman's and that he had urged Herman's name be placed first.

Herman also cited Thomas Ferguson's work on campaign finance and Benjamin Page's survey evidence on the "elite-mass gap" as corroboration, and used Alex Carey, John Stauber and Sheldon Rampton on the public relations industry in his retrospective ([*The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective*][s5]). These are his stated sources, not an independent verification of the claims they support.

## Influence and legacy

The propaganda model became one of the most widely tested frameworks in media studies. In 2018 a group of scholars described it as "one of the most widely tested models of media performance in the social sciences", with studies in the United States, Britain, Canada, Australia, Japan, China, Germany and the Netherlands, and marked the thirtieth anniversary of *Manufacturing Consent* with an open-access collection from University of Westminster Press ([Media Theory discussion][s10] `[Scholarly Secondary]`). The same discussion records both the model's continued use and its marginalisation in mainstream media scholarship, a double judgment worth preserving. Jeffery Klaehn's earlier review articles treated the model as a serious object for sociology and political economy, while Oliver Boyd-Barrett judged the model's five filters pedagogically powerful but "somewhat derivative and truncated" beside the rest of the book and the earlier work of Herbert Schiller ([Klaehn 2002][s33] and [Klaehn 2003][s32], metadata and abstracts `[Scholarly Secondary]`; [Media Theory discussion][s10]).

Beyond academia, the paired-case method and the "worthy/unworthy victims" vocabulary shaped media criticism and activist analysis of the Kosovo and Iraq wars, and the 1997 Media Education Foundation film carried the model into teaching ([Media Education Foundation transcript][s27]). The Penn obituary quotes FAIR's Jeff Cohen calling Herman one of the leading progressive media critics and notes that Herman received primary credit for the propaganda model ([Penn Almanac][s1]). His later work with David Peterson extended the double-standard argument to the language of "genocide" and to wars and mass killings in Yugoslavia, Rwanda, Congo and Iran; that extension is the most disputed part of his legacy and is treated next.

## Criticisms and controversies

The scholarly objections are substantial and should not be reduced to a single line. Herman's own retrospective lists them: that the model is a conspiracy theory; that it derives from Chomskian linguistics rather than industrial organisation; that he failed to ask reporters for their intentions; that it ignores journalistic professionalism; that it confuses media behaviour with media effects; that it is deterministic, functionalist and leaves no room for contestation ([Herman, *The Propaganda Model: A Retrospective*][s5] `[Primary Theoretical]`). These are Herman's summaries of his critics, not the critics' texts read directly. The named critics include Robert Entman (1990), Carlin Romano (1989), Daniel Hallin (1994), Walter LaFeber (1988), Philip Schlesinger (1989), Peter Golding and Graham Murdock (1991), and John Eldridge (1993), whose full works were not opened here. Klaehn's 2002 and 2003 articles assess the model and its reception, and the 2018 discussion adds criticisms from inside the critical camp: Tom Mills found the book poorly integrated with existing communication scholarship, and Boyd-Barrett argued that the filters overstate systemic explanation at the expense of journalists' agency ([Klaehn 2002][s33]; [Klaehn 2003][s32]; [Media Theory discussion][s10] `[Scholarly Secondary]`).

For the Peterson material, the verified record has three parts. First, Peterson interviewed Herman and McChesney in 1997 on global media and democracy; that was an interview, not an adversarial debate. Second, Peterson later became Herman's co-author on *The Politics of Genocide* and numerous articles. Third, the pair replied jointly to critics. I could **not** verify a publication titled "Democracy and the Media" as a Herman–Peterson debate; the phrase appears in the record as a topic, not as a verified title of a joint work. The nearest verified items are the 1997 Z Magazine interview and the "Media Democratization" and "The Importance of a Left Media" articles Herman wrote for ZNetwork ([ZNetwork interview][s7]; [Media Democratization][s22]; [The Importance of a Left Media][s35]).

The sharpest published criticism came from within the anti-imperialist camp. Gerald Caplan's review of *The Politics of Genocide* in *Pambazuka News* (17 June 2010) argued that the book's Rwanda chapter inverted the evidence on the 1994 genocide, relied on a small circle of writers Caplan called deniers, ignored a large scholarly literature and eyewitness testimony, and did grave damage to the anti-imperialist case it sought to advance ([Caplan review][s18] `[Scholarly Secondary]`, a review essay rather than a peer-reviewed article). Herman and Peterson replied in detail, accusing Caplan of selectivity, of defending Paul Kagame's government, and of misreading the Rwanda numbers; the reply was posted under Peterson's byline but is explicitly headed "Edward S. Herman and David Peterson" ([Herman and Peterson response, 4 July 2010][s19] `[Primary Theoretical]`). Adam Jones and others entered the same exchange; those further responses were located but not opened. A related dispute over Iran in 2009 set Herman and Peterson against the Campaign for Peace and Democracy: *New Politics* summarised the campaign's objection that Herman and Peterson dismissed the Iranian election protests and withheld solidarity from movements in countries outside US control while demanding it for movements within it ([Thomas Harrison, *New Politics*, 27 August 2009][s20] `[Scholarly Secondary]`, a participant's summary of an advocacy exchange). Herman confirmed the substance in his 2012 interview, while maintaining that he would always support Iranian workers' right to fight for their rights ([The Real News interview][s9]).

These controversies are facts about the reception of Herman's work; they are not findings that settle the underlying historical disputes. The profile must distinguish, as the project standard requires, between what Herman and Peterson argued, what their critics said, and what the available evidence establishes. For the Rwanda and Cambodia claims in particular, this dossier has opened only the poles of the argument, not the body of genocide scholarship that Caplan says they omitted; no judgment on those claims is made here.

## Related thinkers

Noam Chomsky was Herman's closest collaborator on *Counter-Revolutionary Violence*, the two volumes of *The Political Economy of Human Rights* and *Manufacturing Consent*, and wrote the foreword to *The Politics of Genocide*. Robert W. McChesney co-authored *The Global Media* and edited or introduced collections of Herman's work. Frank Brodhead co-authored *Demonstration Elections* and *The Rise and Fall of the Bulgarian Connection*; Gerry O'Sullivan co-authored *The "Terrorism" Industry*. David Peterson co-authored *The Politics of Genocide* and later articles and was the interviewer in 1997. Ben Bagdikian, Herbert Schiller and James Curran supplied the political-economy of media framework Herman drew on; Daniel Hallin, Philip Schlesinger, Graham Murdock and Peter Golding were the critics he engaged; Thomas Ferguson and Benjamin Page supplied adjacent empirical work he regarded as corroborating. Jeffery Klaehn and the contributors to the 2018 *Media Theory* discussion represent the later scholarly reception. Links should be made to the profiles of Chomsky and McChesney when those exist.

## Gaps and unverified facts

The following could not be verified and must not be filled in when the profile is written.

1. **No work titled "Democracy and the Media" as a Herman–Peterson debate was found.** The verified Peterson material is the 1997 *Z Magazine* interview, their subsequent co-authorship, and their joint replies to critics. The phrase "Democracy and the Media" is a chapter heading in Chomsky's *Necessary Illusions*, which was not opened; it is not a verified Herman–Peterson title.
2. **Herman's role in *Trilateralism* was not established.** The volume was edited by Holly Sklar and published by South End Press in 1980; the available scan and OCR did not reach the contributors pages. Do not describe Herman as its author, editor or chapter author without further evidence.
3. **The exact birth date, 7 April 1925, rests on an author biography and a catalogue record**, not on an institutional record opened here. The death date, 11 November 2017, and age, 92, are confirmed by Penn's own notice.
4. **The full text of *Corporate Control, Corporate Power* was not read.** The 1981 date, publisher and publisher's description are verified through the RePEc/Cambridge record and a library catalogue; the front-matter PDF timed out.
5. **The full texts of both volumes of *The Political Economy of Human Rights* were not opened.** The 1979 date and South End Press imprint are corroborated by Chomsky's 2018 account, publisher biographies and the reprint's front matter as seen in a search result.
6. **The original publication year of "The Propaganda Model Revisited" is uncertain.** Libcom dates its posting to 2008, Klaehn cites the essay as 2000, and Monthly Review reposted it in 2018.
7. **No Empirical Dataset source was opened.** Herman's counts of media usage—for example, the figures reported by Peterson in the 2010 reply on uses of "genocide" for Rwanda and Congo—are his own compilation, not an independently verified dataset.
8. **The scholarly critiques named by Herman were not read directly.** Entman, Romano, Hallin, Schlesinger, Golding, Murdock, Eldridge and LaFeber are known here only through Herman's retrospective and later scholarship that discusses them. Attribute them accordingly.
9. **The genre of several sources is weaker than their content.** Caplan's review is a review essay in an advocacy outlet, and the *New Politics* item is a participant's summary. They document that a dispute occurred; they do not settle it.
10. **Page numbers are deliberately absent.** No quotation in this dossier carries a page number that was not present in the opened source.
