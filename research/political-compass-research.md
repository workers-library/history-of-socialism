# A comprehensive political compass: research and proposed design

## Executive summary

A political compass turns many positions into a few coordinates so a reader can see which differences are close and which are far apart. The useful question is not "how many axes?" but "which distinctions does the map preserve, and which does it erase?"

Two axes separate a market liberal from a statist communist, or a libertarian from an authoritarian. They are not enough for a history of socialism. Marxism-Leninism and social anarchism sit near each other on most two-axis charts while disagreeing about party rule and the state. Social democracy and liberalism can share civil-libertarian commitments while disagreeing about ownership. Inglehart and Welzel find two broad cross-national value dimensions; Alós-Ferrer and Granić find political landscapes that cannot be reduced to one dimension or even to two. The five-axis model below keeps the two familiar distinctions and adds three that historical material requires: community, change and scope.

The design is deliberately modest. It locates *stated political positions* on five named dimensions. It does not rank regimes, measure moral worth, or claim that any coordinate is scientifically "correct". Its output is five numbers with stated uncertainty, not a label.

## Critique: what the existing maps miss

**Conceptual losses.** A single left–right line cannot hold two facts at once: communist parties and fascist movements both subordinated individuals to collective authority, yet differed radically over property and class. Eysenck's second axis was an early answer, but he treated T partly as a personality projection, and his 1975 analysis found three second-order factors, including a separate socio-economic conservatism factor. That is an early reason to keep economy, authority and social outlook apart.

The economic/social split is itself unstable. Migration policy is at once a labour-market question, a sovereignty question and a cultural question. The Political Compass FAQ concedes that its items are propositions intended to trigger reactions and "prejudices", not policy questions, and that its social scale covers religion only indirectly. The site works as a demonstration of a model and is weak as a measurement instrument.

Cultural maps answer a different question. Inglehart and Welzel's dimensions came from factor analysis of ten indicators chosen because they appeared in every wave; the WVS page says these indicate much broader dimensions. Alemán and Woods (2016) find little evidence for consistent traditional–secular/rational or survival–self-expression orientations and find them comparable mainly among advanced post-industrial democracies. Borrow the *distinctions* cautiously; do not treat country scores as a template for individuals.

Moral Foundations Theory began with five foundations and later treated Liberty/oppression as a sixth; MFQ-2 distinguishes equality from proportionality while retaining care, loyalty, authority and purity. A framework whose authors revise its items cannot become fixed compass axes without documenting the choice. It does justify keeping authority separate from community: loyalty and authority are distinct foundations in Graham et al. (2013).

**Psychometric limits.** The Political Compass publishes no scoring formula and refuses paper-and-pencil versions. Faulborn et al. (2025) state that there is no documentation or peer-reviewed research on how its items were developed or pretested, and cite the astrology item as evidence of an English-speaking frame. Röttger et al. (2024) record 62 propositions in six blocks, a four-point scale with no neutral option, and show that answers shift with semantically equivalent prompt changes. A review of 358 ideology instruments found that validity evidence and factor-analytic reporting are often absent, and that results change with the instrument used (Azevedo et al., 2026). Publish items, weights and reliability figures; pilot the instrument; do not assume different tests measure the same thing.

**Anachronism and caricature.** O'Neill records that there is no agreed classification of socialist types and that writers disagree over whether the defining issue is political means, economic institutions or values. Gregson (2017) treats socialism as a family of often opposed variants. Franks (2013) rejects definitions of anarchism built on a universal "anti-statism", because they fuse free-market individualism with anarchist communism and syndicalism. Brown (2013) identifies six features of Communist ideology in power and notes that they changed differently across the Soviet Union, China and Yugoslavia. None of that fits a single dot.

Names are another trap. The Political Compass FAQ argues that Nazism's name does not make it socialist. Nilsson (2024) describes conceptual confusion and ideological confounds in left-wing authoritarianism scales. Any authority axis must be defined by means and institutions, not by which group is discussed. Placements must separate what a thinker wrote, what a movement claimed, what a state did and what later scholars concluded.

## Proposed design: five axes

These five axes are a design proposal for this site. The names and pole order are fixed; the definitions below are the operational content.

| Axis | Poles | Operational definition | Why the evidence supports it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Economy** | Collective/Egalitarian ↔ Private/Market | Ownership, coordination and distribution: who controls productive assets, how wealth is redistributed, and how far class hierarchy is accepted | Retained by the Political Compass, Nolan Chart and Eysenck's socio-economic factor; O'Neill and Gregson show socialists divide over ownership and coordination |
| **Authority** | Libertarian ↔ Authoritarian | Acceptance of coercion and concentrated state power: civil liberties, due process, policing, punishment and the legitimacy of dissent | Political Compass social axis; Eysenck's factors; Authority/subversion as a distinct foundation (Graham et al., 2013); Nilsson (2024) on defining authoritarianism without ideological confounds |
| **Community** | Individualist ↔ Communitarian | Autonomy versus shared obligation and belonging: family, unions, neighbourhood, nation or ethical community; duties beyond contract | Moral Foundations Care and Loyalty and the individualizing/binding distinction; Inglehart–Welzel's family and authority indicators; O'Neill on competing socialist ideas of community |
| **Change** | Traditional ↔ Progressive | Attitude to inherited norms: religion, family structure, gender roles, hierarchy, sexual conduct and the pace of institutional change | Inglehart–Welzel's Traditional/Secular-rational dimension; Gregson and O'Neill on reformist versus revolutionary socialism; kept separate so a religious communist can be plotted |
| **Scope** | Nationalist ↔ Cosmopolitan | Nation and sovereignty versus cross-border solidarity and institutions: trade, migration, international bodies, obligations to non-citizens | Inglehart–Welzel's national pride and outgroup tolerance; Brown's international-movement feature of Communist ideology; van der Linden & Dufresne (2017) on measuring a topic before adding it |

Economy and Authority come from the two-axis tradition. Community, Change and Scope are added because a map that omits them cannot distinguish social anarchists from Bolsheviks, or social democrats from nationalists.

### Example placements

Each entry is a hypothesis about a *doctrine or documented current at a stated period*, not a fact about every person who used the label. Where a tradition contains opposing wings, both are listed.

| Tradition | Economy | Authority | Community | Change | Scope | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marxism-Leninism (party-state model) | Collective/Egalitarian | Authoritarian | Communitarian | Progressive in doctrine | Cosmopolitan in doctrine, often national in practice | Brown's six features describe parties in power; the theoretical end point is a stateless, classless society, which is not the same as the institutional record |
| Social democracy | Moderate Collective | Libertarian, constitutional | Moderate Communitarian | Progressive | Moderate Cosmopolitan | Varies by party and country; historically often tied to national labour movements |
| Social anarchism / anarcho-syndicalism | Collective/Egalitarian | Libertarian | Communitarian | Progressive | Cosmopolitan or internationalist in many currents | Franks (2013) warns against merging social and individualist anarchism; individualist anarchism is Libertarian on Economy and Individualist on Community |
| Right-libertarianism | Private/Market | Libertarian | Individualist | Variable | Variable | "Libertarian" is used differently in the United States and Europe; the Political Compass FAQ addresses this directly |
| Liberalism (social liberal) | Private/Market to moderate | Libertarian | Individualist | Progressive | Cosmopolitan | Nineteenth-century free-trade liberalism and twentieth-century social liberalism are not the same position; the site's own FAQ distinguishes them |
| Conservatism | Private/Market | More Authoritarian | Communitarian | Traditional | Nationalist | National cases diverge; do not treat one country's conservatism as the type |
| Fascism and Nazism | Mixed; private property retained under strong state direction | Authoritarian | Communitarian with exclusion | Traditional or reactionary | Nationalist | The Political Compass FAQ disputes name-based inferences from "national socialist"; placement here is provisional and should not be read as a claim about economic doctrine alone |

**Limitations.** Five coordinates still compress a great deal. Correlation between axes must be reported rather than assumed away; high correlation may mean an axis is redundant in a given sample. Ratings of historical figures are second-order interpretations and should carry a date and a source. No score should be presented without a band of uncertainty, and no tradition should be reduced to its most extreme instance. These rules follow the site's editorial standard on separating text, intention, implementation and outcome.

## Question-item design

The companion interactive compass ([source](../book-src/political-compass.md), published at `/political-compass/`) uses **five-point Likert items**, **six items per axis**, **balanced positive and negative polarity**, with scores **normalised to −100..+100**. Its item list and scoring code are published in the source file. Implementation choices:

1. Write six items per axis, three keyed toward the first pole and three toward the second, so that agreement bias cannot move a whole axis. Keying direction must be stored with each item and published.
2. Use a five-point response scale: strongly disagree, disagree, neither agree nor disagree, agree, strongly agree. Document how the midpoint is scored; do not silently recode it as a lean.
3. Keep items single-barrelled. "The state should confiscate large estates" and "The state should set food prices" are two claims, not one. Migration and taxation items should name the domain being judged.
4. Prefer concrete institutional choices to abstract slogans. Avoid deliberate vagueness, "extreme" propositions and forced choice, all of which the Political Compass FAQ defends but which weaken interpretability.
5. Keep the actor and time frame constant in paired items. "Government" should not mean a local council in one item and a secret police in another.
6. Publish the item list, scoring code, axis correlations, reliability estimates and pilot description. Use pilot data and factor analysis to confirm item loadings, following van der Linden and Dufresne (2017), rather than assigning items by assertion.
7. Test whether items work similarly across languages and political cultures before comparing groups. Alemán and Woods (2016) show why measurement equivalence cannot be assumed.
8. Report five separate scores and their uncertainty bands. Do not generate a single "type" name from a five-dimensional point without stating the rule that produced it.

Scoring is a design decision, not a finding from the literature: reverse-score the negatively keyed items, average each axis on a −2..+2 scale, multiply by 50, and retain unrounded values internally. Any change to wording or keying requires a new pilot. The [validation notes](political-compass-validation-notes.md) record the follow-up research: item wording was revised to remove vague and double-barrelled statements, the similarity figure was replaced by a ranked L1 gap because a rescaled Euclidean "percentage similar" has no defensible interpretation, and the five axes remain a hypothesis rather than an empirical result.
