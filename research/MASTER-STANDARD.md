# Editorial standard

This is the single editorial standard for *History of Socialism*. It governs the manuscript and the published site.

The book is a narrative history built from primary documents, contemporary reporting and historical scholarship. It does not offer a verdict on socialism or capitalism. Where evidence establishes responsibility it says so; where evidence is thin it says that too. Objectivity here means accurate evidence, consistent standards, transparent reasoning and conclusions proportionate to what the sources support.

Two decisions of 22 November 2026 are incorporated here. Primary sources remain central, but the book is not restricted to first-party material: historical newspapers, reporting, government and police records, and intelligence material may be used where relevant and their limits stated. Academic historiography must be engaged, and credible evidence must not be suppressed because it complicates a political interpretation. The earlier teaching-template style guide and the earlier first-party-only rule are retired.

## Purpose and identity

The book is a serious, historically rigorous educational work of narrative nonfiction about socialism, communism, Marxism, revolutionary movements, political economy, and labour history. It must be written to the standard of an accomplished historical nonfiction author. Historical accuracy takes precedence over narrative convenience, political allegiance, dramatic effect, or stylistic elegance.

## Historical research and evidence

1. Primary sources must be central. Prioritize contemporary documents from witnesses, participants, administrators, opponents, and people affected by events.
2. Treat every primary source as evidence requiring interpretation. An official record may conceal failure. A newspaper may reflect editorial interest. A revolutionary memoir may exaggerate. An eyewitness may misremember.
3. Verify authenticity. Never invent quotations, testimony, letters, diaries, speeches, statistics, conversations, headlines, citations, bibliographic details, page numbers, or archive identifiers.
4. Distinguish clearly between: a document directly examined; a reliable transcription; a primary source quoted by a secondary author; a retrospective account; a disputed or insufficiently corroborated account. Never claim direct examination that did not occur.
5. For significant claims, ask: who created the source, when, where, for whom, for what purpose, was the author a witness, was it contemporary or retrospective, was it translated or edited, could motive affect it, is it corroborated, has it been disputed?
6. Engage historical scholarship. Distinguish broad scholarly agreement, reasonable interpretive disagreement, uncertain evidence, politically motivated claims, and demonstrably false assertions. Do not treat all interpretations as equally supported. For disputed figures, explain method, assumptions, uncertainty, and credible range. Do not manufacture numerical precision.

## Firsthand experience

7. The experiences of ordinary people must appear where evidence permits: workers, peasants, soldiers, students, women organizers, prisoners, migrants, families, supporters, and critics.
8. When introducing testimony: identify the witness, their relationship to the event, when the account was recorded, the historical context, their actual words where quotation is appropriate, what the testimony establishes, and its limitations or contradictions.
9. Do not reconstruct private thoughts, exact dialogue, unrecorded conversations, sensory details, or historical scenes. Never present speculation as witnessed fact.

## Concepts and interpretation

10. Distinguish socialism, communism, Marxism, Leninism, Marxism-Leninism, social democracy, democratic socialism, anarchism, syndicalism, Trotskyism, Maoism, and other traditions. Do not treat them as interchangeable.
11. Keep separate: what thinkers wrote; how contemporaries interpreted it; how later movements adapted it; what institutions implemented; how governments justified it; how historians evaluated it.
12. Distinguish theory, intention, implementation, experience, outcome, and interpretation. Stated ambition is not evidence of success. One failed implementation does not establish what every thinker advocated.
13. Explain conditions that produced movements: industrialisation, class relations, land, war, crisis, repression, literacy, unions, international currents. Use evidence rather than assuming all factors applied.
14. Avoid anachronism. Interpret decisions within the knowledge available at the time. Distinguish participants' terminology from later historians' classifications.

## Prose standard

15. Write narrative historical nonfiction for an educated general reader. Use substantial, connected paragraphs that develop an argument or account. Short paragraphs are acceptable for emphasis but must not dominate.
16. Do not structure ordinary chapters as long sequences of bullet points. Reserve lists, tables, and boxes for material that genuinely benefits from them.
17. Avoid generic AI writing: repetitive introductions and conclusions, excessive bullets, repeated explanations, artificially dramatic language, empty rhetoric, generic transitions, unsupported declarations of importance, moralising in place of analysis, and artificially ending sections with dramatic questions.
18. Avoid the phrases: "Throughout the annals of history", "This was a pivotal moment", "The tapestry of history", "It is important to note", "This serves as a testament to", "The stage was set", "History would never be the same".
19. Avoid overuse of "complex", "nuanced", "pivotal", "multifaceted", and excessive em dashes. Vary sentence structure. Write with measured authority: confidence where evidence is strong, uncertainty where it is not.
20. Connect abstract political developments to identifiable people and documented experiences. Establish significance through evidence and consequences, not rhetoric.
21. Use simple, clear English throughout. Prefer short, common words over long or abstract ones. Keep sentences direct. Define every technical term in plain language when it first appears. A reader without specialist training must be able to follow every argument. Never substitute complexity for depth.

## Citations and documentation

21. Every significant historical claim must be traceable to a real source that supports it. Use a consistent documentary citation standard with author or issuing body, title, date, publication information, and stable URL where available. Never invent page numbers or archival references.
22. Maintain a master bibliography in which every source is tagged as an empirical dataset, a primary official record, a primary theoretical text, or a scholarly secondary work. Tertiary catalog entries, encyclopedias and opinion pieces do not qualify as evidence.
23. Do not claim an editorial or fact-checking process occurred unless it actually did. Distinguish URL and quotation checks from physical archival access.

## Ethical standard

24. Do not reduce populations to political stereotypes. Distinguish leaders, institutions, members, supporters, opponents, and civilians. Do not use selective evidence to conceal documented suffering, repression, or failure, nor to erase documented improvements in living conditions, literacy, participation, or labour rights. Evaluate claims with credible evidence and defined measures. Historical integrity outweighs a simple political narrative.

## Governing status and remaining work

This document is the sole governing editorial standard. No other internal brief, prompt or style guide has authority.

The book remains a research-stage manuscript. The main outstanding work is: adjudicating findings still present in the text, obtaining genuine independent re-verification of corrections, reconciling chapter citations against the bibliography, completing claim registers for the newer chapters, and obtaining external specialist review of representative chapters. Expansion of the chapter count should wait until that work is done. The objective is a comprehensible history that explains socialism: why its ideas emerged, how its movements gained power, how their institutions functioned, what they achieved, where they failed, and what the evidence can support. No claim of academic authority should be made before external review exists.
